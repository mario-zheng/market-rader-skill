# **SCOPE NOTE: WHY THIS REPORT FOCUSES ON QUALITY?**

**This report is a deliverable under the Viet Nam’s Start Strong ASA.** It is intended primarily for the


Government of Viet Nam (GoV), particularly the Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) and other


central agencies engaged in the financing, planning, regulation, and delivery of early childhood


education and care (ECEC) services, as well as provincial and commune authorities, and teacher training


institutions. Co-funded by the World Bank, including the Early Learning Partnership Trust Fund, and the


International Finance Corporation (IFC), the ASA was organized around five policy pillars, providing


targeted analytical inputs, technical solutions, and capacity building in each area. Crucially, the ASA was


embedded in the reform process with World Bank experts having worked hand-in-hand with the task


force led by Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) and other stakeholders from inception through


policy formulation.


**The Policy note ‘** _**Start Strong: Viet Nam Early Childhood Education and Care Reforms’**_ documents the


technical assistance activities and how they contributed to policy reforms, which together benefit


approximately 5–6 million preschool-age children each year through 2030, as well as tens of thousands


of teachers and service providers. Results of policy advisory and technical assistance for five national


reforms supported by the Start Strong ASA are covered in that policy note:


  - Equitable access to ECEC for disadvantaged and marginalized children;


  - Preschool universalization for children ages 3—5;


  - ECEC curriculum and pedagogy reforms: a new child-centered, play-based curriculum;


  - Preparing and empowering the ECEC workforce through professional teacher standards, a


national continuing professional development framework, and five priority teacher-training


modules;


  - Diversification of quality service provision through private sector solutions.


The policy note also summarizes detailed recommendations on costing, financing and mechanisms for


two access- and equity-focused reforms.


Building on this reform agenda, this report examines how quality is defined, supported, and enacted in


Viet Nam’s early childhood education and care (ECEC) system, with a forward-looking focus on the policy


and system shifts needed to strengthen classroom practice, professional support, and quality assurance


as access continues to expand. It is intended as a strategic diagnostic to inform the next phase of ECEC


quality reform, rather than a retrospective assessment of past programs or investments.


**Data coverage and scope note.** While the report considers quality across the full ECEC age range (3


months to 5 years old), the availability and depth of evidence vary by age group and modality. Most

access goals in sight, the critical question now is how to ensure that ECEC actually delivers the intended

attention to quality can lead to disappointing outcomes. Children who sit in poorly resourced, rote

learning preschools may gain little more than those who do not attend at all.


**Expanding access must not come at the expense of quality** is the central principle adopted in Viet Nam’s


new ECEC agenda. This report aligns with that principle, examining how Viet Nam can safeguard and


raise quality while broadening access.

# **TERMINOLOGY NOTE**

Viet Nam’s ECEC system serves children from 3 months to 6 years old, divided into two sub-levels


including both nursery for ages 3–36 months and kindergarten for ages 3–5 years. The system operates


through two main types of institutions. The first is preschools–legally recognized entities within the


national education system, with their own management boards and budgets. The second consists of


independent ECEC centers–including home-based groups, satellite classes, and small community-based


providers—that are allowed to serve fewer than 70 children and are funded by private investors,


enterprises, mass organizations, or local communities.


In terms of ownership, preschools may be public (state-owned and publicly financed), people-founded


(established through community contributions), or private (owned and operated by individuals or


organizations). Independent ECEC centers are overwhelmingly non-public: 99.8 percent are privately


owned and operated. By law, all ECEC institutions must register, meet national regulatory standards, and


obtain an operating license from district- or commune-level authorities before enrolling children.


In the 2023-2024 school year, Vietnam has 15,202 preschools—including 12,062 public and 3,140 non

public—alongside 12,582 non-public independent ECEC centers. These institutions collectively educate


and care for over 5 million children. Public preschools continue to enroll the majority of children aged


3–5, while non-public centers account for most growth in services for infants and toddlers and for


children in densely populated industrial zones.


In this report, early childhood education and care (ECEC) refers broadly to all organized services for


children from birth to primary school entry. Early childhood education (ECE) is used in contexts where


the education aspect is more focused on. ECEC institutions are used as an umbrella term to include both


preschools and independent ECEC centers. Non-public ECEC institutions encompass both people

founded and private providers unless otherwise specified.

# **OVERVIEW**

### **Viet Nam’s ECEC progress in access, equity, and quality foundations**

**Viet Nam’s investment in its youngest generation is fundamentally an investment in the country’s**


**future human capital.** Today, over 10 million Vietnamese children under age 6 (with another one


million expected in the next five years) will reach working age by 2040. This cohort is projected to


comprise about one-fifth of the national labor force by that time, forming a vital base of tomorrow’s


workforce. Their education and early development–from preschool participation to foundational skill


acquisition–will significantly shape Viet Nam’s productivity, equity, and long-term growth. Recognizing


this, the nation’s leadership has increasingly prioritized early childhood education and care (ECEC)


within its human capital development agenda. Viet Nam has already made notable gains in education


and health outcomes in recent decades, as reflected in rising Human Capital Index rankings and learning


assessments (with secondary-level math scores now approaching averages in the Organisation for


Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Building on this progress, attention is turning to the


early years as the next critical frontier for sustaining human capital improvements.

**The ECEC sector in Viet Nam has expanded rapidly both in size and reach, meeting the needs of**


**millions of families across urban and rural areas.** As of the 2023–2024 school year, more than 5.3


million young children (from 3 months up to 6 years) are enrolled in some form of early childhood


program. This network encompasses approximately 15,000 preschools and over 12,000 smaller-scale


ECEC providers. In this report, public provision refers to state-run preschools, while non-public


provision refers to privately managed providers operating under national ECEC regulations. Non-public


**|** 1


provision spans both formal private preschools and smaller-scale providers (including homebased and


community-run facilities), which differ in cost structures and in the type and intensity of oversight


required. Given the size and diversity of the provider base, the report distinguishes between the


objectives of expanding service capacity and the requirements for ensuring consistent minimum quality


across provider types. A major milestone was achieved in 2017, when all 63 provinces reported the


universalization of ECE for 5-year-old children, ensuring that nearly every child has access to at least


one year of school –readiness program before entering Grade 1. Public awareness of the importance


of early childhood development has grown in tandem with service expansion. These efforts have driven


high demand for ECEC even among disadvantaged and remote communities. In short, Viet Nam has not


only expanded access to early education, but also cultivated expectations among families that all young


children should have the opportunity to attend a quality ECEC program.


**Viet Nam has also made initial strides in improving the foundational quality of ECEC services, even**


**as access expanded.** Nearly all enrolled children now attend full-day programs, providing a more stable


routine and richer learning environment than shorter half-day sessions. The ECEC teaching workforce


has grown and become more professionalized over time. In 2020, Viet Nam raised the minimum


credential for ECEC teachers from an intermediate teaching certificate to a college diploma


(International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) Level 5). Accelerated in-service training and


hiring of graduates have steadily increased the share of ECEC teachers meeting the new qualification


standard – reaching about 88.7 percent by the 2023–2024 school year. Teacher-to-class ratios have


gradually improved as well, moving closer to the national norm of two teachers per class. These gains,


alongside efforts to modernize teacher training and oversight, signal incremental improvements in


classroom conditions. They form an emerging foundation of quality upon which Viet Nam can build as


it shifts focus from simply expanding access to ensuring that all children receive a meaningful,


developmentally rich early education experience.


**Despite impressive gains in coverage and quality, access for younger children remains uneven–**


**especially in disadvantaged communities where demand continues to outpace available supply.**


While virtually all five-year-olds now attend early childhood education, about one-sixth of children


ages 3–5 are still not enrolled in any program. This access gap is most acute in poorer and hard-to

reach areas, including certain mountainous districts, remote ethnic minority communities, and densely


populated low-income urban neighborhoods. Moreover, quality service provision for children under


age 3 (nursery and childcare services) in IZs and rapidly urbanizing districts far outstripped the


expansion of ECEC services. Indeed, about 71 percent of ECEC facilities in IZs are small home-based


classes operating with limited oversight; non-public providers account for only 16 percent of centers


in these high-demand areas, raising quality concerns.

**Emerging quality shortfalls remain and could undermine**


**the benefits of past access gains if left unaddressed.** In


many localities, classrooms are overcrowded–often with


30 or more children per room–and basic infrastructure is


stretched. The average staffing level of about 1.8


teachers per class, though improving, still falls below the


official standard of 2.2, meaning many classes lack a


second teacher or assistant at all times. Facilities in


poorer districts sometimes lack essentials like clean


water, child-sized toilets, or safe outdoor play space,


hampering the delivery of a healthy, stimulating learning


environment. These constraints are often most acute


especially in those areas now attempting to catch up on

### **National ECEC reform priorities and objectives through 2030**


**A confluence of factors makes the present period (2025–2030) critical for ECEC reform in Viet Nam.**


Demographic and social changes are intensifying demand for services, as industrial-zone employment


and urban migration create urgent needs for affordable childcare (with over one million young children


in industrial areas expected to require care by 2025). At the same time, high-level political commitment


has crystallized early childhood education as a national priority. In June 2025, the National Assembly


approved a Resolution on Universalization of ECEC for children aged 3–5 years. A complementary


National Assembly resolution to renovate the national Early Childhood Education (ECE) Program is under


development. The country’s commitment to modernize education in general–and ECEC in particular–is


further reinforced by Resolution 71/NQ-TW of the Politburo (August 2025) on breakthroughs in


education. Together, these policy moves aim to close remaining gaps in access and elevate the quality


of early education, ensuring that the benefits reach all children, especially those in disadvantaged


groups.


**|** 4


**Guided by these strategic directives, Viet Nam’s ECEC reform agenda for 2022–2030 is structured**


**around four interrelated priorities, each with a strong focus on quality enhancement** .


_Figure 3: Key reforms on access, equity and quality_


**First, renovating the ECE Program and pedagogy** **is identified as a cornerstone of quality improvement** .


MOET has launched a comprehensive overhaul of the ECE Program–the national curriculum framework


issued by MOET–to modernize both _what_ children learn and _how_ they learn. The new framework is


designed to be competency-based, child-centered, and play-oriented. It defines six key domains of child


development–physical well-being, social-emotional development, language and communication,


cognitive skills and early numeracy, creativity and aesthetics, and curiosity and scientific inquiry–


emphasizing interactive, play-based learning over rote instruction.


_Figure 4: Key features of the new ECE Program_


**The ECE Program reform is being rolled out in phases to ensure careful implementation and**


**refinement.** A draft new program was pilot-tested in six provinces during 2023–2024, allowing educators


**|** 5


to provide feedback and demonstrate new teaching approaches in local classrooms. Pending National


Assembly approval, the first phase of the new program will expand to 40 pilot districts across 20


provinces between 2026 and 2029. This phased approach will enable policymakers to evaluate results


and make adjustments before a national rollout by 2030. Alongside the program content itself, MOET is


introducing complementary measures– updating teacher guides and lesson plans, revising early learning


standards, improving classroom environments, and strengthening monitoring–to align the entire


pedagogical system with the new child-centered approach. The overarching goal is to enrich early


learning experiences and boost children’s school readiness and later outcomes through a modernized,


high-quality program.


**Second, developing and professionalizing the ECEC workforce is a paramount reform priority,**


**recognizing that a qualified and motivated teaching force is the backbone of ECEC quality.** Achieving


universal ECEC for kindergarten children by 2030 will require recruiting thousands of additional teachers


while also upgrading the skills of the existing workforce. Accordingly, the reform program places heavy


emphasis on teacher development–both expanding the pipeline of new teachers and investing in the


capacity and morale of those already in service. Key measures under discussion include revising hiring


norms and budget allocations to ensure each class can have two qualified teachers (including classes in


remote and sparsely populated areas).


**In parallel, MOET, with development partners, has revised Early Childhood Teacher Professional**


**Standards and developed a new continuous professional development (CPD) framework.** National


training modules on priority topics–such as the new ECE program, child-centered teaching strategies,


formative assessment techniques, and so on–are being prepared to upskill the existing teacher


workforce at scale. Collectively, these efforts aim to build a larger, more competent, and better

supported cadre of ECEC teachers, sufficient in number and equipped with modern pedagogy, to deliver


quality early childhood education across the country. The teacher workforce reforms are thus central to


Viet Nam’s strategy for raising ECEC quality.


**Resource deployment is being adjusted to address shortages, with priority on underserved areas and**


**incentives are also being strengthened to attract and retain educators in challenging posts.** For


example, hardship allowances and housing support for teachers in mountainous or isolated areas are


being enhanced. The Resolution on Universalization of ECEC for children ages 3-5, noted above, will


introduce a monthly wage subsidy for all ECEC teachers (public and non-public alike). The reform agenda


also reinforces the higher qualification requirements: since 2020 all new ECEC teachers must hold a


college degree, elevating the professional standard of the field.


**Third, achieving universal ECEC for children ages 3–5** **is the headline goal driving much of the access**


**and equity reform agenda** . Building on the success of the universalization of ECEC for 5-year-olds, Viet


**|** 6


Nam is now extending the commitment of guaranteed access down to ages 3 and 4. The target–set by


National Assembly Resolution– is that by 2030 every child will have the opportunity to attend at least


two years of ECEC (at ages 3 and 4) plus one year of kindergarten (age 5) before entering primary school.


Realizing this ambition requires not just expanding enrollment but also ensuring equity and quality in


the expansion process.


**Linked with the quality reforms, the universal ECEC for kindergarten children** **program also includes**


**provisions to train and recruit qualified teachers, to strengthen quality assurance (QA) systems, and**


**to encourage non-state providers** . In essence, the program is designed not only to increase enrollment


but also to ensure that newly enrolled children benefit from quality learning conditions and support. By


making quality and equity explicit objectives of universalization, Viet Nam’s policy framework seeks to


avoid the pitfalls of access expansion at the expense of program effectiveness. If implemented as


intended, the universal ECEC for kindergarten children program will give every 3- to 5-year-old child a


fair start before primary education.


**Fourth, diversifying ECEC service provision and engaging the private sector** **is a key pillar of the reform**


**vision to ensure adequate capacity and innovation** . Viet Nam’s ECEC system has traditionally been


dominated by public preschools, which still account for the majority of enrollment. However, the


government recognizes that relying solely on public provision will not be sufficient–or necessarily


efficient–to meet growing demand, especially in fast-urbanizing areas. The reforms therefore embrace


a _mixed delivery system_, leveraging a range of providers (public, private, home-based, faith-based, and


community-run) under improved quality standards and regulatory oversight. The reform agenda also


anticipates a broader set of delivery mechanisms to mobilize non-state capacity while retaining public


stewardship. These include contracting arrangements and partnerships in which public financing is


linked to defined quality expectations (for example, performance-based contracts), as well as


approaches that allow the state to purchase places or subsidize fees for eligible children in licensed non

public settings. In industrial zones, the mix may also include employer-supported childcare models,


complemented by measures that help smaller home-based child groups formalize and meet baseline


safety and quality requirements. Chapter 5 examines these mechanisms, including how payment flows,


accountability, and monitoring can be designed to support expansion without weakening quality.


**In summary, Viet Nam’s ECEC reform agenda through 2030 is comprehensive in scope–tackling ECE**


**program, teachers, access, and system structure–with quality enhancement for all as a unifying theme** .


These reforms enjoy strong political backing and are unfolding in a window of opportunity when policy


momentum, legislative action, and urgent needs coincide. The successful implementation of these


priorities is expected to yield significant benefits across sectors. By 2030, an estimated 5–6 million


children each year will be enrolled in better-resourced ECEC programs, guided by a revised ECE program


**|** 7


and taught by well-prepared teachers. Over time, children who attend high-quality preschools are


expected to show improved school readiness, better academic performance in later grades, and


promise a more skilled and productive future labor force.


**Achieving these outcomes depends critically on quality** –the extent to which new policies are


effectively implemented and actually improve the day-to-day experiences of children and teachers in


ECEC settings. The following sections turn to the core quality challenges that Viet Nam must address to


realize this vision.

### **Key quality challenges for Viet Nam’s ECEC system**


**Despite the promise of recent policy reforms, multiple challenges continue to impede ECEC quality**


**and equity on the ground.** Although Viet Nam has established reasonable standards for ECEC quality


(teacher qualifications, class size norms, infrastructure benchmarks, and so on), in practice many


preschools have struggled to meet these standards consistently. The unevenness in inputs and


environment means the quality of a child’s early education still heavily depends on location and local


wealth, with implications for the consistency of learning outcomes. In particular, the following five


interrelated issues, corresponding to four key quality drivers, stand out:


i. Increasing shortages and uneven distribution of fully qualified ECEC teachers, coupled with


constrained teacher pipeline;


ii. In-service training and professional support are weakly aligned with teacher needs;


iii. An ECE Program updated in 2021 in which, although child-centered in principle, uneven


integration of play-based, emotionally supportive, and inclusive pedagogy is seen among


schools and classrooms;


iv. A QA system that is leaning more to input-based and compliant than developmental;


v. Limited access to affordable, quality ECEC services for the youngest children (ages 6–36


months) and for 3-year-olds.


_Driver A: Producing more high-quality ECEC teachers_


**Teacher shortages and uneven distribution threaten ECEC quality.** Viet Nam faces a shortfall of more


than 54,000 ECEC teachers—about 13 percent of the approved quota—with acute gaps in rural and


disadvantaged regions. Staffing norms remain rigid, limiting timely recruitment and forcing heavy


reliance on short-term contract teachers, which undermines workforce stability. Fragmented


governance and quota-based planning prevent provinces from responding to local needs, while the


absence of integrated data systems compounds misalignment between teacher supply and demand.


**A weak training pipeline and uneven pre-service quality constrain progress.** Intake at teacher training


institutions (TTIs) has declined by up to 25 percent since 2020, despite financial incentives. Initial


**|** 8


Teacher Education programs (ITE) remain theory-heavy, offering limited practicum exposure and weak


alignment with professional standards. Lecturer qualifications have improved but remain uneven,


particularly in smaller colleges, restricting mentoring quality. Ethnic minority and male teachers remain


underrepresented in remote areas, perpetuating inequities. Without systemic reforms to align


preparation, deployment, and professional growth, Viet Nam risks falling short of its goals of universal


ECEC for kindergarten children.


_Driver B: Strengthening working conditions to motivate and retain high-quality teachers_


**Heavy workloads and limited professional time undermine their teaching and care quality.** ECEC


teachers work an average of 51 hours per week, with continuous child contact and little time for planning


or reflection. Rigid staffing norms and the absence of support staff force teachers to juggle instructional


and care duties, creating stress and limiting opportunities for professional learning. These structural


pressures compromise instructional quality and teacher retention. The confluence of these factors–too


few teachers, heavy workloads, lagging qualifications, and weak professional status–poses a serious risk


to ECEC service quality.


**Professional development and career pathways remain misaligned.** School-based mentoring and peer


learning occur but lack depth, structured follow-up, and protected time. Career progression hinges on


seniority and credentials rather than demonstrated teaching competence, leaving mentoring roles and


contract teachers undervalued. Salary reforms under the _Law on Educators_ signal progress, yet gaps in


recognition and advancement persist, creating a two-tier profession that discourages motivation and


long-term commitment.


_Driver C: Enriching classroom practices and child-centered pedagogy_


**ECE Program reform has advanced, but classroom practice remains uneven.** Viet Nam’s ECE Program


promotes play-based, inclusive, and competency-driven learning, yet implementation often falls short.


While classrooms are orderly and teachers maintain positive climates, guided play, emotional support,


and inclusive participation are inconsistent. Observations show limited use of open-ended questions,


feedback, and scaffolding—key elements of child-centered pedagogy. Inclusive practices for children


with disabilities remain rare, and teachers report low confidence in adapting activities for diverse


learners.


_Driver D: Advancing quality assurance_


**Viet Nam has laid important foundations for early childhood quality assurance, yet structural**


**challenges limit its ability to ensure safe, inclusive, and developmentally rich experiences for all**


**children.** Decentralized QA suffers from uneven professional capacity and weak cross-sector


coordination; quality standards remain insufficiently evidence-based and inconsistently linked to child

**|** 9


development benchmarks; and external QA remains overly administrative, driven by short-term dossier


preparation rather than continuous improvement. Meanwhile, the data ecosystem largely operates as a


one-way reporting channel with fragmented provincial systems and limited analytical feedback, and


accountability mechanisms remain too weak to drive meaningful change. Addressing these


interconnected challenges is essential for building a coherent QA system that reliably measures progress,


supports educators, and delivers transparent, high-quality early learning for every child.


_Expanding quality non-public early childhood education and care provision_


**Both public and non-public providers struggle to keep up and maintain quality with rising enrollment**


**demand in underserved locales.** Industrial zones and densely populated city suburbs illustrate this


challenge vividly: these areas have seen a baby boom linked to migrant labor, causing demand for ECEC


to surge, yet provision has not kept pace. By 2025, the number of young children (under age 6) in


industrial park communities is projected to approach one million, as noted above. However, formal


childcare and ECE services in such areas remain scarce–childcare for under-3-year-olds currently reaches


only about one-fifth of eligible children in industrial zones.


**The gap is partly filled by small homebased child groups, but many operate- with limited oversight**


**and uneven quality.** MOET survey findings (2023) indicate that homebased classes account for a large


share of facilities serving industrial zones; more formal center--based provision remains- limited in these


highd--emand areas.. The overall picture is that even as national enrollment averages rise, certain


groups–notably children from poor rural families, migrants, and working mothers in factory zones–still


face significant barriers to enrolling their children in quality ECEC. These inequities in access risk


widening developmental gaps between advantaged and disadvantaged children, undermining the


inclusive goals of the universal ECEC for kindergarten children policy.


**Addressing these interwoven challenge areas is vital for Viet Nam to ensure that its ambitious ECEC**


**expansion translates into quality improvements in child development** . The government’s reform


agenda explicitly aims to tackle many of these issues at the strategic level. This makes it imperative to


closely manage the expansion process with a clear focus on quality at each step of implementation. The


next sections of this report delve into the determinants of ECEC quality in Viet Nam, analyzing how


policy and practice can be strengthened to overcome these challenges.

_Expanding and aligning the ECEC workforce pipeline_





**In the near term, Viet Nam needs a larger, more strategically developed pipeline of ECEC teachers to**


**meet growing demand.** The government should coordinate with TTIs to plan for pre- and in-service


training of teachers and ensure deployment to the provinces facing the worst shortages. This requires


setting clear targets for expanding teacher education enrollment and aligning training orders and quotas


with projected demand, while investing in TTI capacity where needed, so that the supply of graduates


keeps pace with ECEC expansion. Equally important is improving the quality of teacher preparation.


National guidelines can align all pre-service training programs with the updated professional standards


and child-centered ECE program, emphasizing practical skills through sufficient practicum experience


and coursework on inclusive, play-based pedagogy.


**Attracting talented candidates into ECEC teaching–and getting them where they are most needed–is**


**another priority** . Measures such as targeted scholarships and bonding programs could encourage more


high-caliber students, including those from under-represented ethnic groups, to choose careers in early


education. To address chronic staffing gaps in remote or disadvantaged areas, the government can


strengthen incentive schemes, beginning with piloted, tiered deployment incentives that reflect the


remoteness and needs of each location, making it more attractive for qualified teachers to serve in high

need communities.


**|** 13


**The workforce pipeline must also expand to serve the growing non-public facilities.** Introducing shorter,


certified training courses for child caregivers and forming partnerships (for example, between teacher


colleges and industrial zones) can create new pathways to bring local women into the ECEC workforce.


_Improving working conditions to retain and support teachers_


**To attract and retain quality teachers, Viet Nam must continue strengthening their day-to-day working**


**conditions.** The government should prioritize investing in these basics–building more classrooms in


high-demand areas, equipping schools with child-friendly learning materials, and raising teacher pay


(especially in under-resourced regions)–so that teachers can focus on teaching rather than making do


with shortages. For non-public ECEC institutions, targeted support measures (such as wage subsidies or


hardship allowances) could help improve compensation and stability for staff in disadvantaged areas,


narrowing the gap in work conditions between public and private centers.


**Another pressing priority is to lighten the excessive workloads shouldered by many ECEC teachers.**


Current staffing norms should be updated to reduce class sizes and allow a more flexible deployment of


personnel beginning with piloted “staffing-per-child” models that introduce classroom aides or


paraprofessionals to support teachers in large classes. This tiered workforce strategy would free up


trained teachers to focus on planning, instruction, and monitoring children’s progress, while support


staff handle routine caregiving and administrative tasks. By relieving untenable workloads and stress,


such reforms improve job satisfaction and give teachers the bandwidth to adopt more effective teaching


practices.


**Finally, teachers need a robust professional development system and incentives aligned with quality.**


Viet Nam has laid the groundwork for teacher advancement–with revised professional standards, a new


CPD framework, and clearer career pathways–and the next phase must focus on effective


implementation. This includes protected time for instructional leadership, mentoring, and school-level


professional learning, delivered through career-stage CPD pathways. Teachers should receive regular


training, coaching, and feedback focused on effective classroom practice, guided by the new standards


and the child-centered ECE Program. At the same time, promotions and job stability need to be tied to


demonstrated teaching competencies and contributions to children’s learning growth, rather than just


years of service. Realigning professional support and incentives will motivate educators to continually


hone their skills and remain in the ECEC field.


_Enriching classroom practice to strengthen early learning_


**High-quality early learning depends on what happens inside the ECE classroom.** Developing user

friendly pedagogical guides–for instance, clear rubrics and illustrated exemplars of effective learning


activities–would make the ECE Program’s child-centered principles more tangible in daily practice. Such


**|** 14


resources should be built into both pre-service and in-service training, ensuring that from teacher


colleges to the school level, professional development focuses on how to implement active, engaging


learning experiences. With better support in interpreting the **ECE Program**, teachers can create more


stimulating, inclusive classrooms that truly foster each child’s development.


**As a medium-term priority, continuous improvement should be embedded into classroom practice.**


Teachers and school leaders should regularly reflect on and refine teaching techniques, rather than


simply follow prescribed routines. To facilitate this, the inspection and coaching process can be


reoriented toward giving teachers constructive feedback on their classroom interactions. Using simple


observation tools, mentor teachers or principals can assess how engaged children are, whether play and


rich conversation are happening, and how well teachers are meeting diverse students’ needs–and then


discuss specific ways to improve. Likewise, more opportunities for peer-to-peer learning will strengthen


practice: teachers should have time to observe each other’s classes, share ideas for effective activities,


and jointly solve challenges in reaching children of different abilities.


_Building a quality assurance and data system that supports continuous improvement_


**Clarify and operationalize minimum quality standards across provider types.**


**Over the short to medium term, sustained quality gains will require a robust QA system underpinned**


**by good data** . A first step is to establish clear and consistent quality standards for all ECEC institutions


nationwide, including non-public centers. These standards should encompass not only structural inputs


but also the process elements that matter for children’s development–for example, teacher-child


interaction quality and how children progress in their learning. A unified QA framework covering ages


0–5 across all provider types would set baseline expectations for health, safety, and learning


environments, with flexibility to account for different settings (such as home-based care).


**Equally important is investing in local capacity to enforce and support these standards.** Where


resources allow, provinces can phase in quality support functions—for example by designating and


training focal points within existing teams, piloting classroom-based support in high-need districts, and


scaling as capacity grows—so that decentralization of preschool oversight leads to innovation and


improvement rather than widening gaps.


**Quality monitoring practices also need to lean more toward continuous improvement.** Inspections and


accreditation visits should be more about observing real teaching and guiding teachers. Simplified


observational tools can be adopted so that a substantial portion of each external evaluation–at least


half the time, for instance–is spent in classrooms watching teacher–child interactions and providing


feedback. Critically, any issues identified through inspections should trigger follow-up support, such as


additional training or mentoring for ECE institutions, making the QA process a springboard for


**|** 15


development rather than a one-off audit. Building such a supportive monitoring culture will require


training inspectors in effective observation and feedback; where government capacity is stretched,


accredited third-party experts could be engaged to help evaluate and coach private preschools as they


expand. In parallel, schools should be supported with simple self-evaluation tools that help leaders and


teachers reflect on classroom practice and track improvement over time.


**Finally, strengthened data and information systems are essential to support quality improvement and**


**accountability** . The education management information system (EMIS) needs to cover and create a


universal registry of all ECEC providers–public, private, and home-based. Each ECE institution would be


tracked with key information such as its location, enrollment, staffing, and licensing status, enabling


authorities to map service gaps and monitor quality across the system.


Across a mixed delivery system, measures that enable entry and expansion of non-public provision need


to be paired with a regulatory and QA framework that applies consistently to public, non-public, and


home-based settings. This includes minimum standards that make process quality observable—


including interactions and pedagogy—rather than relying predominantly on input compliance. The


report therefore emphasizes aligning incentives with enforceable standards and strengthening local


capacity so that monitoring supports continuous improvement.


_Enabling quality provision in a mixed delivery system_


**As Viet Nam moves toward universal ECEC for kindgarten children, the non-public sector will be an**


**essential partner in expanding access; realizing this potential will depend on an enabling and**


**regulatory environment that links support measures to compliance with minimum quality standards**


**and brings non-public settings into routine monitoring and quality improvement** . The government can


help lower these barriers through targeted financial support. For example, working with banks to offer


subsidized loans or credit guarantees for childcare businesses, and providing startup grants or matching


funds to help new providers meet upfront infrastructure and safety requirements, would make it easier


for ECEC institutions to launch and comply with standards. In addition, incentives such as tax breaks or


reduced utility fees for employers that establish childcare centers can improve financial viability,


especially if paired with subsidies that encourage private providers to serve low-income working families


while upholding quality benchmarks. A practical first step would be a focused policy paper or


consultation process, led by MOET and involving local authorities, providers, and employers, to define


proportional regulatory, financing, and QA approaches for different non-public providers.


**Another priority is better market information and coordination** . By mapping areas where demand for


ECEC is high but supply is lacking (for instance, in fast-growing industrial zones) and facilitating


investment matches–through forums that connect investors, local authorities, and childcare


entrepreneurs–the government can steer private provision to where it is needed most. All these


**|** 16


measures should be coupled with the robust QA framework described above, so that as the private


sector grows, it adheres to the same high standards and continuous improvement ethos as public


schools. With financial, regulatory, and informational support, Viet Nam’s mixed delivery system can


expand ECEC access in a sustainable way that keeps quality front and center.

### **Analytical framework: Four drivers of preschool quality and equity**


**Viet Nam’s early childhood education and care (ECEC) system is delivered through a mixed model,**


comprising public preschools, private and community-based providers, and a large number of


independent ECEC centers. While governance arrangements, financing mechanisms, and oversight differ


across provider types, the foundations of quality—teacher preparation and support, effective classroom


practice, and strong quality assurance—apply system-wide. Accordingly, the drivers of preschool quality


examined in this chapter are relevant to both public and non-public provision, with differentiation in


implementation addressed where necessary.

# **DRIVER A. PRODUCING MORE HIGH-QUALITY EARLY CHILDHOOD** **EDUCATION AND CARE TEACHERS**

### **Introduction: Building a Skilled and Equitable Workforce**

**Viet Nam’s goal of expanding access to quality ECEC is reshaping teacher demand.** With near-universal


enrollment nearly achieved for 4- and 5-year-olds, the next phase of reform focuses on producing a


sufficient count of high-quality teachers to serve additional children, especially 3-year-olds.


**Achieving these goals depends on a stable, well-qualified, and equitably deployed teaching force.** Yet


teacher shortages, uneven distribution, and rigid workforce-planning mechanisms continue to constrain


progress. Strengthening the Early Childhood Education and Care (ECEC) workforce is central to delivering


on Viet Nam’s broader education and human capital objectives and the systemic reforms outlined in the


Politburo’s _Resolution on Education Breakthroughs_, approved in 2025. [1]


**Teachers are the strongest single driver of ECEC quality.** [2] High-quality teachers are the foundation of


early learning, and their preparation, equitable deployment, and professional growth will determine


whether Viet Nam’s ambitious ECEC goals can be achieved. Building a strong pipeline—supported by


policies that attract capable candidates, equip them with relevant skills, and place them where they are


most needed—is therefore central to delivering quality early learning for all children, regardless of


whether teachers ultimately work in public or non-public preschool settings.


**Over the past decade, Viet Nam has made steady gains in professionalizing its ECEC workforce.** The


number of teachers rose from about 326,000 in 2018–2019 to 362,000 in 2023–2024, an increase of 11


percent. More than 90 percent now hold at least a pedagogical diploma. These workforce gains have


enabled wider access to ECEC and built a stronger foundation for sustained quality improvement.


**Yet sustaining this momentum remains challenging in specific ways.** Persistent teacher shortages,


uneven preservice quality, and rigid recruitment and deployment mechanisms have prevented the


system from keeping pace with rising demand and more effective classroom practices. The current


workforce system functions as a set of parallel efforts among multiple ministries, provinces, and teacher


training institutions (TTIs) rather than as a single, coherent pipeline.


**To build a high-quality and equitable workforce, Viet Nam must therefore address four interlinked,**


**system-level challenges** that constrain both supply and quality and are the key drivers of ECEC quality:


(a) National shortages and uneven distribution—a shortfall of more than 54,000 teachers and large


regional disparities in staffing.


**|** 18


(b) A constrained production pipeline—declining intake among institutes of teacher education,


inconsistent use of training order mechanisms, and weak alignment between training supply


and where new teachers are needed locally.


(c) Uneven quality of preservice training—limited practicum experience, variable lecturer


qualifications, and weak connection to child-centered pedagogy.


(d) Persistent inequities in workforce composition—heavy reliance on contract teachers and


underrepresentation of ethnic minority and male teachers in disadvantaged areas.


**Addressing these systemic constraints is critical to sustaining the progress of the past decade** . By


aligning teacher preparation, deployment, and professional growth within a coherent national ECEC


pipeline, Viet Nam can produce the skilled, motivated workforce needed to achieve equitable, high

quality early childhood education nationwide.

### **Key Workforce Challenges**


_**Challenge 1. National Teacher Shortage and Uneven Distribution**_


**Viet Nam’s decade of rapid ECEC expansion has outpaced teacher supply.** Ambitious plans to


universalize ECEC for children ages 3–5 and to enforce minimum staffing norms have sharply increased


demand for trained teachers, yet recruitment has not kept pace. [3] The result is persistent shortages that


raise group sizes, increase workloads, and limit the quality of classroom practices, including guided play


learning activities. Without adequate staffing, the gains of expansion risk eroding and jeopardizing


instructional quality. [4]


**The national shortfall now exceeds 54,000 teachers—about 13 percent of the centrally approved staff**


**ceiling.** [5] Shortages are most acute in the Central Highlands (21 percent) and North Central and Central


Coast (18 percent), compared with 11 percent in the Southeast and 14 percent in the Red River Delta


(Figure 6). Disparities remain very wide, from under 5 percent in some Red River and Mekong provinces


to over 30 percent in parts of the Central Highlands and Northern Central Coast (Figure 7). At the same


time, several Red River Delta provinces—notably Hai Duong, Hung Yen, and Bac Ninh—report localized


surpluses of **ECEC** teachers, revealing uneven distribution rather than uniform scarcity across the system.

**Staffing levels fall short of class size requirements despite apparent compliance with child-teacher**


**ratios.** Public preschools currently employ an average of 1.8 teachers per class—below the statutory


requirement of 2.2 for kindergarten classes and 2.5 for nursery groups as set out in the 2023 staffing


norms of the Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) for state-run preschools. [6] Teacher–class ratios


range from 2.0 in the Red River Delta to just 1.7 in the Central Highlands (Figure ). This corresponds to


about 15 children per teacher—within Viet Nam’s legal ceiling of 1:15.9 but close to its upper limit.

**National averages mask substantial variation in class sizes and learning conditions.** Administrative


statistics show an average of 24 children per class, yet the 2025 Learning Early Years (LEY) survey reports


a median of 26, with nearly 1 in 10 classes enrolling more than 35 children . [7] These findings suggest that


national ratios obscure large local disparities, with some classrooms operating at sizes that limit


individualized, play-based instruction. Internationally, Viet Nam’s teacher–child ratios are comparable


**|** 20


to those reported by Southeast Asian neighbors but remain high relative to high-performing early


childhood systems such as Australia (1:11) and Finland (1:7). These richer staffing levels are associated


with stronger child outcomes and more sustainable teacher workloads. [8]


**Local reliance on contract teachers has produced an uneven workforce across regions.** Contract


teachers make up roughly one-third of the ECEC workforce nationwide, ranging from about 10 percent


in the Northern Midlands to nearly 60 percent in the Southeast (Figure 9Figure ). This reflects how


provinces have increased child enrollments under fixed staffing ceilings, leading to pronounced


differences in workforce composition and teacher stability across regions. The tendency to employ more


contract employees appears to stem from fragmentation in the planning, approval, and financing of


teacher posts.

**Rigid and slow-moving quota systems limit timely recruitment.** Under civil service management,


teacher numbers are fixed through centrally approved ceilings that change slowly over time. [9] Even with


the additional 38,925 ECEC posts authorized for 2022–2026, the system will still fall short of current


demand—national shortages already exceed 54,000 teachers. [10] Because quotas are approved for


multiyear periods and rarely revised mid-cycle, provinces cannot recruit in line with enrollment growth


or local needs.


**Short-term contracts provide stopgap relief but no structural solution.** To sustain classroom coverage,


provinces are permitted to hire teachers on short-term contracts. [11] This flexibility has helped


accommodate rising ECEC enrollment but has produced a dual workforce with uneven employment


conditions and high turnover, especially in disadvantaged areas.


**Fragmented governance and data compound the challenge.** MOET oversees teacher education and


professional standards, the Ministry of Home Affairs (MOHA) manages staffing ceilings, and the Ministry


of Finance (MOF) allocates budgets. Without a shared planning mechanism or integrated data system,


**|** 21


workforce projections remain ad hoc. [12] Teacher training output and school-level vacancies are rarely


aligned, producing underemployed graduates in some regions and persistent shortages in others.


**Recent reforms mark a turning point toward a more coordinated and evidence-based teacher**


**management.** The 2025 _Education Breakthrough Resolution_ advances formal coordination among


ministries and streamlining teacher and budgeting data systems. [13] Subsequent decrees intend to


strengthen MOET’s mandate to align teacher policy with education planning. [14] The impact of these


measures will depend on effective implementation, ideally advancing real-time data exchange and more


effective recruitment and deployment decisions.


_**Challenge 2. A Constrained Production Pipeline**_


**Sustaining ECEC expansion depends on whether the teacher training pipeline, which varies widely**


**among provinces, can keep pace.** Despite new financial incentives—such as tuition and allowance


support for pedagogy students [15] and training-order contracts for hard-to-staff areas [16] —supply remains


poorly aligned with local needs. Some provinces continue to face persistent shortages while others


produce more graduates than they can employ. Unless these imbalances are corrected, the system risks


deepening regional inequities.


**The flow of new teachers entering the sector has slowed.** Evidence from leading TTIs is stark: between


2020 and 2023, intake fell by 22 percent at Hanoi National University of Education, 18 percent at Ho Chi


Minh City University of Education, and nearly 25 percent at Thai Nguyen University of Education.


Enrollment of teacher candidates at Da Nang and Vinh Universities of Education remained flat despite


continued expansion of ECEC provision (Figure 10). These patterns suggest that the supply of new


teachers is tightening even as the ECEC sector continues to expand. Survey data also reveal an aging


teaching force. Only about 10 percent of ECEC teachers have fewer than five years’ experience,


suggesting limited inflow of new entrants and a tightening supply pipeline.

**The main initiative by the Government of Viet Nam (GoV) to expand teacher intake has yielded**


**modest results.** The tuition support and living allowance scheme for pedagogy students was introduced


to boost enrollment through provincial training orders and guarantee postgraduation jobs in areas of


need. [17] Yet implementation remains modest. Between 2021 and 2023, only about one-third of provinces


issued training orders and budget execution covered just over half of the requested funds. [18] Limited


provincial participation and financing shortfalls have constrained the scheme’s ability to channel new


entrants to underserved areas.


The slowdown in intake and the limited impact of financial incentives stem from two deeper structural


constraints: centralized, input-driven planning and fragmented financing.


**Centralized and input-driven planning constrains the teacher training pipeline.** While MOET's targets


for admitting new teacher trainees are nominally linked to workforce needs, allocation decisions are


often dominated by input-based criteria. That is, lecturer-student ratios and the capacity of TTI facilities


drive enrollments, rather than projected regional shortages of ECEC teachers. [19] Pedagogical universities


may propose quotas, but MOET sets the final limits, and at the college level, quotas are set directly by


the ministry. Several institutions report demand from local authorities to expand ECEC training but are


restricted by the current MOET-approved quotas. There is also no structured feedback between TTIs and


provinces on graduate employment, limiting the ability to adjust enrollment targets to actual workforce


absorption. This structure limits institutional autonomy and responsiveness to local shortages.


**Financing and implementation bottlenecks limit the impact of the national teacher training support**


**scheme.** GoV has adopted policies for tuition fee support and living allowances for students enrolled in


teacher training programs, aiming to attract and retain qualified candidates through commissioned or


ordered training. [20] But the program’s effectiveness remains uneven because provincial co-financing and


quota approvals lag local demand for new teachers. The scheme’s reliance on joint central–provincial


financing has constrained its coverage, further limiting the teacher pipeline. [21]


**Reform momentum to address financing bottlenecks is under way.** GoV has issued new regulations


strengthening the teacher training support framework, clarifying funding responsibilities between


central and provincial budgets and linking training orders to local recruitment plans. [22] These revisions


mark an initial step toward closer alignment of teacher-education supply with workforce demand.


Implementation remains at an early stage and will require consistent monitoring and funding discipline.


_**Challenge 3. Uneven Quality and Coherence of Initial Teacher Education (ITE)**_


**Viet Nam has strengthened the qualification and entry requirements for ECEC teachers,** aiming to


professionalize the workforce and improve ECEC quality. [23] Yet ITE programs have not kept pace with


these higher expectations. The central challenge is misalignment: while standards now demand deeper


**|** 23


pedagogical and practice-based competence, most ITE programs remain theory-heavy and unevenly


structured across TTIs. [24]


**Practice-based learning receives little formal credit in ECEC teacher education programs, with wide**


**variation in overall course structure across institutions.** Among 11 representative TTIs, the share of


credits devoted to pedagogical and professional courses ranges from 51 to 79 percent, while practicum [25]


and internship components together account for only 6–13 percent—well below the international


benchmarks of 20–30 percent (Figure 11; Appendix A, Table A1). [26] This limited allocation for applied


learning reflects the national credit-weighting rule, which counts one fieldwork credit as two to three


times the contact hours of a theory credit. [27] However, the figures indicate that practical training receives


limited formal recognition within program structures.


_Figure 11. Credit distribution in ECEC teacher training programs_


Source: Authors’ compilation of eleven ITE course outlines


**Despite limited formal weighting, practicum remains a central feature of ECEC teacher education.** All


programs include a sequence of observation, guided teaching, and final year internship. This provides


student teachers with direct classroom experience before graduation (Table A2). Many institutions also


embed microteaching and child study tasks within pedagogy courses to extend practice opportunities.


Even so, total practicum time—typically 12–18 weeks—falls short of the 15- to 20-week benchmark


observed internationally. [28] Larger universities sustain partnerships with designated preschools and


mentors, while smaller colleges rely on short-term arrangements, resulting in variable mentoring quality


and exposure to diverse classroom contexts. [29]


**Beyond the structure of partnerships, the content of field experience during practicum remains**


**heavily weighted toward caregiving and routine activities, not imbibing effective learning activities.**


TLEC survey data show that student teachers most frequently engage in caregiving and supervision tasks,


while opportunities to implement play-based learning, document children’s progress, or participate in


reflective discussions occur less often (Figure 12). Among teachers with three years of experience or less,


**|** 24


ratings of how well their preservice training prepared them averaged just 3.1 on a 4-point scale,


indicating moderate but uneven confidence in professional readiness.

**Despite strong curricular content and foundations in child development, teacher education programs**


**rarely help trainees connect theory with play-based pedagogy, serving children with disabilities, and**


**inquiry-driven practice.** While this reflects a long-standing emphasis on child welfare and didactic


curriculum delivery, applied dimensions of teaching receive less attention. Courses on play-based


pedagogy, classroom management, and formative assessment of teaching practices are often elective


or briefly integrated into other subjects. Crucial areas such as digital integration and cultural and


linguistic diversity remain marginal (Table A3). As a result, graduates understand developmental


milestones and curriculum content but are less equipped to foster inquiry, inclusion, and adaptation in


diverse classroom contexts.


**Alignment with current Professional Standards for ECEC Teachers (PST) is partial.** Although the PST


articulates comprehensive expectations for teachers’ knowledge, practice, and collaboration with peers,


most ITE curricula focus narrowly on caregiving and children’s holistic development. Core pedagogical


domains—such as planning, observation, and creating inclusive learning environments—and


collaborative areas like family and community engagement are seldom reflected in course content or


practicum design (Table A4). This gap underscores the need for clearer alignment between graduate


expectations and the competency benchmarks that teachers are expected to meet in practice.


**Lecturer qualifications in early childhood education faculties have improved but remain uneven across**


**institutions.** Under MOET’s _Program 33_ (2018–2025), about 90 percent of lecturers now hold a master’s


degree, while only around 10 percent hold a doctorate, [30] falling below the program’s 2025 target. [31]


**|** 25


Better training faculty remain concentrated in major universities such as Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City.


Smaller regional colleges have few or no doctoral qualified staff, limiting practicum supervision and


linking research to classroom practice. Many lecturers lack recent preschool experience, constraining


mentoring support for student teachers. _Program 33_ expanded postgraduate study and short


professional courses, but disparities in applied expertise and supervision experience persist nationwide.


These inconsistencies in program quality and innovation reflect system-level planning, regulatory, and


financing arrangements, rather than weaknesses in institutional effort or demand. Teacher training


institutions operate within nationally defined frameworks for enrollment quotas, credit structures, and


financing that shape how programs are designed and delivered.


**Variation in the quality of ITE stems from how TTI autonomy is structured and governed.** Under Viet


Nam’s higher education autonomy framework, TTIs design their own curricula, staffing models, and


partnerships with local preschools. [32] MOET provides only broad qualification descriptors with no binding


guidance on practicum duration, pedagogical balance, or alignment with the PST. [33] The credit-weighting


rule, which defines one fieldwork credit as 45 hours versus 15 hours for theory, discourages expansion


of practicum components. [34] Without a national framework defining minimum pedagogical content and


practicum standards, program design remains inconsistent and graduate readiness uneven. These


variations are compounded by weak coordination mechanisms: TTIs have not been systematically


consulted in recent ECE Program and workforce reforms; a gap repeatedly noted in stakeholder


consultations.


**Internal quality assurance mechanisms also limit improvement.** According to MOET’s Program 33


Report (2018–2025), internal reviews concentrate on lecturer inputs and procedural compliance rather


than graduate outcomes. [35] Promising innovations—such as integrated modules and competency-based


assessment of teachers—remain isolated without national benchmarks or graduate-tracking systems,


and feedback from preschools rarely informs ITE curriculum review.


**Methods for developing lecturers further entrench the theory–practice divide.** Promotion criteria


prioritize academic credentials, publication records, and seniority over teaching quality or practicum


supervision. [36] Program 33 (2018–2025) upgraded lecturer qualifications and introduced short


professional courses, but gaps persist. Many lecturers—particularly those recruited from non-ECEC


disciplines—still lack opportunities for learning about pedagogical strategies and learning how research


can inform teacher training. This omission is reinforced by the current in-service training regulation,


which targets practicing teachers but excludes those delivering ITE. [37] Consequently, lecturers have few


options for refreshing their pedagogical knowledge or strengthening links with classroom practice.


**Recent policy directions aim to strengthen coherence and lecturer quality.** GoV’s 2025 plan to


rationalize the pedagogy-institution network [38] and MOET’s draft professional standards for university


**|** 26


lecturers [39] aim to embed teaching and leadership competencies within career frameworks. Alongside


the forthcoming revised PST, these initiatives could help close current coordination gaps and provide the


foundation for a competency-based system that directly links lecturer expertise, program quality, and


classroom practice.


_**Challenge 4. Inequitable Distribution and Representation in the ECEC Workforce**_


**Viet Nam’s ECEC workforce remains unevenly distributed and often fails to represent the families**


**served in disadvantaged areas.** Despite policy commitments to equity and incentives for teachers in


remote and mountainous regions, [40] shortages, high turnover, and low ethnic minority (EM)


representation constrain quality. Unless workforce deployment and support mechanisms are


strengthened, children in remote and low-income provinces will remain less likely to learn from


experienced and culturally responsive teachers.


**Teacher shortages are most acute in poorer and rural provinces.** The heaviest burden falls on areas that


are remote and economically depressed, where weaker staffing ratios and heavier child–teacher loads


reflect the combined effects of remoteness, limited local revenue, and recruitment challenges. Rural


provinces record shortfalls roughly three times higher than urban areas (about 25 percent versus 8


percent) and significantly fewer teachers per class. Similarly, provinces with income levels below half


the national average show the highest child–teacher ratios (Table A6). These patterns indicate that


poverty and geography—not national policy intent—remain the strongest determinants of workforce


distribution and children’s unequal access to quality ECEC.

**Representation gaps persist in EM and remote areas.** Ethnic minority (EM) teachers account for only


14 percent of Viet Nam’s ECEC workforce, compared with 18 percent of children enrolled nationally. This


disparity is far greater in EM-majority provinces: in Lai Châu, 68 percent of children but only 38 percent


of teachers are from ethnic minorities; in Lạng Sơn, the figures are 77 and 36 percent, respectively


(Appendix: Figure A1). These imbalances limit the system’s capacity to provide bilingual and culturally


responsive pedagogy precisely where it is most needed. Such disparities stem from structural rigidities


in how GoV manages teacher numbers and deployment. These patterns, also highlighted in stakeholder


consultations, reflect structural constraints rather than the intent of government policy.


**Fiscal constraints compound inequities in teacher supply.** Even when provinces wish to recruit new


teachers locally, after the national quota is exhausted, many lack the fiscal capacity to support additional


career or contract posts. [41] Wealthier provinces can afford to supplement teacher numbers from local


budgets, while poorer ones cannot. This imbalance reinforces disparities in staffing and representation,


leaving disadvantaged communes chronically understaffed and reliant on short-term contract teachers


whose employment conditions limit continuity and retention.


**Financial incentives exist but are weakly targeted.** Teachers in designated hardship communes receive


supplemental allowances, such as a one-time payment worth 10 months of salary and a 70 percent


monthly attraction allowance for up to five years. [42] But these function more as administrative


entitlements than as workforce planning tools. Because eligibility is tied to fixed geographic


classifications rather than current staffing needs or living conditions, the scheme offers little incentive


for teachers to relocate to the hardest-to-staff areas. Flat payment levels, tied to low salary levels, also


fail to recognize the increasing cost of living.


**No coherent framework guides representative recruitment.** The Education Development Strategy


2030–2045 emphasizes linguistic and cultural inclusion but sets no national targets, scholarships, or


reserved training orders for EM and bilingual ECEC teachers. [43] Without national direction or incentives,


provinces recruit independently, leading to wide variation in how—and whether—they prioritize EM


candidates. The result is a persistent gap between policy intent and actual representation in


mountainous and EM areas. The shortage of new teachers with versatile language skills may intensify


under GoV’s recent mandate to provide some instruction in English.


**Recent reforms are beginning to address both structural and motivational barriers to equitable**


**teacher deployment.** The _Education Breakthrough_ resolution directs GoV to raise hardship and


preferential allowances for teachers in disadvantaged, border, and EM areas, aligning with provisions in


the new Law on Educators. [44] Together, these measures create a stronger foundation for workforce equity,


though their impact will depend on how effectively provinces implement the new incentive frameworks.

_**From Diagnosis to Action: Strengthening the Entry Pipeline**_


**Viet Nam’s progress toward universal ECEC for kindgarten children will likely accelerate under the**


**2025 reform package.** These policy shifts may remove long-standing structural barriers to workforce


planning. The _Education Breakthrough Resolution_ and accompanying decrees on MOET’s structure and


decentralization have created a foundation for unified, data-driven teacher management. Yet converting


these reforms into sustained workforce improvements will require consistent implementation, shared


accountability among ministries, and effective use of the new national teacher-management platform


to guide planning and budgeting decisions. The challenge ahead is less about design and more about


execution—turning new coordination frameworks into tangible gains to increase the production of new


ECEC teachers and distribute them fairly among the provinces.

# **DRIVER B. STRENGTHENING WORKING CONDITIONS TO MOTIVATE** **AND RETAIN HIGH-QUALITY TEACHERS**

### **Introduction: Building a Supportive and Sustainable Profession**

**Recruiting and preparing new teachers, as discussed in Driver A, marks the first step in strengthening**


**the ECEC workforce.** Sustaining those already in classrooms requires supportive working conditions,


meaningful opportunities for professional learning, and clear pathways for recognition and


advancement—hallmarks of high-performing systems such as those in Finland or Singapore, where


teachers are buoyed as professionals engaged in continuous learning. [45]


**Viet Nam’s** _**Law on Educators**_ **, part of the broader 2025 reform package, reflects this ambition for**


**delivering professional development, career advancement, and fair compensation.** [46] Complementary


regulations on school organization, professional standards, and career-title frameworks motivate a


profession that is based on competence, collaboration, and lifelong learning. [47] Together, these


instruments help structure continuous professional development (CPD) for teachers, along with revised


PST, aiming to advance teachers’ growth and daily classroom practices.


**Preschool leaders in several provinces already experiment with promising models.** For example, peer

learning clusters in Thai Nguyen, mentoring networks in Ho Chi Minh City, and blended modules led by


TTIs show how effective CPD can be achieved within existing resources (see Appendix B, Table B1). The


next step is to ensure these promising practices become consistent and sustainable across the ECEC


sector.


To build a motivated and high-quality teaching force, Viet Nam must address four interlinked system

level challenges in how professional support, time, and incentives are structured and experienced within


the system, which together constrain teachers’ professional conditions and their capacity to remain and


thrive in the profession:


(a) **Heavy workloads and limited professional time** —Long hours, rigid staffing norms, and


nonteaching demands leave teachers with little time for planning, reflection, or professional


learning.


(b) **Generic in-service training disconnected from classroom improvement** —While participation is


widespread, the quality and focus of existing in-service training vary considerably. Many


programs remain too broad and repetitive to help teachers improve classroom activities for


children.


**|** 32


(c) **Frequent but superficial professional-support systems** —Mentoring, observation, and


collaboration with peers do occur, but often with little depth or sustained efforts to improve


pedagogical practices.


(d) **Limited career structures and promotion incentives that prioritize seniority** —How teachers


are promoted emphasizes seniority and credentials over demonstrated competence and the


leadership skills that strengthen classroom practice.


By linking support, growth, and recognition within a coherent national framework, Viet Nam can


continue to improve ECEC teaching—a respected profession that attracts and develops skilled educators.


The report next turns to each of these key challenges—what can be seen as constraints on this second


driver of ECEC quality.

### **Key Support and Growth Challenges**


_**Challenge 1. Heavy Workloads and Limited Time**_


**Viet Nam’s government and MOET already demonstrate a strong commitment to improving**


**conditions for ECEC teachers and learners.** [48] Yet the daily reality for many teachers remains highly


demanding with modest compensation. Teachers are expected to implement a more play-based and


inclusive curriculum while also managing childcare routines, documentation, and family engagement—


often with little time for planning or collaboration. Teachers must plan a curriculum or learning activities


for 30 or 40 children. Heavy workloads and scarce time affect both teacher's well-being and instructional


quality, leaving little opportunity for the sustained, reflective practice that quality ECEC requires.


**ECEC teachers work long hours with limited professional time.** Official statistics indicate an average


41.9-hour workweek, yet results from the LEY survey suggest a true workload of about 51.5 hours,


including more than 42 hours of continuous child contact. [ 49] Contract teachers (≈57 hours) and Grade IV


staff [50] (≈53 hours) report the longest schedules, leaving little scope for preparation, reflection, or


learning with peers (Table B2).


**Teachers’ time is concentrated on planning and spread across multiple responsibilities.** Survey data


show that planning and preparation take by far the largest share of teachers’ weekly time, with more


than one-third of all ECEC teachers devoting over 60 percent of their workweek to these tasks.


Substantial proportions also spend between one-third and one-half of their time recording children’s


progress or participating in professional development, while collaboration with parents and colleagues


takes a moderate share. Administrative work, care routines, and peer reflection occupy smaller but


significant portions of the week. The pattern illustrates the breadth of the ECEC teaching role—balancing


instruction, documentation, and communication within long hours that leave limited space for pursuing


classroom improvements.

**Teachers’ investment priorities underscore workload pressures.** Although all areas scored highly on a


3-point scale (where 1 = highest priority), teachers in both the LEY and TLEC surveys rated smaller group


sizes and reduced administrative load among their key areas for improvement (means ≈ 2.0). These


priorities followed closely behind pay and classroom resources, indicating that teachers see workload


relief—through additional staff and administrative support—as crucial as well (Table B3).


**The teaching role itself is the main source of stress.** Preparation workload (58 percent), behavior


management (56 percent), and responsibility for children’s outcomes (51 percent) rank highest among


stressful aspects of the teacher’s job, closely followed by recordkeeping and administrative demands


(about 49 percent each, Figure 16). Stress is common for teachers, especially when considering large


class size and reporting demands, all in the context of limited autonomy and professional discretion over


how work is organized. These patterns point to structural rather than individual causes—rooted in how


staffing and classroom roles are defined and resourced.

**Rigid staffing ratios prioritize administrative consistency over classroom flexibility.** Staffing rules and


quotas help ensure (variably) equitable teacher allocation among provinces. But these regulations


**|** 34


operate primarily as a budgeting tool rather than as a mechanism for adjusting classroom support to


local needs. Staffing rules, for example, set maximum staffing ratios—2.2 teachers per ECEC class and


2.5 in nursery groups—but create a fixed ceiling for payroll rather than a responsive baseline for


quality. [51] Although intended to promote efficiency, these limits make it difficult to adapt to enrollment


changes, class diversity, or teacher absences. This adds to time pressure on teachers, since they shoulder


both instructional and childcare duties with limited flexibility to adjust workloads based on local


exigencies.


While private preschools are not bound by public staffing quotas, survey evidence indicates that non

public teachers experience similarly high workloads, reflecting parallel constraints such as large groups,


limited support staff, and full child-contact schedules shaped by financial and enrollment pressures


rather than instructional design.


**The absence of support staff leaves teachers carrying the full classroom load.** These pressures are


compounded by the lack of paraprofessional or instructional support roles—a direct outcome of the


current ‘teachers-per-class’ model, which inherently excludes funding for additional classroom adults.


Staffing rules list administrative and service posts—accountants, clerks, nurses, cooks, and guards—but


omit teaching aides.


**Teachers shoulder multiple burdens with little support from instructional aides or support staff.** In


contrast, systems such as Singapore’s apply a tiered staffing model, where a diploma-qualified lead


teacher is supported by a certificate-qualified assistant, forming a collaborative team that shares


classroom and care responsibilities. [52] In Viet Nam, the absence of this second-tier workforce means


qualified teachers devote a large share of time to routine care and recordkeeping tasks, leaving limited


capacity for the planning, observation, and reflection that underpin quality teaching.


**Policy momentum points toward more sustainable conditions, not yet achieved.** The new _Law on_


_Educators_ establishes the right to a fair workload, and the _Party Resolution on Education Breakthroughs_


commits to financing “enough teachers” that would award teachers time and flexibility to focus on


classroom practice. These are important signals that recognize workload as a core quality issue, yet as


long as staffing norms remain administrative rather than pedagogical instruments, teacher allocation


will continue to follow budget logic instead of educational need—perpetuating uneven workloads and


constraining a preschool’s ability to enrich teacher knowledge and classroom practice.


_**Challenge 2. Generic In-Service Training Disconnected from Teacher Needs**_


This challenge concerns regular in-service training and applies to all preschool teachers across both


public and non-public early childhood education institutions, where participation in refresher training is


mandatory under national regulations.


**|** 35


**Viet Nam’s in-service training system for ECEC teachers is guided by professional development**


**procedures** [53] and upgrade program that includes three components: nationally mandated priorities,


provincially adapted priorities, and modules selected by teachers. [54] Together, these training elements


intend to balance policy priorities with each teacher’s individual learning needs.


**Despite this framework, teacher participation remains uneven across provinces and teacher groups.**


In-person attendance ranged from 35 percent in Quang Binh to 50 percent in Hai Phong, and even lower


rates online. Contract and non-public-sector teachers were least likely to attend (Figure 17). Survey data


identify distance, scheduling conflicts, and lack of substitutes as key barriers (Figure 19), yet provincial


supports—mainly reimbursement or after-hours scheduling—do little to remove them (Figure 18). Many


teachers therefore rely on self-study or peer learning to meet the 120-period requirement, showing that


low participation reflects structural inequities, not low commitment. These patterns point to constraints


in the depth, focus, and follow-up of in-service training, rather than an absence of professional


development opportunities or teacher engagement.

**Centrally organized training shows limited depth and practical utility.** LEY survey data suggest that


centrally defined topics are weighted toward administrative or policy themes. Of the 574 topics reported,


about one-third were labelled simply as ‘professional training’, and more than half focused on digital or


administrative areas such as Science, Technology, Engineering, Arts, and Mathematics (STEAM), artificial


intelligence, or recordkeeping, while areas central to early childhood pedagogy—child development,


play-based learning, and inclusion—appeared far less frequently (Table B4). Although 71 percent of


teachers rated trainers knowledgeable, only half found the sessions practical, and just 17 percent


reported follow-up on how to implement changes back in preschools or classrooms. As one teacher


noted, much of the training remains “lecture-based with little opportunity to apply what we learn.”


**Self-selected modules broaden access for teachers but often lack depth.** Content analysis of all 35


modules shows that they are largely theoretical—focused on general pedagogy, ethics, and safety, with


few applied tools helpful in improving classroom activities or tracking children’s progress. Topics such as


inclusive practice and family engagement remain introductory, while mentoring, adaptive classroom


practice and teacher well-being receive little or no attention (Table B5)—areas embedded in high

performing systems such as those in Singapore, Finland, and Canada. [55] Leadership-related content also


prioritizes regulations and procedures over instructional coaching and fostering children’s motivation to


learn (Table B6).


**School-based training under the self-selected stream is widely implemented but varies in quality.**


About three-quarters of teachers in the LEY participated in locally organized sessions based on the


modules. Most found them relevant (81 percent), but only 24 percent reported structured follow-up


through classroom observation or coaching. Twenty percent found these courses overloaded with


information, showing wide variation in depth and continuity across provinces.


**Training participation and perceived effectiveness decline with seniority and particular roles.** Senior


teachers (Grade I) report the largest gaps between participation and expressed need, and rate training


as less effective than junior colleagues (Figure 20). Core teachers—responsible for mentoring peers—


report collaboration-related training gaps that are about 14 percentage points larger than those of


regular teachers (Figure 21), showing that the school-level training stream remains generic rather


differentiated by career stage or mentoring responsibilities. These symptoms point to three underlying


constraints in the system design: incentive structures that prioritize compliance over differentiated


learning, a largely uniform curriculum, and uneven implementation capacity.

**Regulatory structures constrain professional learning.** Although the 2022 amendments to the in

service training rules introduced a competency-based orientation, reporting and budget systems still


assess ‘seat time’ and completion—120 periods and certificates—rather than what competencies


teachers gained from the training. [56] Provinces therefore design and evaluate training around


documentation rather than improved teaching practice and classroom impact. Governance reforms


under the 2025 package clarified roles but not accountability logic, so the system may still reward


attendance more than professional growth. [57] This pattern echoes findings from Project 33, which noted


that post-training evaluation and follow-up remained largely procedural, focusing on documentation


rather than evidence of classroom improvements. [58]


**A uniform training framework limits differentiation and practical application.** Teachers identify their


learning needs, weighed against national standards. Meanwhile, provincial plans consolidate the most


common themes, reinforcing basic proficiency instead of advanced or role-specific skills . [59] The


nationally approved module suite was meant to expand choice for teachers, but most content remains


theoretical and introductory . [60] Although Project 33 originally established a mechanism for annually


updating national CPD modules, this process was never maintained, contributing to the current problem


of outdated or repetitive content. [61] The draft Professional Standards may provide a stronger base for


differentiated planning, but their impact depends on clear operational guidance—such as mentoring


time and mechanisms linking self-assessment data to CPD design.


**The gap between barriers and supports reflects this same design logic.** Because training success is


defined by participation rates and certificates, provincial plans emphasize simple attendance over


**|** 38


addressing real obstacles such as heavy teacher workloads, distance, or Wi-Fi connectivity. Limited


feedback mechanisms mean that such constraints that teachers face rarely inform planning.


**Gaps in implementation weaken training quality and coherence.** Training delivery remains uneven


because providers receive limited guidance on duration, interactivity, and follow-up, and there is no


feedback loop from classroom practice to module design. Provincial autonomy is broad but lacks clear


quality parameters, coaching, or fiscal flexibility. Without a coherent framework to guide adaptation and


feedback, professional learning remains focused on information delivery rather than development of


specific competencies.


**Fragmented coordination limits the use of pedagogical expertise.** Responsibility for in-service training


is split across MOET departments and provincial authorities, while TTIs—formally identified as


partners—are rarely commissioned or included in planning or quality assurance. Overlapping mandates


between MOET’s Department of Teachers and Education Managers (DTEM) and Department of Early


Childhood Education (DECE) blur accountability, and the system lacks regional or university-based


training hubs that in high-performing systems connect national standards with local delivery. [62] With the


abolition of district education offices, this institutional gap has widened, leaving schools with limited


technical support.


**Taken together, these regulatory, curricular, and implementation gaps help explain why professional**


**development remains largely procedural rather than developmental** —achieving broad participation


but offering few opportunities for teachers to apply, refine, and sustain new practices in classrooms.


Recent decentralization reforms have expanded provincial autonomy in planning and delivery, but this


autonomy has not yet been matched with consistent technical capacity, fiscal flexibility, or clear


operational guidance to support more practice-focused professional learning.


_**Challenge 3. Frequent but Superficial Professional-Support Options**_


**Viet Nam has a strong policy framework for school-level professional support.** Centrally crafted tools


include professional standards, observation and mentoring of teachers, the designation of core teachers,


and peer learning embedded within preschools. [63] These mechanisms operate within schools and shape


professional practice across both public and non-public early childhood education institutions, though


their formal regulation, resourcing, and enforcement vary by sector and employment status. In principle,


these mechanisms form the practice-based layer of a CPD structure that links formal training with


everyday professional learning inside preschools. In current practice, mentoring, observation, and


collaborative activities are widely reported across schools, but typically occur in episodic and lightly


structured ways, and remain weakly connected to sustained improvement. The discussion that follows


examines how these support mechanisms operate in practice and where gaps remain.


**|** 39


**Induction and mentoring for new teachers remain procedural and uneven.** In public schools,


regulations require schools to provide induction and assign mentors to new teachers, while in non-public


settings similar practices are often adopted in practice but with less formal guidance or consistency.


Across both sectors, these processes seldom emphasize pedagogical development. [64] In the LEY survey,


only one in three teachers reported formal induction and just over half had a mentor, while the TLEC


survey shows higher participation but modest learning gains (mean = 2.9 / 4; Tables B7–B8). Induction


focuses mainly on planning (85 percent) and safety (59 percent), with less attention to inclusion (39


percent). Overall, induction systems vary widely in coverage and quality and only partially strengthen


classroom readiness or pedagogical confidence.


**School-based professional learning is widespread but limited in depth.** About two-thirds of teachers


in the TLEC survey reported receiving some form of school-based professional support—such as


observation, mentoring, or coaching—confirming that opportunities exist for most teachers.


Participation is highest for observation-based activities (around 50 percent), but much lower for


coaching (around 26 percent). While breadth is considerable, developmental intensity remains modest:


about half described their participation as ‘some’ and only 28 percent as ‘a lot’ (mean = 3.03 of 4).


**Regular professional meetings—intended under the Kindergarten Charter to foster reflection about**


**classroom practices—often prioritize administrative issues.** As one teacher noted, “We meet regularly,


but mainly to review plans, not to discuss teaching problems.” Teachers rate the value of school-based


learning only moderate (mean = 3.11 of 4), suggesting that its potential for deeper, more personalized


learning is largely untapped. These patterns suggest that while participation in school-based learning is


relatively broad, its more developmental forms reach only part of the workforce.


**Collaboration is regular and collegial but not deeply analytical.** Teachers report consistent participation


across collaborative tasks, with mean scores clustered around 3 on a 4-point scale (Figure 22). Planning


and material sharing are slightly more common than reflective discussions of children’s development,


suggesting that teamwork is established but not yet inquiry focused. Engagement is highest among


Grade I–II teachers, moderate among core teachers, and lowest among Grades III–IV, indicating that


collaboration expands with experience. The only area without a clear seniority effect is discussion of


individual children’s needs, where participation levels are comparably high, suggesting reflection on


children’s development is a shared professional focus.


**Collaborative activities operate mainly through informal networks rather than structured professional**


**learning communities (PLCs).** In both the LEY and TLEC surveys, most teachers reported sharing ideas


and discussing practice, but few identified belonging to a formal PLC or network—around 30 percent in


TLEC and below 5 percent in LEY. This contrasts with stakeholder perceptions that PLCs are widespread,


reflecting differences in how the concept is understood and practiced.

**Many preschools hold regular professional meetings that teachers may not regard as PLCs** because


these sessions focus on administrative coordination rather than reflective dialogue. Regular meetings


provide structure but not the sustained reflection or peer inquiry that characterize PLCs.


**International research consistently finds that professional learning communities (PLCs) are most**


**effective when they are structured, facilitated, and focused on instructional practice** rather than


administrative coordination. Evidence from OECD countries shows that collaborative professional


learning improves teaching quality when it involves joint inquiry into classroom practice, use of evidence


on children’s learning, and sustained peer reflection, rather than episodic information sharing [65] . Reviews


of PLC implementation highlight the importance of facilitation, instructional leadership, and protected


time in enabling collaboration to translate into improved pedagogical practice [66] .


**These symptoms of uneven and often superficial school-based support are not random.** They reflect


three interrelated factors shaping the current system: limited provision for mentoring time and


recognition, underdeveloped instructional leadership, and weak alignment between mentoring roles


and career advancement as an ECEC teacher.


**Structural constraints prevent school-based professional learning from becoming genuinely**


**developmental.** National policy expects preschools to organize peer observation and collaborative


learning yet provides no provision for reduced teaching loads or protected hours for teachers serving as


mentors in either public or non-public settings. [67] Continuous child contact, large classes, and


administrative reporting, discussed above, limit time for meaningful peer learning. Senior and core


teachers must add these duties to full schedules, often informally or after hours. Without staffing


adjustments or workload recognition, these activities are sustained largely through individual effort,


limiting their capacity to drive consistent improvements in classroom practice. As a result, these


**|** 41


expectations are absorbed into already heavy workloads and implemented unevenly, with particularly


fragile arrangements for contract and non-public teachers.


**Weak instructional leadership and limited mentoring limit teacher learning.** Core and senior teachers


are expected to mentor colleagues, and principals to guide instructional practice, yet both groups may


not be well prepared. [68] Regular in-service training includes no dedicated modules on mentoring or


instructional leadership. [69] In the TLEC survey, self-assessed mentoring and peer-support competencies


ranked lowest among all professional-practice domains for those expected to mentor others (Table B9).


Many may feel underprepared for the role.


**Preschool principals across all settings are required to convene regular professional meetings but**


**reportedly operate in a largely compliance-oriented culture emphasizing managerial duties over**


**pedagogical improvement.** [70] Without clear models for facilitating professional dialogue, these sessions


rarely become opportunities for reflection and learning. The revised draft PST address this gap by


defining collaboration through PLCs as a core expectation, offering a stronger foundation for mentoring


across career stages. [71]


**The removal of the formally designated core teacher category in the draft revised PST creates a**


**structural gap.** [72] Mentoring is now framed as a competency expected of senior teachers but not as a


distinct role, dissolving the dedicated mentoring layer that once linked national policy with daily school


practice. Current regulations on staffing norms, in-service training, and professional titles provide little


direction on how these functions should be organized. Future guidance will need to specify how mentors


are assigned and prepared.


In essence, Viet Nam’s professional-support system remains compliance-oriented. The _continuing_


dimension of professional development—reflection and peer inquiry that embed new knowledge in


practice—currently depends more on local initiative than on system design. To become a genuine engine


of professional learning, the focus would need to shift from delivering discrete training events to


cultivating a continuous culture of inquiry, feedback, and reflection among teachers within schools.


_**Challenge 4. Limited career structures and promotion incentives that prioritize seniority**_


This challenge concerns the structure and functioning of career progression and recognition systems,


which are formally defined for public-sector preschool teachers but shape expectations, motivation, and


perceived status across the wider ECEC workforce, including contract and non-public teachers.


The new _Law on Educators_ marks a major step toward elevating the teaching profession in the public


sector, and signals a broader professional vision that shapes expectations across the wider ECEC


workforce. For the first time, teachers are placed at the top of the civil service salary scale, with ECEC


teachers explicitly prioritized for higher pay and allowances. Yet stronger financial recognition alone


**|** 42


cannot ensure professional growth or retention. Promotion systems continue to rely heavily on seniority


and credentials, offering limited incentives to demonstrate classroom competencies or take on


mentoring and leadership roles—especially in fixed-term positions . [73]


**Salary reform has yet to change perceptions of reward.** ECEC teachers have historically earned about


6–10 percent less than primary school peers, and real wages have stagnated since 2018 (Figure 23). [74]


The 2024 base salary reform raised base pay but did not close the gap, and salary provisions under the


_Law on Educators_ have not yet been felt by local educators. [ 75] Salary remains the top investment priority


in the TLEC survey and a close second in the LEY study (Table B3). It is also the largest source of


dissatisfaction, with 43 percent of teachers reporting they are unsatisfied with pay.

**Contract and non-public teachers face constrained career progression and weak recognition.**


Although dissatisfaction with career-path clarity among contract teachers is similar to that of


permanent staff (Figure 25), interviews across provinces reveal a persistent pattern of perceived


stagnation and exclusion from advancement opportunities. In Thái Nguyên, teachers described feeling


‘outside the system’—carrying equivalent teaching loads but without equal prospects for promotion or


allowances. Non-public teachers report similar frustrations, with 22 percent dissatisfied with career

path clarity (compared with 14 percent in public preschools) and 13 percent with overall job satisfaction


(versus 8 percent; Figure 25).

**Teachers disproportionately invest in formal qualifications as a pathway for career progression.** LEY


survey data show that nearly 48 percent of ECEC teachers are enrolled in degree programs—


**|** 44


considerably higher than the participation rate in coaching (26 percent) or peer-learning visits (37


percent). This pattern is observed across employment categories and contrasts with lower engagement


in practice-based professional learning activities. This preference is understandable given concerns


about job security in a more competitive hiring environment, with school leaders reporting a growing


preference for university-educated ECEC teachers—an expectation that also shapes demand for teacher

training pathways. While this underscores a current prioritization of formal qualifications, it can be seen


as a foundational phase. As the qualification baseline rises, it paves the way for the broader ecosystem—


including teacher-training colleges—to focus resources and teacher energy on the practice-based


professional learning that will ultimately enhance classroom quality.


**An input-based promotion system limits recognition of teaching competence.** Viet Nam’s promotion


framework continues to favor managerial and administrative inputs—such as seniority, appraisal ratings,


and emulation titles—over demonstrated teaching skills and children’s assessed growth . [77] The revised


PST mark progress by defining competence through observable indicators of pedagogical effectiveness


rather than duty-based descriptions. [78] However, actual classroom competency is only one of five equally


weighted criteria. The others—particularly qualifications and time served—are easier to document and


more consistently rewarded. [79] As a result, teachers still view degree study as the most reliable route to


advancement, while practice-based development carries little reward.


**Administrative quotas and fiscal ceilings restrict advancement opportunities.** Promotion also depends


on the availability of approved posts within provincial staffing quotas. Once these are filled, even eligible


teachers cannot advance—tying career growth to budget limits rather than merit. [80] This rigidity


discourages initiative and weakens motivation to improve practice. By contrast, systems such as those


in Australia and Singapore allow teachers who meet competency standards to progress regardless of


post availability—sustaining motivation and reinforcing instructional quality. [81]


**Fragmented recognition and advancement systems weaken motivation across teacher groups.**


Contract, non-public, and core teachers face structural barriers to recognition and progression despite


equivalent or higher responsibilities. Contract teachers fall outside civil service titles, while carrying full


teaching loads. They have few pathways to higher grades, pay scales, or benefits. In the non-public sector,


salaries and advancement depend on individual organizations under the Labor Code, with no alignment


to the national title or allowance system. [82] Core teachers, who mentor and coordinate peers, also


remain outside formal reward structures—receiving no differentiated pay or promotion credit for these


developmental functions. These differences—also noted in Project 33 findings—have created a two-tier


profession that likely undermines motivation and long-term commitment. [83]


**Persistent uncertainty around conversion from contract to permanent status illustrates how gaps in**


**implementation can weaken otherwise sound policy intent.** Although contract teachers play a vital role


**|** 45


in maintaining staffing levels, delays and inconsistencies in conversion—often driven by mismatches


between approved quotas and provincial budget wages—undermine job security and professional


commitment. Without clearer operational criteria and protected funding channels, recent reforms risk


reproducing a two-tier workforce that weakens retention and morale.


_**From Entitlements to Professional Growth**_


More broadly, these patterns highlight a central tension in Viet Nam’s teacher support system: reforms


have strengthened formal entitlements, but links between demonstrated teaching competence,


recognition, and long-term reward remain weak. Pay reform provides an essential foundation, yet its


impact on motivation and retention will depend on whether professional growth, mentoring roles, and


advancement are meaningfully connected to improved practice rather than administrative compliance.

# **DRIVER C. ENRICHING CLASSROOM PRACTICES AND CHILD-** **CENTERED PEDAGOGY**

### **Introduction: Translating Curriculum Policy into Classroom Practice**

Recruitment and support of ECEC teachers are essential foundations for advancing children’s early


learning. **But the real measure of reform lies in what unfolds inside classrooms** . This driver examines


how Viet Nam’s preschool curriculum is translated into everyday practice—how teachers interpret its


principles of guided play, inclusion of diverse children, and emotional encouragement in daily classroom


interactions. Because implementation of the national ECEC Program is mandatory for all early childhood


education institutions, the evidence presented draws on surveys, classroom observations, and


interviews spanning both public and non-public preschools.


**Viet Nam’s national ECE Program has evolved through updates in 2009 and 2016, leading to the**


**current ECE program.** These periodic revisions have sustained efforts to improve equity, inclusion, and


learning quality. [84] The ECE program advances a competency-based and child-centered shift toward


classrooms that place children’s development and agency at the center of the learning process. In this


context, ‘child-centered’ signifies a move from routine, teacher-directed activity toward guided play and


interaction that draw on children’s curiosity and motivation as the basis for learning.


**Four interconnected challenges shape how the curriculum is enacted in classrooms** :


(a) **Planning is procedural rather than adaptive.** Lesson planning remains largely template

driven, emphasizing topic- or month-based coverage rather than motivated by play-based


learning activities, responsive to children’s interests and developmental capacities.


(b) **Child assessments are procedural and rarely inform teaching practices.** Classroom


observation and assessment emphasize reporting exercises, producing reports that seldom


inform adjustments to classroom practices or supplementary aid for diverse children.


(c) **Uneven integration of play-based, emotionally supportive, and inclusive pedagogy.** While


teachers echo these priorities in principle, they often struggle to integrate them into daily


routines or adapt them to diverse learners.


(d) **School-level evaluation reinforces compliance rather than pedagogical improvement.**


Classroom observation and evaluation processes focus on documentation and procedural


checks instead of analyzing teacher–child interactions and fostering professional reflection


and dialogue with peers.


**These challenges inform how MOET and local educators can better implement the national ECE**


**Program as it continues to improve** . By deepening the focus on how children learn and interact and


**|** 49


aligning daily classroom practices with the curriculum’s pedagogical aims, this driver can lift ECEC quality.


This analysis is informed by new evidence, stemming from World Bank research inside ECEC classrooms,


that illuminates the strengths of teachers and where classroom practices at times fall short.

### **Progress Since Curriculum Reform**


Viet Nam’s national curriculum reflects more than a decade of reform to modernize early childhood


education and align it with the vision set out in the _Resolution on Fundamental and Comprehensive_


_Reform of Education_ (2013), reaffirmed under the _Education Breakthrough Resolution_ (2025) and the


_Education Development Strategy to 2030, with a Vision to 2045_ . Together, these frameworks call for


classroom content and play-based activities that advance cognitive and social competencies for young


children. [85] The policy vision has translated into concrete curriculum reform and classroom change over


successive stages of implementation. This section analyses where more work remains and how


curriculum and pedagogical implementation might bring further gains for teachers and children.


**National child assessments reveal progress in children’s early development, although gains remain**


**uneven across learning domains** . Between 2012 and 2016, the _Early Development Instrument_ (EDI)


recorded significant improvements in physical health, social competence, and emotional maturity


among five-year-olds, with most rated ‘ready’ or ‘very ready’ for primary school. [86] A smaller share—


about 8–14 percent—remained at developmental risk, mainly in language and cognitive skills (Appendix


C, Table C2).


**Findings from the** _**East Asia–Pacific Early Childhood Development Scales (EAP-ECDS 2019)**_ **show a**


**similar pattern.** Children perform above regional benchmarks in socio-emotional, motor, and health


domains while progress in language, literacy, and cognition continues to lag (Figure 27).

**Major steps to implement the national curriculum have been achieved nationwide** . New materials and

As implementation deepens, variation in quality and practice are seen among preschools and classrooms.


The following section examines the key constraints that continue to shape how curriculum reforms are


implemented in classrooms and explores the factors that limit their consistent and effective enactment.


_**Challenge 1. Planning Remains Procedural Rather than Focused on Enriching Classrooms**_


**Curriculum flexibility and teacher professional judgement are central to child-centered ECE, enabling**


**teachers to connect learning to children’s experiences and local contexts** . [91] Viet Nam’s curriculum was


designed with this principle in mind—to give preschools greater responsibility for organizing content

rich, play-based learning and to promote teacher initiative within the national curriculum framework. In


practice, however, measures intended to support teachers have sometimes limited their discretion.


MOET’s planning templates, trainer networks, and related implementation instruments (Table C2) were


intended to make curriculum design more consistent, but they have also tended to emphasize


compliance over local adaptation. Many teachers still rely on prescribed annual themes and fixed


sequences, with limited opportunity to tailor content or approaches to children’s interests or local


realities. Compliance to set topics, rather than fostering teachers’ capacity to structure learning activities,


limits mindful implementation of the national curriculum.


**Teachers’ planning practices continue to reflect this emphasis on standardization.** Most preschool


plans follow school-level templates that allocate educational activities by month or week, focusing on


coverage rather than children’s emerging interests or developmental capacities. Field interviews and


observation data confirm that plans are seldom revised once the school year begins, and fewer than one


in four teachers reported planning “with or for children.” As one teacher in Ho Chi Minh City explained,


“We use the same topics, according to the instructions.” This procedural approach narrows


opportunities for adjusting classroom activities that flow from children’s expressed interests, without


losing sight of learning objectives contained in the national curriculum.


**Instruction across the preschool day is fragmented, limiting opportunities for cumulative learning.**


Classroom observations conducted for this analysis (utilizing the TEACH ECE tool) show that only 29


percent of classrooms were rated ‘Good’ on continuity of instruction. Teachers described daily activities


**|** 51


as connected to the weekly topic but rarely to one another: “Each day has its own goals—we do not


associate yesterday with today,” explained one teacher in Ho Chi Minh City. Another from Thai Nguyen


noted, “There is a clear connection between the topic and daily learning activities, but no link between


play and experiences during the day.” Such fragmentation prevents teachers from building on children’s


prior knowledge or reinforcing emerging concepts, reducing opportunities for sustained,


developmentally responsive learning.


**Box 1: TEACH ECE**


TEACH ECE is a classroom observation tool for early childhood education developed by the World


Bank to holistically measure the quality of teacher-child interations and teaching practices. It


captures both (i) the time ECE teachers spend on learning activities and the extent to which children


are on task, and (ii) the quality of ECE teaching practices that help develop children´s cognitive and


socioemotional skills, as well as cross-cutting issues such as inclusion, language facility, and child

centered learning. Quality of Teaching Practices component is organized into 3 primary Areas,


including Classroom Culture, Guided Learning, and Socioemotional Skills. These areas comprise nine


Elements that map on to twenty eight behaviors, which also aligns with twelve criteria of Viet Nam’s


Professional Standards for ECEC Teachers (see Annex C7). The behaviors are characterized as Low,


Medium, or High, based on the evidence collected during the observation. These ratings are then


translated into a 5-point scale that quantifies teaching practices.


**Opportunities for mid-lesson adaptation are similarly limited.** Our classroom data show that only half


of the teachers made any instructional adjustment, while one in five made none at all. As one teacher


in Nghe An remarked, “Changing plans in the middle of the week? Impossible.” Teachers’ self

assessments mirror this rigidity: confidence in adapting instruction to individual needs (mean = 3.2) and


in supporting disadvantaged children (mean = 3.0) ranked among the lowest of all pedagogical skills


(Figure 28).


**These patterns point to deeper structural constraints** within the 2021 ECE Program and implementation


framework—where detailed prescription, limited guidance, and procedural oversight limit teachers’


professional discretion.


**The 2021 ECE Program’s detailed design limits flexibility in structuring learning-rich and play-based**


**classroom activities.** Integrated learning—where children explore ideas across domains through


connected activities—has been a core goal of recent reform. Although the current ECE program


represents clear progress toward this approach, its design remains quite rigid. By defining content and


outcomes for each age group in predictive rather than demonstrable terms (‘capable of …’ rather than


‘can do’), flexibility is constrained in practice. This structure supports national consistency but restricts


**|** 52


local adaptation. The accompanying Implementation Assessment Toolkit was intended to foster


flexibility, yet its broad criteria offer little practical guidance—few rubrics, exemplars, or prompts for


interpretation. [92] As a result, many schools use it primarily as a compliance checklist rather than as a tool


for building learning activities for children.

**‘Professional discretion’ provisions have advanced faster than management capacity.** The Preschool


Charter grants teachers and principals authority to plan and organize learning, yet day-to-day


supervision still focuses largely on checking plans and paperwork. [93] Few principals have been prepared


to lead pedagogical improvement, and some provincial and district offices lack the staff or expertise to


provide curriculum support through technical networks or demonstration models. In the absence of


practical frameworks and sustained mentoring, preschools tend to rely on generic templates and await


direction from above. Providing this kind of coaching becomes more challenging with the closing of local


education bureaus.


**The result is a cycle of procedural conformity** : detailed design requirements, limited practical guidance,


and uneven instructional support leave little room for professional discretion. Teachers rely on fixed


themes and routines not because they resist innovation, but because the system provides little


scaffolding for creative, responsive planning. Strengthening local capacity and pedagogical leadership


will be essential to realize the play-based and inclusive vision of the current Program.


_**Challenge 2. Child assessment practices remain procedural and rarely inform teaching**_


**Assessing children’s growth is central to responsive teaching.** When teachers systematically observe


children and adjust learning experiences, classrooms become more inclusive, engaging, and


developmentally appropriate. [94] Viet Nam’s early childhood education assessment framework was


**|** 53


designed with this intent: teachers are expected to record progress, reflect on developmental patterns,


and use these insights to adjust learning activities. [95]


**In practice, however, child assessment often becomes a narrow reporting requirement** . Records are


produced and filed but seldom analyzed or discussed among teachers to improve classroom practice. As


a result, assessment has limited influence on daily practice and opportunities to adjust learning activities


to children’s varying strengths, needs, and interests are often missed.


**Although teachers collect observational data, these efforts seldom inform lesson planning or daily**


**learning activities.** Field interviews confirm that most documentation is completed to satisfy


administrative requirements or sent to district offices without discussion. As one teacher explained,


“These reports are sent to the Department, not for our plan.” Another noted, “We never meet to discuss


what the checklist shows.” Assessment thus remains largely a compliance task rather than a mechanism


for guiding or improving teaching practice.


**Observation practices also emphasize task completion over the learning process** . Teachers often record


whether a child finishes an activity rather than how they approach it, what they are learning, or what


they fail to grasp. Analysis of classroom observations confirms this tendency: 55 percent of teachers did


not acknowledge children’s perseverance, 31 percent offered no feedback, and more than half provided


only basic comments to children. As a result of this limited interaction with children, the opportunity to


motivate children, scaffold thinking, and advance complex language is lost.


**Preschool leaders and local education offices play a limited role in helping teachers use assessment**


**data to inform instruction.** Principals are expected to guide staff in interpreting child observations and


linking them to teaching strategies. But in practice this support is minimal. Interviews show that most


school meetings focus on verifying that assessment forms are completed rather than analyzing what


they reveal about children’s learning. As one principal explained, “We check the reports for accuracy,


not to discuss what they show.”


**Teachers similarly reported receiving little guidance on how to translate assessment results for**


**classroom daily planning** . Leadership attention therefore remains procedural—focused on compliance


and reporting rather than developmental improvement—limiting the integration of assessment into


teaching and learning. Strengthening this feedback link between observation, reflection, and planning


would help ensure that child assessment informs how learning activities are designed and adapted to


children’s interests and progress.


**Developmental standards are widely recognized but mainly used for reporting rather than to help**


**improve teaching.** While the 2010 and 2024 child development standards (for five-year-olds) are well


known, teachers report applying them primarily “to write reports, not to teach,” as one teacher


**|** 54


explained. [96] In many preschools, criteria from multiple standards are merged into hybrid checklists that


help track compliance but remain too generic to guide instructional classroom planning or synchronize


learning activities with children’s developmental capacities.


**Pilot initiatives demonstrate how assessment can effectively support learning.** Projects such as the


SRPP and BaMi introduced observation checklists and reflection templates to help teachers connect


child observations with classroom activities. However, these approaches remain limited to pilot sites


and have not been scaled within the professional learning system.


The limited diffusion of these practices underscores the systemic constraints that prevent teachers from


using child assessment as a tool for improving pedagogy. These constraints stem from three core factors:


design weaknesses, limited leadership capacity, and insufficient professional learning support.


**System design and guidance remain too abstract.** National templates and the 2022 _Implementation_


_Toolkit_ emphasize observation-based, responsive planning but offer no rubrics, exemplars, or prompts


to help teachers interpret what they observe in classrooms (their own and classrooms led by peers). In


practice, planning formats emphasize documenting activities and curricular themes over analyzing


children’s growth in specific domains, leaving teachers dependent on fixed classroom routines even


when diverse children display differing strengths or learning gaps.


**Leadership and professional learning gaps reinforce the disconnect between child assessment and**


**instruction.** Principals are expected to guide teachers in interpreting assessment data, yet their training


under the self-selected regular in-service program focuses mainly on inspection, administration, and


reporting rather than on pedagogical improvement. Relevant modules address how to organize and


monitor assessment activities but provide little guidance on interpreting children’s growth or facilitating


teacher reflection (Table C3). As one principal explained, “I was never trained to help teachers use data


about children.” Without this expertise, school leadership cannot effectively bridge the gap between


assessment and teaching.


**Similar constraints mark teachers’ self-selected modules**, where content emphasizes the procedural


aspects of assessment rather than its instructional value. Only one module explicitly addresses child


observation and assessment, and even this elective focuses on theory and data collection rather than


practical application (Table C3). As a result, both teachers and leaders are trained in the mechanics of


child assessment but not on how simple data can improve classroom activities and attention to


individual children, again turning observations into a compliance exercise.


Together, these weaknesses explain why assessment remains disconnected from teaching. Teachers


record data but seldom analyze or act on it. Principals verify completion rather than facilitate reflection


among staff, and training introduces assessment concepts without demonstrating how to carry out


**|** 55


simple, reliable observations of children. Without practical tools and continuous learning support, Viet


Nam’s system sustains procedural routines that fail to improve classroom practices.

examples of how child-centered pedagogy can take shape in practice. Observation data from the TEACH


ECEC study show that most teachers perform well in maintaining positive classroom climates and


organizing structured activities. As shown in Figure 29, several indicators already exceed or approach


the good-practice benchmark, particularly those related to creating order, engagement, and routine.

_**Challenge 4. School-Level Evaluation Reinforces Compliance rather than Pedagogical**_


_**Improvement**_


**High-quality teaching depends on feedback that helps teachers reflect and improve** . International


evidence shows that structured observation and formative feedback are among the most effective ways


to strengthen classroom practice and children’s learning. [98] Viet Nam has established systems of


observation and teacher evaluation guided by the Professional Standards for ECEC Teachers (PST 2018


and the draft 2025 revision), which formalize self-assessment, peer feedback, and principal appraisal.


However, these mechanisms may continue to emphasize compliance, “checking the boxes,” rather than


deep conversations about how to enliven classrooms. Teachers report being frequently observed and


yet receiving limited coaching on how to refine classroom activities, build from children’s ideas and


language, and encourage them emotionally.


**Internal observation is a routine yet under-leveraged practice.** While most teachers view classroom


observation as improvement (around 90 percent), a significant share also views it as evaluation (69


percent). This dual purpose is blurred in practice: although over 80 percent of teachers discuss how to


improve, far fewer experience modelling or acquire new methods for designing learning activities (32


percent), receive follow-up (16 percent), or change practices (22 percent). The fact that over half of the


teachers surveyed (58 percent) are praised for specific teaching methods suggests that feedback focuses


more on reinforcement than on critical analysis and refinement (Table C6). These patterns reveal a


system that observes frequently but provides limited coaching or time to discuss how to enliven


classrooms with peers.


**School-level evaluations consistently produce high ratings under the PST-based appraisal system.**


Administrative data from Program 33 show that 75.5 percent of teachers were rated “Good or higher”


in 2018–2020, rising to an estimated 88.2 percent by 2025 (MOET, 2025). These ratings are derived from


the Professional Standards for Preschool Teachers (PST), which are used for self-, peer-, and principal


appraisal. However, standardized TEACH ECE observations of the same competency domains—such as


feedback and autonomy—place all domains below the good-practice benchmark of 4.0 (Figure 34).


Interviews reflect a similar pattern of uniformly positive assessments; as one district official explained,


“All teachers are evaluated as ‘Good’ or above—there’s no distinction unless something goes seriously

and compliance, producing ratings do not differentiate the quality of teacher–child interactions.

**The challenge lies less in the practice of observation and more in creating time and capacity to discuss**


**results among fellow teachers** . The tools and norms surrounding teacher evaluation currently


emphasize procedural compliance over pedagogical reflection and encourage teachers to experiment,


surrounded by positive and discerning encouragement from principals and peers. Understanding these


underlying factors—spanning instrument design, professional norms, and a culture of supportive


feedback—clarifies why current systems have limited influence on diagnosing and improving classroom


practices.


**Evaluation design emphasizes compliance over pedagogy.** The _Professional Standards for ECEC_


_Teachers_ (2018) were designed for both self-assessment and accountability, embedding compliance


logic into classroom evaluation. Because the standards define what teachers should do but not how well


they should do it, they offer few guideposts for judging the quality of classroom practices and shaping


supportive coaching of teachers. The draft 2025 standards introduce career-stage expectations but


retain the same procedural structure and do not yet include nationally endorsed rubrics or methods of


supportive feedback. Without these operational instruments, evaluations are likely to continue


emphasizing procedural assurance rather than the pedagogical depth intended by the national


curriculum.


**Inconsistent interpretations of practice reflect the absence of a common interpretive framework.** Even


where indicators exist, there is no shared set of reference points clarifying how classroom practice


should be understood. Without such interpretive guidance or moderation processes, schools rely on


individual judgment, and assessments naturally vary across settings. This can shift attention toward


visible order and routines rather than the kinds of teacher–child interactions emphasised in the national


curriculum.

**P** rincipals and peers often interpret critique as personal disapproval rather than a normal part of


professional learning. As a result, discussions of classroom observation often remain polite and affirming


rather than reflective or problem-solving in nature.

diagnose and improve in practice. Observation, assessment, and appraisal systems are active and widely

# **SPOTLIGHT: EXPANDING QUALITY NON-PUBLIC EARLY CHILDHOOD** **EDUCATION AND CARE PROVISION**

This section examines non - public ECEC provision from a system - diversification perspective, under the


overarching objective of improving quality for all children. In practice, many non-public services rely


primarily on household payments, creating affordability constraints, and reinforcing the importance of


regulatory oversight and quality assurance for expansion. Here the starting point is that quality in


non - public settings depends on three interlinked layers that must be developed together:


(i) An enabling regulatory policy and operating environment – including regulations, financing,


land use, workforce and oversight – that allows responsible non       - public providers to enter,


formalize and operate sustainably, especially in urban areas and industrial zones;


(ii) A mix of viable non      - public models that can expand coverage for disadvantaged groups


without eroding care and pedagogical standards; and


(iii) Robust mechanisms to monitor and learn from implementation, with particular attention to


child safety, learning environments and equitable enrolment, so that effective models can


be adapted and scaled while those that do not meet minimum standards are improved or


phased out.


Throughout, the analysis is guided by the principle that access must be access to quality: expanding


non - public provision that is unsafe or of low educational value would risk using scarce resources on


services that do not benefit children and, in some cases, may expose them to harm, undermining Viet


Nam’s broader ECEC goals.

### **Access and Coverage Disparities (0–3 versus 3–5 age groups)**


**Coverage for children under 3 remains low**, **especially in high-growth urban and industrial areas.** In


the 2022–2023 school year, only about 776,000 children ages 6–36 months were enrolled in any form


of early childhood education nationwide–roughly 28-30 percent in that age group. This falls short of the


government’s target of 35 percent by 2025, indicating significant unmet demand.


**These national figures also mask** **regional and population disparities, with the** **children of migrant**


**laborers in industrial zones often having the poorest access** . Urban municipalities and Red River Delta


provinces have the highest enrollment rates, while poorer and remote regions (such as Mekong Delta)


lag. In 300+ industrial parks nationwide, an estimated 1.2 million children of workers need care, yet


public provision in such zones is very limited. [100] For example, Ho Chi Minh City’s industrial parks and


export-processing zones serve barely 2–15 percent of workers’ young children in public preschools. [101]


**|** 65


The rest are cared for by a patchwork of private centers, home-based “independent” child groups, or


remain in rural hometowns with relatives. This gap not only undermines early childhood development


for a vulnerable cohort but also hurts workforce participation with many young mothers leaving the


labor force due to lack of childcare.

### **Regulatory and Policy Environment**


Viet Nam’s legal framework has progressively opened up space for non-public ECEC participation but


ensuring quality services provision for children from disadvantaged groups and implementation lags


remain challenges. Key policies and recent changes that shape the sector landscape are analyzed below.


**|** 67


**Box 2: Government’s oversight of non-public ECEC provision**


Government’s role in training support, supervision, and monitoring of non-state provision now
operates through a devolved two-tier local governance model. Commune authorities serve as
frontline implementers and monitors of compliance and professional development support, while
district and provincial education authorities provide technical guidance, supervision, and oversight.
Overall, government roles cluster into three main functions:


1. **Gatekeeping and licensing:** People’s Committees at the commune level are responsible for
decisions on establishment, permission to operate, suspension, merger or division, and
dissolution of non-public ECEC services, in accordance with the conditions and procedures set
out in Decree No. 125/2024/NĐ-CP and related guiding circulars. Higher-level authorities—
particularly provincial education departments—play supervisory and verification roles.
Provincial People’s Committees retain responsibility for equivalent licensing and regulatory
functions for preschools with foreign investment.


To obtain the license to operate, providers must demonstrate (and submit evidence of)
readiness, including:


  - A valid establishment decision;


  - Adequate land/facilities/equipment meeting standards;


  - Staffing (management and teachers) and evidence of employment contracts; and


  - Documentation of implementation readiness for the approved plan.


To encourage socialized investment in ECEC, the Government provides a range of support
measures, including investment incentives (tax, land-use, and credit incentives), public–private
partnership arrangements, and public investment in infrastructure facilities, as stipulated in
Decree No. 105/2020/NĐ-CP on policies for ECEC development.


2. **Technical support and workforce capacity building:** Commune-level education offices have
specific responsibility for organizing education development activities within their local context.
This includes contributing to planning and implementation, assessing local workforce needs, and
providing initial support for workforce development in non-public ECEC, in coordination with
provincial education authorities.


3. **Ongoing quality assurance and enforcement:** Commune-level education offices conduct
routine supervision, inspection, complaint handling, reporting, and enforcement actions—
including suspension or dissolution where necessary—to safeguard children’s safety and ensure
compliance with minimum quality standards. These functions are carried out under the
technical guidance and oversight of higher-level education authorities.

**National Assembly Resolution on Universalization of ECEC for Children Aged 3-5 (2025)** mandates


universal ECEC for _all_ 3–5 year-old children by 2030. Crucially, this policy extends tuition fee exemptions


and financial support to children in _non-public institution_ _**s**_ as well. Starting in the 2025–2026 school year,


preschoolers ages 3–5 years in non-public or public ECEC insitutions will be eligible for state-funded fee


waivers or subsidies. This is a significant reform to ‘level the playing field’ and leverage private capacity


for universal access.


**Targeted Industrial Zone (IZ) childcare policies are** recently approved in the Program 2270 ‘Improving


the quality of ECEC education in urban areas and industrial zones, 2025–2035, vision 2045’ [105] . This


Program’s overall objective is to ensure that children in urban areas and industrial zones (Izs) can access


fair, high-quality ECEC services. By 2035, it aims, among others, that: (i) in IZs, all children ages 6–36


months who are children of workers and have childcare needs can attend preschools or child groups and


access quality services; (ii) all ECEC institution managers, teachers and staff in these areas receive annual


professional development; (iii) infant–toddler groups in ECEC institutions in industrial areas expand by


at least 20 percent, with at least 10 percent more public preschools enrolling children under 24 months;


and (iv) all independent nonpublic centers in industrial zones meet national safety and injuryprevention


standards, while 100 percent of worker parents receive basic parenting and childcare information.


**The government has moved to address the severe ECEC teacher shortage and quality issues.** A new


Law on Teachers (2024) was passed, aiming to elevate teacher status and conditions. Previously, MOET


had also adjusted ECEC teacher qualification requirements: while the Education Law 2019 raised the


standard to a college degree for ECEC teachers, in 2022 MOET allowed localities to hire teachers who


hold only secondary teacher training, short of the new standard, as a stop-gap, with a roadmap to


upgrade their qualifications later. This move acknowledged reality – many private and community


caregivers lack a college degree, yet the system needs them.


**The government takes a firmer step in QA of non-public establishments in** the **new Program 2270** . It


requires provinces to develop a digital database for ECEC in urban areas and IZs and to guide


independent non-public centers to conduct regular self-assessment of safety standards and update


their information into this system. It also calls for intersectoral inspection mechanisms involving


education, home affairs, health, and mass organizations. However, in the context of the newly


restructured two-tier local government system, it is not yet clear how communes will be able to perform


this QA functions. Before this restructuring, each district had an ECE inspectorate, but the explosion of


small private classes strained oversight capacity. Commune People’s Committees were tasked to


monitor independent childcare groups, but they lacked the expertise.


**Investment incentives and public-private partnership (PPP) framework exist but are blocked by**


**procedural hurdles and cross-ministry conditions:** Viet Nam’s education law and Party resolutions


**|** 69


endorse ‘socialization’ of education, that is, private sector involvement. Various tax, land, and credit


incentives exist _on paper_ . For example, social investment in education enjoys a preferential corporate


income tax rate of 10 percent for the project’s life, and land leases for education projects can be


discounted 50 percent in disadvantaged areas. However, until recently, there was no widely used PPP


model in ECEC, and no major ECEC PPP pilot has been implemented. Decision 2270/QĐTTg- now


explicitly encourages applying public–private cooperation through business–management (O&M)


contracts between the state and non-public ECEC providers in urban areas and IZs, with fee levels


calibrated to workers’ incomes, and prioritizes the use of redundant government office buildings and


social housing projects as sites for preschools. This creates a more concrete legal basis for provinces to


design PPP pilots in ECEC.


**One critical barrier for expanding childcare in IZs has been the lack of land earmarked for social**


**infrastructure.** Decree 35/2022 on industrial and economic zones stipulates that up to 10 percent of an


IZ’s land can be used for services and public facilities–implicitly including childcare centers. In practice,


enforcement is uneven: many older industrial parks still lack dedicated ECEC land (as noted by local


officials). [106] Some provinces (for instance, Hải Phòng, Nghệ An) now mandate that any new IZ project


_must_ include plans for a kindergarten.


**Overall, Viet Nam’s policy environment is becoming more enabling of non-public ECEC, but practical**


**barriers remain.** Complex administrative procedures, limited financing mechanisms, and insufficient


coordination across ministries (education, finance, labor, construction, and so on) have slowed the


growth of quality private and community ECEC services. Going forward, a concerted effort is needed to


streamline these regulations and actively support non-state providers.

### **Market Realities: Costs, Affordability, and the Private Sector**


**Overall, the non-public ECEC sector is bifurcated** . A few _high-end centers_ in cities charging VNĐ 5–10+


million per month, often corporate or international preschools, versus a large number of _low-cost home-_


_based groups_ charging perhaps VNĐ 1–2 million per month per child but operating on razor-thin margins.


Without external support, it is not financially attractive for private investors to open quality childcare


centers in low-income areas. Return on investment is low unless land or capital is subsidized. This is


partially why many private kindergarten entrepreneurs target affluent neighborhoods or open as part


of K-12 private schools.


Delivering quality ECEC is resource-intensive, and current financing arrangements leave a gap between


_what it costs to provide care and what parents can afford or are willing to pay_, particularly for the under

3 age group.


**|** 70


**High unit costs for under-3 can undermine quality.** Infants and toddlers require low child-to-caregiver


ratios (often 8:1 or 10:1 in Viet Nam’s norms) and constant supervision, making their care cost per child


much higher than that for older preschoolers. Additionally, suitable facilities (with cribs, sanitary areas,


safe play space) demand upfront capital. Estimates from private operators suggest that _fees of at least_


_VND 2–3 million per child per month (US$80–120)_ are needed just to break even for decent quality infant


care in urban Viet Nam (higher for premium centers). However, household incomes in worker


communities are low–for example, a garment factory worker earns around VND 7–10 million (US$300–


400) per month. As a result, non-public providers either charge high fees, catering to middle-class


families, or, if serving low-income clients, struggle to maintain quality on shoestring budgets. Many


small independent ECEC centers rely on untrained, low-paid staff, earning near minimum wage of


around VND 4–5 million, and overcrowding to cut costs, which can undermine quality.


Public subsidies focus on preschoolers ages 3-5 years and nursery services for under 3 years receive


very limited public funding. Consequently, the private sector now supplies a large share of 0–3 care. The


new Resolution 217/2025/QH15 opens opportunities for preschool-aged children attending non-public


ECEC centers but results have not been realized because it depends on local government policies and


budgets.


**Faced with cost barriers, parents often turn to informal, even unlicensed, options.** In IZs, it is common


for entrepreneurial local women (sometimes retired teachers or even untrained caregivers) to run


unlicensed child-minding services out of their homes for a dozen children, charging lower fees than


formal centers. These micro-enterprises avoid regulatory costs (no license, no tax, minimal facility


investment–perhaps just a room in a boarding house). While they increase access, they operate _outside_


_the quality assurance system_, and some have been linked to child safety incidents reported in the media.


The prevalence of such services indicates a market failure: the formal sector is not providing enough


affordable supply, so informal substitutes arise despite risks.


**Private provision has grown mainly where it’s most profitable (big cities), and not enough in the**


**locales of highest need (factory towns, remote districts) without additional support** . There are capable


private and non-profit operators interested in expanding ECEC in Viet Nam (including some chains and


social enterprises). IFC’s research finds that employers and investors see opportunities in childcare


given the demand. [107] [108] However, the key constraints they face include: _access to finance_ (banks


perceive daycare startups as risky–lacking collateral and stable cash flow), _land and space_ (especially in


dense cities or built-out industrial parks–securing a site can be prohibitively expensive), and _regulatory_


_uncertainty_ (changing policies on licensing, uncertainty about whether fee controls might be imposed,


and so on).


**|** 71


In summary, the landscape of non-public ECEC provision in Viet Nam is challenging. Quality service is


costly, but ability-to-pay is low for communities with the greatest unmet need. This calls for public


financing to bridge the gap via subsidies or cost-sharing and leveraging economies of scale, for example,


employer-supported centers or larger PPP projects, to lower unit costs. It also highlights why simply


deregulating and “letting the market work” won’t suffice. **Targeted public investment and risk**


**mitigation** **are needed** **to spur private and community providers to enter the areas where they are**


**needed most.**

# **DRIVER D. MECHANISMS TO ADVANCE QUALITY ASSURANCE**

### **Introduction: Strengthening Quality Assurance in Early Childhood Education**

Quality assurance (QA) is an essential driver of improvement in early childhood education. While


strengthening the ECEC workforce and enriching children’s day-to-day learning experiences are


indispensable, these efforts can only achieve consistent impact when supported by a well-functioning


QA system. QA provides the structures that ensure children receive safe, inclusive, and developmentally


appropriate learning experiences—regardless of location or provider type.


Viet Nam has already established many of the core components of an effective QA system: learning goals


for children, professional standards for teachers, mechanisms for inspection and accreditation, and a


national information system that tracks basic structural indicators. These QA mechanisms apply system

wide to licensed early childhood education institutions, regardless of location or provider type, although


implementation capacity and oversight intensity vary across settings. Taken together, these elements


form **a broader system that must function coherently** . A strong QA architecture depends on the


alignment of five interconnected pillars:


  - **Governance and coordination** that ensure responsibilities are coherent and consistently


implemented across levels of government.

  - **Standards and guidelines** that clearly define quality and make core aspects of teaching and


learning observable.

  - **Monitoring, inspection, and accreditation processes** that assess performance reliably and


support improvement.

  - **Data systems and feedback loops** that provide timely, actionable information for decision

making.


  - **Accountability and public reporting** that promote transparency and reinforce continuous


learning within the system.


When these pillars work together, QA becomes a mechanism that links expectations, evidence, and


support. It enables the system to identify where children are learning well, where practice needs


strengthening, and which schools require targeted assistance. When the pillars are not aligned—or


when any of them is weak—QA activities become fragmented, compliance-oriented, and disconnected


from classroom practice.


While Viet Nam has established many of the core elements of a quality assurance (QA) system for early


childhood education, these elements do not yet function together in ways that consistently support


**|** 74


instructional improvement or equitable quality in practice. In Viet Nam, QA is shaped by five interlinked


system-level constraints:


  - Governance and coordination gaps across levels of government, including the allocation,


capacity, and oversight of licensing functions at the local level that affect consistency in


implementation.

  - Quality standards and guidelines remain vague and compliance-oriented, making process


quality difficult to observe;

  - Monitoring and inspection practices that prioritize administrative compliance over continuous


improvement;

  - Data systems and feedback loops oriented toward reporting rather than instructional support;


and

  - Accountability and public reporting mechanisms that emphasize structural indicators more than


children’s learning experiences.


Understanding these challenges—and the system conditions that sustain them—is essential for


strengthening QA as a driver of quality and equity. The following section analyzes each challenge in


detail, explaining how current arrangements shape practice and what prevents the QA system from


operating as an integrated, improvement-oriented whole.

### **Key Quality Assurance Challenges**


_**Challenge 1. Governance and Coordination—Responsibilities are clear in law but**_


_**inconsistently implemented**_


Viet Nam’s legal framework clearly defines who is responsible for QA in early childhood education. MOET


sets national policy, standards, and QA regulations and oversees preschools through provincial


Departments of Education and Training (DOETs). [109] Other state bodies—including the National Assembly


and Government, the Ministry of Finance (MOF), and the Ministry of Home Affairs (MOHA)—establish


foundational legislation and determine staffing norms and budget allocations that underpin QA. [110] At


the local level, DOETs organize external evaluations and inspections, [111] and commune authorities carry


out routine monitoring and review self-assessments as part of the 2025 decentralization reform. [112]


Within this structure, preschools prepare quality plans, conduct self-assessments, and report data


upward through commune and provincial levels using national QA guidance. [113] The challenges in


governance therefore relate not to the clarity of mandates but to the system conditions that affect how


consistently these responsibilities are carried out.


**Commune authorities lack the capacity to carry out newly assigned QA responsibilities.** Field


interviews indicate that _“in many communes, no specialized education officer or dedicated quality-_


**|** 75


_assurance budget line exists,”_ leaving key tasks—such as licensing, routine monitoring, and reviewing


school self-assessments—only partially implemented. [114] This creates wide variation in oversight, with


some preschools receiving structured support while others rely on minimal administrative checks.


**The removal of district-level roles has disrupted continuity and left communes without sufficient**


**operational support.** Stakeholders reported _“uncertainty about new procedures and a loss of support_


_previously provided by district offices,”_ affecting core QA processes during the transition to the two-tier


system. This gap is visible in school-level cycles, where Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) processes are often


_“interrupted, not maintained continuously… due to changes in principals or local disruptions such as_


_floods,”_ weakening routine quality monitoring.


**Persistent staffing shortages constrain QA implementation across provinces and communes.** Officials


described _“acute human-resource constraints,”_ noting that limited personnel restrict their ability to


observe practice, analyze reports, or follow up on agreed improvement actions. Without adequate


staffing, some preschools _“repeat content”_ in self-assessments because they receive no diagnostic


feedback to guide revisions.


**Coordination across education, health, and social sectors remains inconsistent, limiting a holistic view**


**of children’s wellbeing in QA processes.** Consultation participants observed that _“coordination between_


_departments is not synchronized”_ and that units _“have not coordinated regularly in providing data and_


_tracking improvement actions.”_ As a result, important dimensions of child development—such as


nutrition, health checks, and family support—are not routinely integrated into QA reviews, despite their


relevance to early learning.


Together, these indicators point to deeper system-level constraints. Although roles and responsibilities


are clearly defined in law, the mechanisms and resources needed to fulfill them remain uneven. Two


structural factors help explain why these governance and coordination challenges persist.


**Fragmented ministerial responsibilities create a structural misalignment between standards, staffing,**


**and resources.** MOET defines QA requirements, but the MOHA sets staffing norms and the MOF


determines budget ceilings, while health and social agencies operate under separate mandates. This


division of authority makes it difficult to align quality expectations with the personnel and financing


required to implement them consistently across localities.


**The 2025 decentralization reform expanded commune responsibilities without building**


**commensurate local capacity.** Commune authorities now hold significant responsibility for licensing,


monitoring, and sanctioning preschools, but the reform has not yet been accompanied by equivalent


investments in staffing, training, or digital tools. Without transitional support, communes tend to rely


**|** 76


on administrative procedures rather than developmental oversight, limiting their ability to assess


practice or guide improvement.


_**Challenge 2. Standards and Guidelines—Quality definitions remain vague and compliance-**_


_**oriented**_


A strong QA system depends on standards that are clear, observable, and anchored in children’s


development. In Viet Nam, the national frameworks provide structure but do not yet translate


pedagogical expectations into measurable indicators of process quality. As a result, quality is defined


and assessed largely through documentation and planned activities rather than children’s day-to-day


learning experiences.


**Existing standards and tools make process quality difficult to observe.** The ECE Program and


accreditation standards define broad domains for review, but neither is operationalized through


behavioral indicators that capture teacher-child interactions or classroom climate. The ECE Program


Implementation Assessment Toolkit and Curriculum Evaluation Handbook [115] standardize templates for


plans, checklists, and observation forms, yet these concentrate on whether required activities occur


rather than the quality of practice..


**Evidence highlights a persistent emphasis on documentation over lived experience.** More than three

quarters of the Toolkit’s 61 criteria review plans, schedules, or records. Even within the ‘teacher


competency’ domain, only two of nine items relate to child engagement or teacher–child interactions,


contributing just 20 percent of the score. This leaves relational and instructional dimensions of quality


largely invisible and prevents evaluators from judging whether activities effectively support children’s


development.


**Criteria remain descriptive rather than diagnostic, limiting consistent interpretation.** Terms such as


‘child-friendly environment’ or ‘learning through play’ describe general expectations but lack observable


anchors that distinguish strong from weak practice. Evaluators report that these criteria are _“difficult to_


_measure objectively”_ without behavioral anchors. Observation forms are narrative rather than


calibrated, provincial variations are minor, and few provinces add indicators related to pedagogy,


inclusion, or children’s well-being, reinforcing reliance on easily verified records.


**Overall, process quality is weakly represented across QA definitions and tools.** National curriculum


priorities for child development and supportive learning environments have not been embedded in


instruments used for accreditation, monitoring, or self-assessment. A review of Viet Nam’s QA


frameworks against international ECEC principles (see Appendix D) shows strong alignment on structural


and developmental expectations but much weaker integration of process-quality indicators and


**|** 77


mechanisms for continuous evaluation. This leaves insufficient basis for assessing whether classroom


practice aligns with curriculum intent.


These patterns point to deeper design features of the QA system that shape how standards and


instruments operate.


**Existing standards and tools were developed for assurance rather than diagnosis.** The Implementation


Toolkit and Curriculum Evaluation Handbook were designed to promote uniformity and minimum

standard compliance, not to distinguish between stronger and weaker practice. Because the instruments


do not include rubrics, exemplars, or moderation processes, evaluators lack shared reference points and


rely on individual judgment. **Indicators have not yet been empirically validated or consistently applied.**


Most current indicators have not been tested for their association with children’s learning or well-being,


limiting their usefulness for prioritizing what matters most. Application also varies across provinces,


reflecting differences in observation skills, templates, and opportunities for coordination with TTIs.


These conditions constrain the system’s ability to generate diagnostic information for support or


curriculum refinement.


Other countries track large samples of young children to determine progress in early learning and


identify those inputs and classroom practices that empirically advance children’s development.


Together, these constraints leave Viet Nam with quality standards that define procedural expectations


but provide a limited basis for observing or judging the quality of children’s day-to-day experiences.


When standards lack clear, observable indicators, monitoring systems naturally default to what can be


easily verified on paper.


_**Challenge**_ _**3: Monitoring and**_ _**Inspection–Processes remain compliance-oriented and do not**_


_**yet drive continuous improvement**_


Monitoring and inspection are central components of Viet Nam’s ECEC QA cycle, combining annual


school self-assessment with external review and accreditation. National guidance—including the


Preschool Accreditation Standards, [116] self-evaluation templates set out under the Preschool Charter, [117]


and the Implementation Assessment Toolkit for the ECE Program—provides a clear procedural


framework that is well understood by schools and subnational authorities.


**Yet monitoring still operates mainly as a compliance mechanism and only weakly supports continuous**


**improvement.** Participation in accreditation remains uneven, especially in rural and remote areas where


some facility-related criteria are unsuitable for kindergartens in these regions or for small-scale private


schools. Although internal QA activities are mandated at regularly interval, they often become intensive


only in the months leading up to an external visit. Fewer than half of all preschools in Viet Nam have


achieved accredited status, underscoring the system's limited reach. Principals and district officers noted


**|** 78


that while annual reviews are prepared, sustained reflection and follow-up are inconsistent outside


these ‘pre-accreditation periods.’ Schools 'prepare intensively in the two–three months before


accreditation, with some principals reporting that _“the PDCA cycle was interrupted, not maintained_


_continuously”_ outside of these periods.


**Monitoring practices focus on verifying documentation rather than observing lived practice.** Routine


visits by provincial and district officers primarily review lesson plans, schedules, and required records.


Stakeholders reported that observations tend to confirm whether planned activities occurred, rather


than examining the quality of teacher–child interactions or children’s engagement. As one evaluator


explained, _“We mainly consider data and records, not reality,”_ reflecting the absence of calibrated


observation tools that could anchor monitoring in real practice rather than paperwork.


**External assessments tend to verify dossiers rather than examine daily practice.** Although external


teams conduct on-site verification, stakeholders described a pattern in which evaluators confirm


reported evidence but provide limited analysis of classroom processes. During fieldwork in Hanoi, Thai


Nguyen, and Ho Chi Minh City, principals and local officials noted that accreditation often feels ‘formal’


and documentation-heavy, and that internal QA activities often intensify only in the months before an


external visit rather than being maintained throughout the year. Without diagnostic rubrics or calibrated


criteria, external assessments offer limited insight into interactional or developmental quality.


Taken together, these indicators reveal a monitoring system that validates procedural compliance but


offers limited information about whether teaching quality is improving or whether curriculum priorities


are being put into daily practice. Understanding why these patterns persist requires examining the


design of monitoring tools and the incentives that shape evaluator behavior.


**Monitoring tools were developed for assurance rather than diagnosis.** The Implementation


Assessment Toolkit and Handbook for Curriculum Evaluation standardize templates for plan review and


record-keeping. These instruments ensure uniformity and minimum-standard compliance, but they do


not articulate observable indicators of interactional quality or child engagement. In the absence of


diagnostic rubrics, evaluators naturally rely on documentation because it is the only clear, verifiable


evidence available.


**System-level targets create upward pressure on ratings and reinforce compliance.** National policy on


teacher training and development sets an expectation that 85 percent of ECEC teachers meet the


Professional Standards at ‘Good or higher.’ [118] While intended to promote high performance, such


universal competency expectations are known internationally to produce compressed appraisal


distributions, as evaluators feel obliged to assign scores within the expected band even when practice


varies. [119] Interviews with Viet Namese principals and district officers echoed this dynamic, noting that it


is “difficult to rate teachers below ‘Good’” because appraisal results contribute to system-level


**|** 79


performance reporting. This reinforces reliance on documentary evidence and limits the diagnostic


value of monitoring.


**Evaluator capacity and interpretive guidance remain limited.** Provincial and district officers have had


differing opportunities to build skills in observing instruction or interpreting classroom evidence.


Observation templates vary across provinces, analytical guidance is sparse, and communication with


TTIs is limited. Without shared reference points or moderation processes, monitoring practices gravitate


toward visible routines and documentation rather than interactional quality. This is compounded by the


fact that many evaluators _“lack experience and have uneven expertise”_ in pedagogical observation.


**Inspection authority has expanded, but practice remains compliance-oriented.** The preschool


establishment and operational provisions under the 2025 decentralization reform strengthen DOET


authority to guide, inspect, and sanction preschools. [120] These provisions may enhance consistency and


public confidence, but their effect will depend on whether they are implemented as formative tools


rather than procedural compliance checks. Stakeholders noted that inspection still focuses primarily on


facilities, safety, and administrative completeness. Several provincial leaders also observed that


accreditation standards have become increasingly ambitious while resources have not kept pace, making


it difficult for many schools—particularly in rural and mountainous areas—to meet requirements. This


imbalance reinforces a compliance mindset: schools focus on demonstrating minimum procedural


conformity rather than using inspection results to strengthen teaching and learning.


_**Challenge 4. Data Systems and Feedback Loops**_ - _**Designed for reporting rather than**_


_**diagnosis and improvement**_


High-quality ECEC systems rely not only on clear standards and strong teaching, but also on data systems


that help schools and authorities understand where quality is improving, where it is stagnating, and why.


In Viet Nam, national data systems—particularly EMIS—collect extensive administrative information, yet


reveal little about classroom processes or how children experience the ECE curriculum. Schools, district


divisions, and DOETs regularly submit data upward, but the information rarely returns to teachers or


school leaders in a form that supports analysis, reflection, or targeted action. As a result, Viet Nam’s


data architecture is well suited to reporting but less aligned with the instructional and QA demands of


the ECEC sector.


**Data flows one way—upward for reporting—and rarely returns as usable insight.** Schools consistently


noted that they submit large volumes of information but _“rarely receive analysis back.”_ Without a


mechanism for provincial or district authorities to _“return feedback data,”_ schools have limited visibility


into their performance or how it compares with peers. Many schools often submit similar or unchanged


information in successive self-assessments because criteria are interpreted inconsistently and analytical


feedback rarely returns to the school level.


**|** 80


**EMIS is structurally limited in capturing classroom quality and functionally rigid.** EMIS collects key


administrative inputs—facilities, staffing ratios, and enrollment figures—but lacks indicators that reflect


children’s experiences or teachers’ practices. Core dimensions of process quality—teacher-child


interactions, pedagogical approaches, curricular enactment, and classroom environment—are not


represented in the national system. EMIS design features also limit its use at the school level: once data


are submitted, _“EMIS is locked after import,”_ preventing updates or further analysis. These features


make the system effective for compliance monitoring but less suited to supporting continuous quality


improvement.


**Internal improvement cycles are inconsistently sustained.** National guidance under the Preschool


Charter requires all licensed preschools to conduct regular self-assessment and maintain continuous


Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycles. In practice, however, many preschools report that these cycles are


_“interrupted, not maintained continuously”_ due to weak instructional leadership, local disruptions, or


low prioritization in some non-public institutions. As a result, schools face challenges in conducting root

cause analysis or tracking progress over time. Without a reliable internal cycle, external QA has limited


evidence to build on, and schools cannot generate consistent information to guide improvement.


**Analytical capacity for data use varies across schools and subnational levels.** Schools—particularly


non-public ones—reported that they lack sufficient tools, time, or training to interpret data or translate


raw figures into actionable insights. District and provincial officials similarly noted that analytical work


is often ad hoc, in part because existing data systems are not designed to support routine analysis. These


constraints make it challenging to shift from compliance reporting toward more learning-oriented data


use.


**Parent and community feedback is inconsistently collected and rarely used.** Stakeholders explained


that parent surveys are _“not implemented methodically,”_ often lack statistical rigor, and are seldom


incorporated into improvement planning. As a result, bottom-up perspectives on children’s experiences


and service quality do not consistently inform school-level or system-level decision-making.


The data system's limited impact on improving classroom quality stems from three interconnected,


systemic constraints.


**First, the system’s foundational design prioritizes administrative compliance over instructional**


**improvement.** The architecture of Viet Nam’s data systems—including EMIS and associated reporting


templates—was created to monitor administrative inputs such as coverage, infrastructure, and staffing.


It was not designed to diagnose pedagogical quality or track curriculum implementation, creating a


structural misalignment with the goal of improving classroom practice. This design reinforces a one-way,


upward flow of information: schools submit data but rarely receive analytical feedback, as no formal


requirements obligate provincial or district authorities to return insights to guide school-level action.


**|** 81


**Second, fragmented data architecture prevents the formation of a coherent, system-wide view of**


**quality.** The potential for evidence-based insight is constrained by a fragmented data ecosystem.


Provincial databases vary in scope and often lack interoperability with the national EMIS, while different


digital tools and inconsistent data standards across localities complicate comparison and synthesis. This


fragmentation limits real-time visibility into quality across regions, undermining the system’s ability to


identify patterns, monitor curriculum implementation, or direct support where it is most needed.


**Third, limited analytical capacity and weak data-use culture restrict meaningful interpretation and**


**application.** Even where data exists, schools and subnational authorities lack the tools, training, and


time to analyze it effectively. School leaders and officials frequently report that they “collect a lot but


analyze little,” resulting in descriptive rather than diagnostic use of data. This is compounded by weak


bottom-up and horizontal communication, which isolates schools and limits opportunities to learn from


peers or integrate community perspectives into planning and quality improvement.


**Recent reforms provide a stronger foundation for improving ECEC data systems.** The accreditation


standards have supported more consistent reporting practices, and several provinces have piloted digital


tools to improve timeliness and inclusion of non-public ECEC institutions. The 2025 reform on


decentralization clarifies documentation and reporting responsibilities across levels of government,


while the new Party resolution on breakthroughs in education and training and the 2025 decree on


MOET’s organization give the ministry a clearer mandate to lead digital transformation, develop


integrated education databases, and strengthen data interoperability. [121] The extent to which these


reforms will address current gaps will depend on implementation, including whether they enable real

time data exchange, more systematic analysis, and meaningful feedback to schools.


Together, these factors mean that Viet Nam’s ECEC data systems are effective for monitoring coverage


and compliance but not yet equipped to support continuous quality improvement. Strengthening the


alignment between data collection, analysis, and feedback—while ensuring that information flows in


both directions—will be essential for understanding how the ECE program is being implemented and for


guiding targeted support to teachers and schools.


_**Challenge 5: Accountability and Public Reporting—Focused on structural inputs rather than**_


_**children’s learning experiences**_


Local accountability and public reporting are essential components of a well-functioning QA system. Viet


Nam has recently strengthened transparency requirements through the public disclosure regulations,


which require preschools to publish annual education plans, structural information, and aggregated


achievements in children’s care and participation, along with internal and external QA results. [122] These


measures are intended to support parental engagement and enable communities and local authorities


to better understand preschool operations. In practice, however, reporting is inconsistent, unevenly


**|** 82


implemented, and still oriented toward structural inputs rather than children’s learning or classroom


experiences.


**Reporting is inconsistent and not standardized across provinces.** Although preschools disclose selected


information, most provinces do not require or support a unified annual quality report that consolidates


internal and external QA findings. As a result, information made public varies significantly in depth,


format, and frequency, limiting comparability and weakening local accountability.


**Public information focuses on inputs rather than teaching and learning.** Schools typically publish


information on staffing, facilities, enrollment, nutrition, and operational arrangements. However,


information on process quality—such as interaction quality, classroom climate, inclusive practices, or


how well the curriculum is being implemented—is largely absent. Parents therefore receive little insight


into what children experience day to day or how well pedagogical practices support development.


**Parent- and community-facing information is limited and uneven.** While some preschools present


quality information during annual meetings, most do not publish parent-friendly summaries of QA


findings or improvement plans. Parents often rely on informal channels, such as social media groups or


CCTV feeds, rather than formal reporting mechanisms. Reports from external QA are only selectively


disclosed, especially among non-public providers.


**Public reporting requirements do not systematically include feedback from families.** Although


regulations emphasize cooperation with families, parent surveys are not consistently implemented,


analyzed, or published. In many preschools, feedback is collected informally and does not feed into


planning or improvement cycles. As a result, community perspectives rarely influence school-level or


system-level decisions.


Taken together, these indicators show that although preschools disclose required information, the


system does not yet provide families or communities with meaningful insights into children’s learning


experiences or the quality of teaching. The reasons for this lie in how reporting requirements are


designed, interpreted, and supported across levels of government.


**The design of reporting mandates prioritizes administrative compliance over a holistic view of quality.**


Current disclosure requirements emphasize readily documented structural inputs—such as facilities,


staffing, and enrollment—but fail to capture essential aspects of children’s daily experiences, including


supportive interactions, inclusive practices, and the extent to which the curriculum is enacted in


classrooms. As a result, the information available to families provides little visibility into process quality.


**A lack of standardized, accessible formats and local capacity prevents clear communication with**


**families.** The absence of a unified national model for annual quality reports results in reporting that is


inconsistent across provinces, uneven in depth, and often difficult for parents to interpret. Limited skills,


**|** 83


tools, and time at school and local administration levels further constrain the ability to synthesize QA


results into clear, parent-facing summaries.


**The regulatory framework fosters one-way communication rather than genuine accountability**


**partnerships.** Regulations frame parent engagement primarily as the dissemination of information


rather than a reciprocal process. There are no structured feedback mechanisms, no formal inclusion of


parent perspectives in quality reviews, and no requirements to publish parent satisfaction data. This


leaves families in a largely passive role and limits opportunities for parents to influence school-level


improvement efforts or system-level decision-making.


Overall, Viet Nam’s accountability and public reporting arrangements provide transparency on structural


quality but do not yet offer families and communities meaningful insight into children’s daily learning


experiences. Strengthening the relevance, consistency, and accessibility of public reporting—


particularly around process quality and parent feedback—will be essential to ensure that accountability


mechanisms contribute to improved teaching and learning in preschools.


_**Toward a Coherent System for Quality**_


The analysis of Driver D shows that Viet Nam’s QA mechanisms, while well established in principle, do


not yet function as a coherent system. The five core pillars of QA—governance and coordination,


standards and guidelines, monitoring and inspection, data systems, and public accountability—operate


largely in parallel rather than as mutually reinforcing components. This fragmentation limits visibility of


process quality, weakens feedback loops, and reinforces compliance over improvement.


Figure 39 synthesizes these challenges by tracing how weaknesses across the five QA pillars interact to


block system learning—and the strategic shifts required to realign them. The figure provides a bridge


from diagnosis to action, highlighting that improving QA is not a matter of strengthening individual tools,


but of reconfiguring how standards, monitoring, data, and accountability work together to support


classroom practice and children’s learning.

# **KEY RECOMMENDATIONS: IMPROVING THE QUALITY AND BENEFITS** **OF EARLY CHILDHOOD EDUCATION AND CARE**

Viet Nam’s efforts to build a highly educated and versatile workforce begins in ECEC. The nation has


created a high-quality education sector over the past half-century, including the steady expansion of


ECEC programs. Viet Nam’s students have begun to outperform peers even in wealthier societies.


This report has detailed how the quality of ECEC remains uneven and unfairly distributed among the


provinces. Many teachers labor in difficult conditions–large classes, insufficient teaching materials, and


little guidance on how to improve their pedagogical practice. ECEC in Viet Nam yields significant gains in


children’s early learning, yet at modest levels of magnitude, as reviewed in the Overview section.


This analysis highlights four drivers of ECEC quality, that is, pathways of policy reform and local practice


that can enrich the benefits that children and families experience from the ECEC sector. The report has


detailed the challenges that must be confronted within each of the drivers of ECEC quality:


  - _Driver A. Producing additional high-quality ECEC teachers_ through a cohesive network of teacher


training colleges and institutes.

  - _Driver B. Improving the working conditions of teachers_ by distributing new teachers and inputs


equitably, then supporting ongoing improvement in classroom practices.

  - _Driver C. Enriching classroom practices_, moving away from didactic delivery of content to


creating motivating child-centered learning activities and emotional support.

  - _Driver D. Strengthening quality assurance mechanisms_, led by central government and fostering


community-level reviews and pedagogical coaching inside preschools.


The recommendations that follow are organized around these four drivers and synthesized in a single


implementation framework (Figure 40), building on and scaling up promising practices and


opportunities identified throughout the report, while highlighting priorities, sequencing, and cross

driver linkages across reforms.


This report urges GoV leaders and local educators to build from their remarkable success and think about


quality in novel ways. The current ECE Program encourges teachers to become more child-centered–


offering feedback and encouragement to each child, creating motivating learning activities, becoming


less concerned with didactic delivery of topical information. Fostering motivating teaching requires


more than attention to core inputs–class size, facilities, and free meals. These ingredients are necessary


but insufficient in fostering a culture of continual improvement and care among teachers within the


nation’s diverse preschools. Also, the sector must become more sensitive to serving diverse children,


including those with disabilities.


**|** 87


### **Overarching Recommendations : High Priority Reforms and Improving Local Practice**

No government could feasibly make progress on all the reforms and local practices suggested in this


report. So, this final section arrives at the key improvements likely to yield the greatest gains in ECEC


quality and results for young children.


From the Overview section of the report, the World Bank team recommends these actions, undertaken


by GoV, MOET, and local educators:


1.1 As the ECEC sector continues to expand, the government should articulate a _coherent pla_ n for


raising the quality of ECEC. A flurry of decrees and National Assembly initiatives have addressed


differing elements of ECEC quality, including the 2025 legislation. The time is right for GoV to


devise a simple yet comprehensive agenda for lifting ECEC quality.


1.2 Growth in ECEC enrollment will likely continue in the _non-public sector_ . This includes the


expansion of preschools in industrial zones (IZ), where the private sector already plays a role in


building and running preschools. GoV’s efforts to improve quality should include the non-public


sectors, recognizing that differing forms of regulation and quality oversight may be required to


ensure innovation and diverse forms of ECEC organizations.


1.3 GoV must simultaneously consider how to _distribute inputs_ and elements of quality fairly


among provinces and _assess how local oversight_ (quality assessment) can better equip


preschool directors to move teachers toward child-centered classrooms rich in motivating


learning activities.


1.4 Until GoV _tracks children through ECEC and into primary school_, which inputs and local practices


lift students will never be known empirically. Viet Nam hosts several research centers that could


carefully track random samples of children over time.

### **Producing Additional High-Quality Teachers**


To address persistent shortages and inequitable deployment of preschool teachers, Viet Nam should


strengthen and better coordinate the production, preparation, and deployment of new teachers. The


following recommendations outline priority steps to expand the teacher pipeline while improving


quality and equity:


A.1 _Achieve a larger and adaptive teacher pipeline_ . Unite central government ministries and teacher


training institutes (TTIs) around a coherent plan for the number of new ECEC teachers to be


produced and how they will be equitably distributed to province with the most severe shortages.


This should be implemented through a time-bound coordination process using existing planning,


**|** 88


training-order, and budgeting mechanisms—rather than through the creation of a new standalone


strategy or decree. In practice, this could take the form of a time-bound inter-ministerial planning


group, led by MOET, with clear decision rights and structured consultation with TTIs and provinces,


drawing on existing data and planning cycles.


Implementation of production targets must recognize present capacities of TTIs, use real-time data


to verify local scarcities of teachers, aligning training orders, quotas, and budget support through


careful and ongoing feedback from MOET, TTIs, and provincial leaders.


Detailed evidence, modeling assumptions, and reform options are provided in the accompanying


technical working paper [insert link].


A.2 _Strengthen initial teacher preparation_ . MOET should develop clear national guidelines for TTIs


regarding high-priority course content, duration and balance of the student practicum, and align


preservice training with the revised professional standards (PSTs) and evolving structure of the


national ECE program.


To ensure practical impact, these guidelines should be co-developed with TTIs and universities,


piloted in a small number of institutions, and refined based on implementation feedback rather than


introduced solely as a compliance requirement.


The ongoing growth of TTI lecturers should be aligned with the same professional learning system


deployed to upgrade ECEC teachers. TTI lecturers must be prepared to grasp and instruct the


evolving national curriculum. Initial teacher preparation must emphasize the child-centered


features of the curriculum, how to create motivating classroom activities for diverse children,


including those with learning delays or disabilities.


A.3 _Recruit strong teacher candidates, promote equitable participation and deployment_ . Viet Nam’s


developing economy offers a diverse range of job opportunities. TTIs must remain competitive in


attracting and graduating effective young teachers. Targeted scholarships and bonded training


(including ‘grow-your-own’ pathways that build partnerships to recruit and train local community


members in high-need areas—often students, graduates, and paraprofessionals—to become


teachers in their own schools) could help attract teacher candidates from under-represented ethnic


groups. When teacher graduates are deployed more fairly to provinces with severe shortages, GoV


should replace flat hardship allowances with tiered incentives aligned with degree of remoteness,


living costs, and vacancy severity. These measures should be designed as incentive-based


mechanisms—linked to voluntary participation and transparent criteria—rather than mandatory


placement, to ensure sustainability and teacher retention.

### **Improving Working Conditions to Retain and Elevate Teachers**

To improve teacher retention and professional motivation, Viet Nam should build on recent reforms by


strengthening working conditions, professional support, and career pathways. The following


recommendations focus on reducing workload pressures, improving professional learning, and better


aligning incentives with effective teaching practice.


B1. _Revise staffing norms to reduce large classes and enable flexible classroom support_ . To relieve


unsustainable teacher workloads, the ‘teachers-per-class’ model should shift to ‘staffing-per-child’,


allowing for less costly classroom aides or so-called paraprofessionals, common in other nations.


This would offer a tiered workforce strategy, freeing qualified teachers to focus on instructional


practice, planning, and monitoring children’s progress over time. Under this model, every


classroom should continue to be led by a qualified preschool teacher, with paraprofessionals


providing complementary support rather than replacing professional teaching roles.


B2. _Create a coherent continuing professional development (CPD) framework_ . This framework


should be tied to the learning priorities for children delineated in the national curriculum, set


standards for classroom observations and coaching, and establish a continuous professional


learning thread tied to classroom competencies and pathways for career advancement across


different career stages.


_Building on existing strengths in the system_, Viet Nam can scale localized successes with


professional learning communities, mentoring networks, and blended CPD modules led by teacher


training institutions. To avoid a compliance-driven approach, the framework should prioritize


practice-focused learning, co-developed content with teachers and TTIs, and structured follow-up


that supports changes in classroom practice rather than additional documentation.


Effective implementation should be led by MOET, operating through regional training hubs tied


closely with local TTIs, with core teachers positioned as a key instructional support asset for


mentoring, classroom observation, and school-level professional learning. To guard against


administrative overload, core elements of the framework should be piloted and refined based on


evidence of changes in classroom practice, rather than compliance with training hours or


documentation.


B3. _Operationalize the revised professional standards for teachers_ (PSTs). The forthcoming revised


PSTs can anchor the coherent inservice training program for teachers. MOET’s departments of


teacher education and management and early childhood education could issue user-friendly


guidance and digital tools that synchronize the new PSTs with continuing professional development


for teachers. This should emphasize how inservice training intends to advance discrete teacher


**|** 90


competencies and classroom practices, rather than continuing to be seen as a compliance routine,


with clear separation from summative evaluation or disciplinary processes.


B4. _Recognize and reward local educators who develop ECEC teachers_ . ECEC institution leaders play


a critical role in creating the conditions for effective mentoring, peer learning, and reflective


practice, and should be explicitly supported through leadership development and protected time


for instructional leadership. Mentoring and instructional leadership require time and specialized


skills. Formalizing these role and better equipping local educators to carry them out would protect


dedicated time for professional learning and advance the careers of highly effective teachers.


B5. _Align incentives and career advancement with discrete teaching competencies_ . Promotion and


rewards should place greater weight on demonstrated classroom practice, rather than rely


primarily on seniority, courses completed, and credentials. Given the complexity of promotion


reform, a practical first step would be to commission a local feasibility study or pilot, examining


culturally appropriate models for assessing teaching competence and mitigating risks such as


inflated feedback. Competency-based criteria could then be introduced gradually alongside


existing processes, with evaluation of feasibility and unintended effects before scaling.


Stable employment and reduced reliance on contract teachers would further support retention and


promotion of effective teachers and are best addressed through strengthening the teacher pipeline


and improving working conditions, alongside ongoing administrative reforms led by MOET. In this


framework, reducing reliance on contract teachers is not treated as a standalone reform, but as an


outcome of strengthened teacher production, improved deployment, and better retention. Where


conditions allow, gradual conversion of long-serving contract teachers into stable posts should


occur in parallel with pipeline expansion and quota adjustments, rather than as a one-off


administrative exercise. Teachers who build and demonstrate strong classroom practice should


advance within the teacher personnel system.

### **Enrich Classroom Activities, Raising Early Learning**


To deepen implementation of the child-centered curriculum and improve early learning outcomes, Viet


Nam should strengthen classroom practice, instructional support, and collaborative professional


learning. The following recommendations focus on translating curriculum intent into engaging daily


practice that supports all children’s learning and development..


Building on recent experience with staged curriculum reform, Viet Nam has already demonstrated the


value of piloting, feedback, and iterative refinement before national scale-up. Initiatives such as the SRPP


and BaMi pilots illustrate how curriculum guidance, teacher support, and monitoring tools can be tested


and adapted in real classroom settings. These experiences provide a practical foundation for


**|** 91


strengthening classroom practice under the forthcoming revised curriculum, reinforcing the importance


of incremental implementation that emphasizes learning, coaching, and professional dialogue rather


than compliance.


C1. _MOET and local educators must clarify how to enact the evolving national curriculum_ . Clear


rubrics and exemplars of engaging classroom activities would help translate the curriculum into


effective pedagogical practices. Vivid practices and classroom activities– tied to children’s early


learning–could help improve initial training in TTIs and courses offered through continuing


professional education. These resources should illustrate principles of effective practice rather than


prescribe uniform lesson formats, allowing teachers to adapt activities to children’s interests,


developmental levels, and local contexts.


C2. Similarly, simple tools for _observing classrooms and providing careful feedback_ to teachers could


help improve classroom practices. To support improvement rather than compliance, these tools


should be low-stakes, formative, and used primarily for coaching and professional dialogue—not for


ranking teachers or determining sanctions. As the World Bank research shows, mentors and core


teachers —building on their broader role in the CPD framework—can use such tools to assess how


children are engaged in learning tasks, supported emotionally by teachers, and benefiting from


pedagogical approaches tailored to diverse learners.


This approach requires sustained investment in Core Teachers, including clearer job descriptions,


targeted training in instructional support, and explicit expectations that they facilitate pedagogical—


not administrative—professional dialogue. Strengthening these roles places Core Teachers at the


center of instructional improvement, rather than positioning them primarily as compliance or


management agents.


C3. _Embed collaborative inquiry into daily routines_ . Through strengthened professional learning


communities (PLCs) and other collaborative formats, including group-based coaching and peer


learning, teachers and principals can refocus professional conversations on how to better motivate


children’s learning. Compliance-oriented routines and content priorities often distract from this


deeper pedagogical work.


This requires local leadership that creates space for mindful reflection and trusts that teachers can


learn from one another. Local accreditation and external reviews should continue to assess basic


inputs and facilities, while also creating space for informed professional dialogue about classroom


practice—drawing on simple but meaningful observations and encouraging experimentation with


new learning activities.

### **Advancing Central and Local Quality Assurance Mechanisms**

To build a strong QA system that ensures consistent, equitable, high-quality early childhood education


and care nationwide, coordinated action across the five core pillars is essential. The following


recommendations outline how governance, standards, monitoring, data systems, and accountability can


be realigned into an integrated architecture that drives continuous improvement.


D1. _Planning and investment in professional capacity at the commune level are essential for_


_decentralized quality assurance._ Establishing dedicated teams with specialized education officers is


critical to providing continuous oversight and support to schools. Targeted investment in staffing,


training, and digital tools is urgently needed to ensure that decentralization strengthens school


performance—otherwise, it risks amplifying rather than reducing implementation gaps. In practice,


this capacity-building should be phased and targeted to high-need localities first, drawing on


existing education staff where possible and scaling up gradually as systems and resources allow.


D2. Q _uality indicators should comprise a practical and measurable set that captures observation-_


_based aspects of teaching-learning interactions, including the tracking of children’s progress_


_through preschools and primary school_ . These indicators need to reflect elements such as child


engagement, play facilitation, the emotional and cognitive climate of classrooms and feedback


practices, which are central to process quality. To support consistent application, measurement


tools should be standardized and embedded within QA regulations. Standardization should ensure


consistency in expectations and interpretation, while allowing flexibility in how schools


demonstrate quality across diverse contexts.


D3. _ECEC external quality assurance (EQA) should move beyond compliance exercises_ . Under


decentralized governance, local inspection and voluntary accreditation of preschools should


emphasize formative monitoring—where evidence is collected through classroom observation and


used for professional dialogue, coaching and improvement. A shortened, standardized observation


tool with clear rubrics could support this shift, with guidance that a substantial share of inspection


time be devoted to observing practice rather than reviewing documentation. This would also help


EQA teams provide more targeted, developmentally meaningful recommendations.


To be effective, EQA should be accompanied by proportionate post-inspection support, including


structured follow-up coaching aligned with identified needs, and sufficient investment in inspector


training, communication, and resources. In parallel, schools should be supported to strengthen self

evaluation through simple tools and guidance that help teachers and leaders reflect on classroom


practice and track improvement over time, complementing external inspection.


**|** 93


D4. _Advance integrated, longitudinal early-learning information systems that combine structural,_


_process, and outcome indicators._ Integration can be achieved incrementally, by linking existing


EMIS, inspection, and assessment datasets over time rather than building entirely new platforms.


Data flows should link observation results, formative assessments, and administrative records into


EMIS dashboards, supported by clear data governance on ownership, QA, sharing, and retention.


Local preschool directors would benefit by gaining access–in real time– to basic data on quality


elements, along with ongoing tracking of early learning derived from large samples of children.


Easy access to data would inform GoV’s progress in narrowing disparities in ECEC quality among


provinces and local districts.


D5. _Accountability could be operationalized through a simple, standard Quality Card_ for all ECEC


institutions. The Quality Card should be designed as a light-touch transparency tool that supports


improvement rather than a high-stakes ranking mechanism. The card would disclose a minimum


set of indicators: safety compliance, teacher–child ratios, core educational activities, parent


satisfaction results, and progress on the school’s improvement plan. Updating the Quality Card on


a semiannual basis would make accountability an active process, align disclosure with


improvement, and give parents reliable information beyond informal channels. In the longer term,


public reporting on process quality should be institutionalized and a _national Early Childhood_


_Education Quality Portal_ that consolidates inspection results, EMIS indicators, and summaries of


school self-assessments would enable more meaningful parents and public engagement in


enhancing ECEC quality.

### **Lifting Quality while Expanding Coverage of Non-Public ECEC**


To ensure that the expansion of non-public ECEC provision strengthens both access and quality, Viet


Nam should adopt a differentiated, proportional approach to regulation, financing, and quality


assurance across provider types. The following recommendations outline concrete options to expand


non-public provision while safeguarding quality, equity, and child safety.


As an initial implementation step, MOET could convene a targeted consultation process—drawing on


international experience and local feasibility analysis—to **calibrate and sequence** proportional QA and


support models for different non-public providers prior to scaling.


E1. _Adopt a mixed, context-specific strategy to expand affordable, quality non-public ECEC._ Viet


Nam should deliberately leverage a combination of public–private partnerships (PPPs), employer

supported childcare, community- and home-based care, and targeted subsidies for low-income and


migrant families, selecting models based on local demand, provider capacity, and regulatory


readiness.


**|** 94


(i) _PPP models_ _combine public financing with private operational efficiency._ Build–operate


models reduce investor risk by having the government construct facilities while private


operators manage them under performance contracts. Conversely, private investors can


build centers when supported with concessional measures such as low-cost land, tax


incentives, or viability-gap funding. Service contracting and vouchers allow the state to


‘purchase seats’ from private providers or give parents fee subsidies. Anchor-operator or


franchise networks can professionalize small providers. Each model requires clear contracts,


risk-sharing arrangements, and strong monitoring to ensure quality and access.


(ii) _With clearer incentives and stronger enforcement of existing labor provisions, employer-_


_supported childcare can rapidly expand accessible services for_ _under-3 children of factory_


_workers_ . Companies can establish on-site or near-site childcare centers, benefiting from


simplified approval, co-financing, or regulatory requirements tied to industrial park


development. Evidence shows employer childcare improves retention and productivity,


strengthening the business case. Firms unable to run centers can partner with nearby


providers to reserve seats or subsidize fees, supported by tax deductibility or matching


grants. Such cost-sharing leverages private resources for public goals..


(iii) _Community- and home-based care can be strengthened by structured support and quality_


_assurance_ . Policy should focus on licensing micro-providers, offering basic training, and


assigning mentors or cluster supervisors to ensure safety and quality. Local authorities can


provide learning materials, small grants, or performance-based incentives, helping home


providers meet minimum standards.


(iv) _To ensure equity, Viet Nam should pair supply-side expansion with demand-side subsidies_


_that reduce out-of-pocket fees for vulnerable families._ Options include fee waivers or


reductions for children under 3 of poor, near-poor, or migrant workers; portable childcare


vouchers redeemable at licensed providers that would give families choice and create


demand for quality private provision. Safeguards such as fee caps and monitoring are


needed to prevent misuse. When aligned with PPP and community models, subsidies can


ensure access for all children. Although our focus is non-public, one option is for the


government to set up more public crèches specifically in worker dormitory areas or remote


villages, which serve as a safety net for those who absolutely cannot pay. Given the


constraints on public teacher payroll, such expansion should complement, not replace,


efforts to harness private providers.


In practice, these models should be selectively applied and piloted based on local demand, provider


capacity, and regulatory readiness, rather than rolled out uniformly across all provinces.


**|** 95


E2. _Targeted financial and market supports can help lower barriers for non-public ECEC providers_ .


The government can partner with banking authorities to extend subsidized small and medium


enterprise (SME) loans, establish credit-guarantee mechanisms, and earmark social-enterprise


lending for childcare operators. Targeted startup grants or matching funds—building on but


significantly scaling up the limited incentives under Decree 105—can help providers meet upfront


infrastructure and safety requirements. Demand-side subsidies tied to child enrollment and quality


standards would incentivize providers to serve low-income worker families while maintaining


compliance. Complementary regulatory incentives—such as tax deductions for employer

supported childcare or reduced utility rates—can further improve financial viability. Finally, better


information and matchmaking, including childcare needs maps and investment forums, can


connect potential investors with high-demand areas and encourage partnerships between


industrial zones, enterprises, and childcare operators. These financial supports should be tightly


targeted to priority areas and linked to clear quality and compliance requirements to manage fiscal


risk and ensure value for money.


E3. _Ensuring quality and continuous improvement in non-public ECEC establishments._ Viet Nam’s


rapid expansion of non-public ECEC provision makes a strong, coherent QA framework essential to


prevent quality dilution and ensure safe, developmentally appropriate services for all children. A


unified QA framework covering ages 0–5 and all provider types would establish clear, measurable


standards—ranging from health and safety to learning environments and caregiver-child


interactions—with tiered requirements suited to different settings. Tiered standards should


preserve proportionality, ensuring that requirements are appropriate to provider size, age group


served, and operating context. To support compliance and improvement, Viet Nam should invest


in practical capacity-building tools, including a national quality toolkit for non-public providers,


regular training, and peer-learning opportunities across public and private centers. As the number


of private operators grows, inspector capacity must also be expanded, and provinces may consider


authorizing MOET-accredited third-party inspectors. Any use of third-party inspectors should


operate under clear MOET accreditation, oversight, and accountability arrangements. Finally,


introducing quality-recognition mechanisms—such as a simple rating system or incentives for high

performing centers—would motivate providers to exceed minimum requirements and promote


continuous improvement across the sector.


E3. _Strengthening non-public ECEC workforce._ First, the pipeline should be expanded through


increased early childhood training enrollment and the introduction of shorter certified courses for


caregivers of younger children, supported by partnerships between teacher colleges and industrial


zones to create training-to-employment pathways for local women. Second, improving


remuneration and career development—such as targeted wage subsidies or hardship allowances


**|** 96


and better use of existing supports under Decree 105/2020—would help retain staff, particularly in


underserved areas. Finally, building structured professional networks for non-public providers, and


scaling successful models of collaboration between public and private preschools, would


strengthen continuous learning and give the non-public workforce access to shared resources and


peer support. These measures should build on existing policy instruments and be phased to avoid


creating parallel employment systems or unsustainable fiscal commitments.


E4. Viet Nam’s growing and increasingly diverse ECEC ecosystem requires _a modern, comprehensive_


_management information system (MIS) capable of tracking all providers_ —including public, private,


and home-based groups. Expansion of the MIS can be achieved incrementally by extending existing


MOET data platforms and registration processes, rather than developing entirely new systems.


Expanding MOET’s current data system to include a universal registration database would allow


every childcare provider to be logged with core information such as capacity, licensing status, staff


qualifications, and enrollment, supported by close coordination with ward and commune


authorities. A strengthened MIS can also drive better decision-making and transparency through


public dashboards showing supply–demand gaps in industrial zones and helping parents identify


licensed providers. Integrating an inspection module into the MIS—capturing visit records,


violations, and follow-up actions—would reinforce accountability and enable timely responses to


safety and quality concerns, especially in light of recent incidents in unregulated settings.

**Appendix E**

were identified through analysis of TEACH ECEC observations, national survey data, stakeholder interviews, and the current ECE Program. Each domain reflects a
foundational element of high-quality teaching that is currently underdeveloped in practice and requires targeted support through CPD, mentoring, and schoolbased professional learning. strengthening. They do not represent the full universe of CPD content required for ECEC teachers. Health, safety, ICT, ethics, and administrative skill areas remain
important and should continue to be maintained through provincial and school-based priorities. The focus here is on the core pedagogical practices that are
currently underdeveloped and most critical to improving teaching and learning.
