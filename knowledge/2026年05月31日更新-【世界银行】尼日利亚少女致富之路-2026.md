###### **C. Key policy** **opportunities:**


To increase Nigeria’s educational achievements and

unlock economic opportunities, priorities should be

to:

→ Identify and target the most vulnerable groups

of girls, mitigating geographical and wealth

disparities

→ Improve sexual and reproductive health

outcomes and shift gender norms

→ Improve girls’ access to, retention in, and

completion of school, particularly at the

secondary level

→ Improve access to productive assets and high
return economic opportunities.

→ Improve legal and policy frameworks


This context sets the stage for targeted

interventions that can help Nigeria maximize

its investment in girls’ education through

improved economic opportunities and stronger

implementation of existing legal and policy

frameworks.

**Pathways to prosperity for adolescent girls in Nigeria**

###### **A. Different pathways to empowerment**


Data reveal how adolescent girls (ages 15-19) in Nigeria follow different pathways to empowerment, aligning with

five key profiles identified in the Pathways to Prosperity Report framework (see Ajayi and Koussoubé, 2024).



→ **Grace’s path** (In School, Not Working, Not

Married, No Child): This path represents the

largest group in Southern geopolitical zones,

especially in South East (62.4%), South West

(50.4%) and South South (45.4%). However,

this pathway becomes significantly less

prevalent in the Northern zones, particularly in

the North East (22.8%).


→ **Imani’s path** (In School, Working, Not Married,

No Child): This path is less represented, with a

relatively homogeneous presence within zones

ranging from 8.2% in the South East to 13% in

the North East zones.


→ **Mariam’s path** (Not in School, Not Working,

Not Married, No Child): This vulnerable pathway

is particularly alarming in the Northern zones,

North East (23.3%) and both North Central

and North West (24.7%), signaling a critical risk

of these girls transitioning to early marriage

and childbearing. The levels remain high,

although lower, in the southern zones as well,

with a peak in the South South (18.4%).



→ **Chantal’s path** (Not in School, Working, Not

Married, No Child): This pathway shows a

significant presence in the South South, North

Central, and North East, with around 17.7%

of girls, while the lowest rate is observed

in the South East (7.4%). This distribution

represents both challenges and opportunities

for supporting girls’ economic participation.


→ **Aya’s path** (Not in School, Not Working,

Married with Children): The most vulnerable

pathway is present at very low levels in the

southern zones, with a peak of only 1.3%

of girls in the South South. However, it is

prevalent in the North West (11.6%) and North

East (7.5%). This highlights areas where early

marriage and childbearing significantly impact

girls’ development.

###### **B. Geographic disparities**


Nigeria presents considerable North/South disparities concerning the prevalence of girls who are out of school

and not working or are married or have children:


→ **Northern zones** : North West (55.1%), North East (46.4%) and North Central (42.2%) zones

exhibit high rates of vulnerability requiring tailored interventions for conflict-affected and

fragile regions.


→ **Southern zones** : The prevalence of vulnerable girls is lower in the southern part of the

country: South East (21.9%), South West (22.5%) and South South (25.1%), reflecting the

zone’s comparatively stronger economic opportunities, higher levels of urbanization, and

broader access to education.

Sexual and Reproductive Health Education:


- **The use of a tablet-based app on sexual and reproductive health education modules**

improved knowledge and attitudes towards sexual and reproductive health for girls in

school in Southwest Nigeria (Akande et al., 2024). [12]


- **As teenage mothers often lack nutritional education and do not have access to**

**counseling, using mobile apps on maternal and infant feeding practices** significantly

improved knowledge of food types for infants, exclusive breastfeeding and complementary

feeding (Sosanya et al., 2025). [13]


School fee reduction and e-learning:


- **In 1976, transitioning into tuition-free primary education and expanding primary**

**classrooms** significantly increased female education and reduced fertility among women

under the age of 25 (Osili & Long, 2008). [14] However, improvements stagnated with the

reintroduction of school fees in 1981.


- **The use of generative artificial intelligence to support learning through an after-**

**school program** is showing promising results to bridge gender gaps in learning. Preliminary

evidence from an innovative pilot reveals that girls, who were initially lagging boys in

performance, seem to gain more from this intervention and improve their skills and

knowledge (De Simone et al., 2025). [15]


Community engagement and norm change:


- **A community-wide big-push intervention in northern Nigeria (Pathways to Choice)**

combining mentored girls’ clubs, life skills, and vocational training reduced child marriage

rates by over 80%, from 86% in the control group to 21% among participants, and

increased school enrollment by 69 percentage points. The whole-community focus —

engaging religious and community leaders alongside girls — was essential to producing

effects on entrenched social norms (Cohen, Abubakar and Perlman, 2026).

**Research from similar contexts suggests four key strategies:**


Improve adolescent girls’ access to, retention in, and completion of secondary education


- **Education policy reforms** : In Kenya, the 2008 secondary education expansion program

successfully shifted young women toward skilled employment and delayed age at

first marriage and first birth (Brudevold-Newman, 2021). [16] In Ghana, secondary school

scholarships for rural girls increased aspirations for tertiary education, enhanced

cognitive skills, and improved math and reading achievement, as well as digital and

financial capital. Scholarship recipients also experienced reduced fertility rates and

secured better jobs (Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer, 2021). [17] When combined with health

services and rights-based reforms, education policies have effectively delayed marriage

and childbearing (Mason-Jones et al., 2023; Harari, 2019). [18,19]


- **Reducing schooling costs** : In Niger, the provision of conditional cash transfers reduced

the dropout rate by half three years after the intervention and delayed age at first

marriage and first birth (Giacobino et al., 2024). [20] In Burkina Faso, conditional cash

transfers yielded larger gains especially among girls by explicitly incentivizing school

participation (Akresh et al., 2013). [21] Cash transfers, in-kind support, and school feeding

programs have also proven effective at improving education outcomes and delaying

marriage across sub-Saharan Africa, including in Ghana, Uganda, and Malawi (Evans,

Mendez Acosta, and Yuan, 2024). [22] Beyond financial transfers, in Zambia, distributing

bicycles to rural schoolgirls improved school attendance and shifted aspirations, including

increasing the ideal age of marriage and reducing the desired number of pregnancies

(Fiala et al., 2025). [23]


Improve adolescent girls’ access to productive assets and economic opportunities


- **Integrated skills training** : Programs combining skills training, grants, and mentorship

have consistently increased employment and earnings, as demonstrated in Liberia (Adoho

et al., 2014). [24] Skills training paired with market information has also increased women’s

participation in male-dominated trades (Hicks et al., 2016). [25]


- **Micro-franchising initiatives** : In Kenya, a micro-franchising initiative provided young

women from underprivileged areas of Nairobi with vocational training, life skills education,

start-up funding, and ongoing business guidance. The program initially generated

significant income gains, primarily by enabling transitions from wage employment to self
employment; however, these gains declined over time (Brudevold-Newman et al., 2024). [26]


- **Low-cost informational interventions** : Providing information on trade-specific earnings

can reduce gender-based occupational segregation by encouraging more equitable and

Improve sexual and reproductive health outcomes and shift towards positive gender attitudes by:


- **Girls’ safe spaces and community engagement** : In Côte d’Ivoire, programs equipping

out-of-school girls with life skills, sexual and reproductive health (SRH) knowledge, and

entrepreneurship training—along with grants—significantly improved SRH behaviors, gender

attitudes, mental health, and income-generating activities. Impacts were even stronger

when interventions included husbands’ and future husbands’ clubs (Boulhane et al., 2024). [28]

Similar results were found in Uganda, where bundled vocational and soft-skills training

in girls’ clubs increased engagement in income-generating activities and improved bodily

autonomy (Bandiera et al., 2020). [29]


- **Community engagement to address violence against women** : In Tanzania, a combined

program offering adolescent girls goal-setting sessions on sexual and reproductive health,

alongside a soccer-based curriculum for male partners focused on gender norms, reduced

intimate partner violence and improved relationship quality, particularly among sexually

active girls (Shah et al., 2022). [30]


Improve legal and policy frameworks


- **Supporting pregnant girls and young mothers** : In Senegal, policies allowing pregnant girls

to continue their education through flexible timelines and supportive school environments

have reduced educational disruption (UNFPA, 2020). [31] In addition, access to early childhood

services increases women’s labor market participation. In Burkina Faso, community

childcare centers established near urban public works sites enabled mothers in temporary

employment programs to keep children close to their workplace, significantly reducing

childcare constraints and improving economic participation (Ajayi et al., 2024). [32]


- **Comprehensive health education** : In Kenya, education subsidies combined with HIV

education positively influenced adolescent girls’ sexual behavior, delaying teenage pregnancy

and marriage (Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer, 2015). [33] In Cameroon, a low-cost intervention

combining sexual health education with HIV risk-salience questionnaires increased risk

awareness and reduced early pregnancies. The intervention was cost-effective, with the risk
salience component achieving substantial behavioral change at minimal cost (Dupas, Huillery

and Seban, 2018). [34]

## **Conclusions**


Nigeria stands at a critical juncture in addressing adolescent
girls’ pathways to prosperity. While the country has
immense potential, it still grapples with significant social
and economic challenges, including insecurity in the North
West, ongoing insurgency by terrorist groups in the North
East, and separatist agitations in the South East zones.
Amid these challenges, by focusing on the areas highlighted
in this brief - addressing the geographical and wealth
disparities, strengthening adolescent girls’ access to, retention
in, and completion of secondary education, expanding
economic opportunities, and strengthening its legal and
policy frameworks – Nigeria can unlock the potential of its
adolescent girls. The economic returns from such investments
present Nigeria with an unparalleled opportunity to transform
the lives of adolescent girls and substantially advance its
broader development trajectory. Addressing these challenges
requires coordinated action among multiple sectors, with
interventions tailored to diverse contexts within Nigeria and
particular attention to vulnerable populations, especially in
conflict and fragile settings.
