About ESMAP


The Energy Sector Management Assistance Program (ESMAP) is a partnership between the [World Bank](http://www.worldbank.org/) and [over 20 partners](https://esmap.org/governance)
to help low- and middle-income countries reduce poverty and boost growth through sustainable energy solutions. ESMAP’s
analytical and advisory services are fully integrated within the World Bank’s country financing and policy dialogue in the energy
sector. Through the WB, ESMAP works to accelerate the energy transition required to achieve [Sustainable Development Goal](https://sustainabledevelopment.un.org/sdg7)
[7 (SDG7), which ensures access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all. It helps shape WB strategies](https://sustainabledevelopment.un.org/sdg7)
and programs to achieve the WB Climate Change Action Plan targets. Learn more at: [https://www.esmap.org.](https://www.esmap.org)

Burundi’s National Development Plan prioritizes economic transformation and improved social welfare,
recognizing the energy deficit as a critical obstacle. Despite significant hydroelectric potential, chronic
underinvestment has resulted in severely limited electricity access. Per capita electricity consumption
is among the lowest in Sub-Saharan Africa; wood biomass accounts for 95 percent of primary energy
consumption. This dependence drives deforestation and contributes to respiratory illnesses and physical
strain. In this context, the Government of Burundi implemented reforms and innovative mechanisms to
address its energy challenges. These include the revision of the electricity law in March 2024 and the
introduction of a results-based financing scheme aimed at bolstering private sector development. Further
demonstrating its commitment to expanding electricity access, the government launched a national
electrification strategy in October 2024 and pledged its support to the Mission 300 initiative in 2025. [3]


In 2016, the World Bank, with the support of the Energy Sector Management Assistance Program (ESMAP),
launched the Global Survey on Energy Access, using the Multi-Tier Framework (MTF). The MTF survey aims
to provide detailed data on access to electricity and modern energy cooking services. The MTF moves
beyond binary access measures—namely, having access or not to electricity and having access or not
to clean cooking fuels and technologies—to capture the multidimensional nature of energy access at
the end-user level, the range of technologies, and diverse user experiences.


The MTF survey in Burundi was conducted in November–December 2021 covering all five provinces. A

approach is used to fill in missing data points from 1990 on. This model, which was adapted from a method used
by the World Health Organization, predicts electricity access by accounting for the hierarchical structure of the
data (country and regional levels) based on United Nations classifications. The model is applied to all countries
with at least one data point. Actual survey data are used whenever available, and the model is used only to fill
gaps for missing years or to perform global and regional analyses.

The World Bank’s Global Electrification Database estimates that Burundi reached a 10 percent electrification
rate in 2021. This figure is a model-based estimate, calculated using survey data from the 2017 Demographic and
Health Survey and a 2014 national survey (both of which were originally sampled from the 2008 census). Because

In Burundi, over 99 percent of households use biomass as their primary cooking fuel, with wood being

The MTF measures access to modern energy cooking services along a spectrum ranging from Tier 0 (no
access) to Tier 5 (full access) across six attributes: Cooking Exposure, Cookstove Efficiency, Convenience,
Safety of the primary cookstove, Affordability, and Fuel Availability. [6] Approximately 91 percent of households

Limited access to modern energy cooking services is strongly linked to significant health and genderrelated impacts. When families rely on polluting fuels, women and children are exposed to dangerous
levels of indoor air pollution, causing serious respiratory and cardiovascular illnesses. The lack of efficient
cooking solutions also places a disproportionate burden on women and girls, whose time-consuming
duties of gathering fuel and managing stoves contribute to time poverty, limiting their opportunities for
education and employment. Therefore, improving access to modern energy cooking services is crucial
for advancing public health and gender equality.

BOX 1.3 • **ASSESSING THE CAPACITY TIER OF SOLAR SOLUTIONS**


Solar devices are classified into three types based on the number of light bulbs and the type of appliances
or electricity services they can provide. This categorization is used to assess their Capacity tier.

   - Solar lanterns (SLs) power only one light bulb, and may or may not include a power outlet (to charge a
phone or power a radio). The Capacity tier of a solar lantern may be Tier 0 or Tier 1, depending on their
score. The score will depend on (1) the possibility of charging a mobile phone or powering a radio and
(2) the level of the lighting service calculated based on the ratio of the number of people served with
adequate lighting to the household size (annex 1).

   - Solar lighting systems (SLSs) power two or more light bulbs, and may or not include a power outlet (to
charge a phone or power a radio). The Capacity tier of a SLS may be Tier 0 or Tier 1, depending on the
score. The score will depend on (1) the possibility of charging a mobile phone or powering a radio and
(2) the level of the lighting service calculated based on the ratio of the number of people served with
adequate lighting to the household size (annex 1).

   - Solar home systems (SHSs) power two or more light bulbs and can power at least low-load appliances
(such as a television, fan, computer, or VCD/DVD player). The Capacity tier of a SHS is Tier 2 by default,
unless the household owns appliances that reach an Appliance tier above Tier 2, in which case the Capacity
tier will correspond to the highest Appliance Tier reached by the appliances (in working condition) owned

**USING THE MULTI-TIER FRAMEWORK TO DRIVE POLICY AND INVESTMENT**


The MTF provides crucial data and insights to accelerate global energy access. It offers robust insights
that inform evidence-based policy reforms and targeted project interventions, in addition to delivering
valuable market intelligence to the private sector. Across many countries, MTF survey results are driving
deeper sector dialogue and guiding strategic investments toward ambitious access goals. Among
others, they are supporting the optimization of policy frameworks, development of effective investment
strategies, improvement of project design and utility performance, and impact assessments that enable
successful development efforts. The MTF serves as a vital instrument for establishing baselines and
monitoring progress toward SDG target 7.1.


The MTF survey delivers detailed data on household energy consumption. Stakeholders, including
governments, development partners, the private sector, nongovernmental organizations, investors, and
service providers, use these data for planning and delivery. On the supply side, the survey captures all
energy sources that households use, and measures each MTF attribute. On the demand side, it captures
energy expenditure, energy usage patterns, user preferences, willingness to pay (WTP) for grid connections,
off-grid solutions, and cooking services, and consumers’ satisfaction with their primary energy source.


MTF data empower governments to establish country-specific energy access targets. Governments can
use these data to set universal access goals tailored to unique domestic conditions, available resources,
and achievement timelines. These data can also help governments upgrade access for existing users—by
elevating electrified households to higher tiers—and add new connections, while also determining the
minimum target tier for new connections.


MTF data inform the design and prioritization of interventions for expanding access. This ensures
interventions achieve maximum impact within a given budget. The data can be disaggregated by attribute
and technology, revealing why households remain at lower tiers and pinpointing key barriers (e.g.,
insufficient generation capacity, high energy costs, or a weak transmission and distribution network).
This enables targeted interventions that maximize benefits to households. MTF data also guide the
selection of the most suitable technologies for nonelectrified households, indicating whether grid or
off-grid solutions are more appropriate. Moreover, demand-side data, including energy spending, WTP,
energy use, and appliance ownership, can inform the design and targeting of government programs,
projects, and investments for energy access.

**MTF SURVEY IMPLEMENTATION IN BURUNDI**


Burundi’s MTF survey used an innovative spatial algorithm to overcome outdated census data. Standard
MTF surveys typically rely on recent census data for sampling frames. However, with Burundi’s most
recent census dating back to 2008, using those data for the 2021 MTF survey would have introduced
significant bias. This bias would result because substantial demographic changes between 2008 and 2021,
including population growth, migration, and evolving household structures, would not be accounted for.


An alternative sampling frame was developed to ensure accuracy of data. To mitigate the risk of bias,
an alternative sampling frame was constructed using a spatial algorithm developed by EED Advisory,
in collaboration with the National Autonomous University of Mexico and the Stockholm Environment
Institute, as detailed in annex 5. The algorithm leverages the World Gridded Population Density Version
4.11 (CIESIN 2018) from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center and 2015 data from the Global
Rural-Urban Mapping Project (GRUMP) of the Center for International Earth Science Information Network.


The Global Rural-Urban Mapping Project defined urban areas [12] for a national sampling frame. The
population was divided into primary sampling units (PSUs), each with an average size of approximately
241 households, though PSU sizes varied considerably (see annex 5). Notably, unlike typical MTF surveys,
the first-stage sampling weights in Burundi were calculated based on the number of households within
each PSU, rather than the number of PSUs, to account for the significant variation in PSU size.


Data collection for the MTF survey was conducted in two phases: a household listing exercise, followed
by primary data collection. The household listing exercise was performed in August 2021 across 65
enumeration areas—40 urban and 25 rural—to determine electrification status (see annex 5). This listing,
encompassing approximately 23,000 households, informed the subsequent sampling strategy, ensuring
a statistically representative sample of electrified households.


Subsequently, primary data were collected using comprehensive household and institutional surveys.
This second phase took place from October 25 to December 4, 2021. The household survey employed
a two-stage stratified sampling approach to achieve national representativeness. It covered all 18
provinces and maintained an approximate 50:50 ratio of electrified to nonelectrified households, as
well as urban to rural areas (see annex 5).

- There is a marked divergence in electricity access between urban and rural areas. Approximately

- Burundi faces significant challenges in the Quality and Reliability of its electricity supply.

which damage appliances.


- High costs and limited infrastructure pose major barriers to grid expansion in Burundi.
Nearly 70 percent of households cite a lack of grid availability as the primary reason they
remain unconnected. For those with physical access to the network, the connection fee of
FBu 492,000 (US$174.41) is a significant obstacle for about 30 percent of households. While
one-third of unconnected households are willing to pay the current fee up front, this figure
nearly doubles to 64 percent if the fee is reduced to FBu 68,880 (US$24.42).


- Most electrified households in Burundi own only a few appliances, indicating underutilized
grid capacity. Eight out of ten electrified homes use only very low– or low-load appliances.

**Households without electricity access**


- Most households without electricity access (63 percent) live in communities where the grid is not
available. Meanwhile, 11 percent of these unconnected homes are actually “under the grid,” meaning

- Households lacking electricity are often the most economically disadvantaged, especially those

solar lighting systems is much lower (17–32 percent).


**Grid-connected households**


- Grid-connected households face significant challenges with electricity supply performance. Many
contend with limited daily availability: 44 percent receive electricity less than 23 hours per day,
often totalling between 16 and 23 hours per day. Reliability is also a major concern, with over 50
percent of grid users experiencing frequent or long unscheduled outages. Furthermore, voltage
issues affect about 12–13 percent of grid-connected households.


- Electricity consumption remains low, varying by location and wealth. Nationwide, 39 percent of gridconnected households consume up to 60 kWh per month. Urban households consume considerably
more electricity than rural ones, and consumption also increases with wealth. For instance, only
27 percent of grid-connected households in the poorest quintile consume over 100 kWh monthly,
a figure that nearly doubles to 50 percent in the wealthiest quintile.

**POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS**


In Burundi, 74 percent of households lack electricity access. These households can be categorized
into two groups: those residing “under the grid,” meaning they live in communities with existing grid
infrastructure, and those living in communities not yet reached by the grid. For the 11 percent of
households without access who live “under the grid,” grid densification could provide electricity access,
especially if connection costs could be spread over time or through available financing options. For
the 63 percent of households without electricity in nonelectrified communities, off-grid solar solutions




Electrification initiatives hold significant potential to unlock a range of productive uses of electricity, driving
economic growth and improving livelihoods, especially given that half of all grid-connected households in
the country are currently underutilizing the grid’s capacity with only very low– or low-power appliances.
To advance households to higher tiers of energy access (Tier 4 and 5), a strategic focus on expanding the
availability and affordability of energy-efficient appliances is crucial. By enhancing the supply of popular
items like refrigerators, fans, electric kitchen mills, and televisions, and coupling this with accessible
consumer financing options, households will be empowered to adopt higher-load appliances. This not
only directly improves their quality of life but also enables small businesses to grow, supports the cold
chain for food and medicine, and boosts overall economic activity, moving beyond basic lighting to truly
transformative energy use.


Approximately 18 percent of households use off-grid solutions, primarily solar lanterns. However, 34
percent of households relying on off-grid solar solutions are classified as Tier 0, due to insufficient capacity.
Promoting the distribution of high-quality solar devices and enhancing the availability and affordability
of higher-tier solar products can enable off-grid solar households to progress to higher access tiers,
facilitating the use of desired appliances such as televisions and radios.


To strengthen the roadmap for implementing stand-alone SHSs and mini-grids, it is critical to improve the
enabling environment for decentralized energy. Burundi’s energy compact features a number of priority
actions, such as the enforcement of duty exemptions for solar products and the strengthening of capacity
at the Burundi Tax Office (OBR). The adoption and enforcement of quality standards are also key to ensure
reliable service. Financing tools that accelerate expansion and ensure equitable access should be prioritized.
For example, results-based financing (RBF) incentivizes private sector developers by tying payments to
verified outcomes. It is also important to develop well-designed subsidy frameworks that reduce up-front
costs for both providers and low-income end users. These subsidies should be transparent and targeted
to bridge the affordability gap without distorting the market. Combining these tools—for instance, using
an RBF mechanism to reward scale while a targeted subsidy supports vulnerable consumers—will create
a robust financial ecosystem that drives rapid, sustainable, and equitable off-grid electrification.


**KEY MESSAGES**


- There is a stark urban-rural divide in cooking patterns. In urban areas, 82 percent of households
cook primarily with charcoal, using either traditional (47 percent) or improved (35 percent)
cookstoves. About 40 percent of urban households reach Tiers 1 or 2. In contrast, 95 percent
of rural households use a three-stone stove, with collected wood as the primary fuel.


- Poor ventilation and exposure to harmful emissions are major concerns. Approximately 94
percent of households nationwide are in Tier 0 for Cooking Exposure. This is largely due to
the widespread use of three-stone stoves and poor ventilation.


- Simply improving ventilation could elevate users of traditional stoves and improved stoves
to Tier 2 for Cooking Exposure. To achieve this, households cooking indoors need external
or integrated exhaust systems. Campaigns raising awareness of indoor air pollution’s health
impacts, coupled with affordable stoves and exhaust systems, are crucial for driving change.


- Cooking fuels are widely considered Affordable and Available, but Convenience and Safety vary.
For 96 percent of households, the cost of fuel is less than 5 percent of their total expenditure,
and the primary cooking fuel is deemed “mostly” or “always” available by a large majority of
households (90 percent). In terms of Safety, 87 percent of households reported no injuries,
and 12 percent sustained minor injuries from cooking. In terms of Convenience, the majority
(62 percent) of households are in Tier 2, spending under seven hours per week on acquiring
and preparing the fuel but over 10 minutes preparing the stove per meal.


- Improved cookstoves alone are insufficient for ensuring clean cooking. While approximately 6
percent of households nationally use improved cookstoves, 83 percent of these users still fall
into Tier 0 for Cooking Exposure. This is because they cook indoors without proper ventilation
and must therefore install adequate exhaust systems. This ventilation gap presents a significant
opportunity: 46 percent of urban households currently using traditional stoves could switch
to improved cookstoves and incorporate proper ventilation without needing to change their
fuel (e.g., charcoal).


- A notable number of charcoal users are willing to pay for improved cookstoves, with a
significant increase in interest at a lower price point. Among households using charcoal as
their primary cooking fuel (9 percent of all households, but 82 percent of urban households),
41 percent are willing to pay FBu 15,000 (US$5.32) up front for improved cookstoves like the
Kamaro or Biikigiti, with another 24 percent willing to pay over time. Reducing the price to FBu
4,950 (US$1.75) increases the willingness to pay up front to 62 percent, with another 12 percent
choosing to pay in installments.

- Most households using wood for cooking are willing to pay for improved cookstoves, especially at
a lower price point. Among the 91 percent of households that rely on wood, 56 percent are willing
to pay FBu 7,333 (US$2.60) up front for a low-end wood-based improved cookstove. Switching to

Only 0.1 percent of households use clean fuels as their primary cooking fuel. These households are
all in urban areas and fall within the wealthiest socioeconomic quintile. Biogas stoves and electric

high urban electricity access, the adoption of electric stoves is extremely low, likely due to high
up-front costs. A national clean cooking strategy is urgently needed to address the affordability
and availability of clean fuels and to raise awareness about the health benefits of clean cooking.


- A successful rollout of modern energy cooking services requires integrating planning and
electrification. By treating cooking as a core electrical load during the planning phase, utilities can
properly size and reinforce the grid, avoiding overloads. This unified approach also allows for the
creation of special tariffs, making electric cooking more affordable for households.

Several key household characteristics affect the adoption of modern energy cooking services. Factors

**EXPANDING ACCESS TO IMPROVED COOKSTOVES**


In Burundi, approximately 6 percent of households primarily use an improved cookstove—two-thirds
of them relying on charcoal (see figure 3.1). A concerning 83 percent of these households are still in
Tier 0 for Cooking Exposure due to poor ventilation (indoor cooking without an exhaust system). The
remaining 17 percent have good ventilation, because they cook outdoors or on a veranda (see box 3.2).
To improve access to modern energy cooking services, households cooking indoors using improved
cookstoves must address ventilation issues, by using external or embedded exhaust systems.


Households using traditional stoves could also improve their access to modern energy by switching
to improved cookstoves with exhaust systems, without switching fuel. This opportunity applies to 46
percent of urban households.

**POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS**


Burundi must support the 95 percent of households at Tier 0-1 in terms of access to modern energy
cooking services to transition to higher tiers through the adoption of improved cookstoves and clean
fuel stoves. Key steps toward this goal are outlined below.


Address poor ventilation among traditional and improved cookstove users. Campaigns to raise awareness
of indoor air pollution’s health impacts, coupled with affordable stoves and exhaust systems, can be
leveraged to promote external or integrated exhaust systems among households cooking indoors. The
main households to target are those using traditional and improved stoves, which, although meeting
Tier 1 for Cookstove Emissions, push over 50 percent of traditional and 80 percent of improved cookstove
users to Tier 0 for Cooking Exposure, due to poor ventilation. Simply improving ventilation could elevate
8 percent of households to Tier 2 for Cooking Exposure.


Accelerate the adoption of improved charcoal cookstoves in urban areas. Offering fast-ignition improved
cookstoves and addressing fuel affordability through reduced up-front costs and flexi-payment options
can help urban households adopt improved cookstoves—without switching fuel. The segment to target
is the remaining 35 percent of urban households using charcoal but not yet with an improved cookstove
(of the 82 percent of urban households using charcoal). Notably, the improved cookstoves should
have exhaust systems. Over 65 percent of charcoal users are willing to pay for stoves like the Kamaro
or Biikigiti, and their willingness increases as prices decline.


Promote improved wood-based cookstoves in rural areas. Considering 91 percent of households rely
on wood, primarily collected for free, switching to improved wood-based cookstoves is the most viable
solution, especially in rural areas. While many are willing to pay a reduced price for low-end stoves,
adoption of high-end stoves requires consumer finance or subsidies. These mechanisms are crucial
for driving stronger rural adoption.


Develop a comprehensive strategy to drive the adoption of clean fuel stoves. Given the low penetration
of electric stoves in urban areas and the absence of reported LPG use, targeted interventions are needed.
Consumer finance or subsidies can address the high up-front costs of electric stoves, though ongoing
electricity costs remain a barrier. In rural areas, both biogas potential and LPG adoption should be
assessed, placing priority on Fuel Availability and Affordability. A comprehensive plan, including supply

The Multi-Tier Framework (MTF) measures electricity access provided by any technology (grid or offgrid) used as a main source of electricity, through seven attributes that capture key characteristics of
the energy supply that affect the user experience. Households without any source of electricity (also
including those using dry-cell batteries as their main source) are designated as having _no access_ to
electricity.


The seven attributes are Capacity, Availability, Reliability, Quality, Affordability, Formality, and Health and
Safety. Based on the combination of the attributes, the MTF defines six tiers of access with minimum
requirements, ranging from Tier 0 (no access) to Tier 5 (full access) along a continuum of improvement
(table A1.1). Each attribute is assessed separately, and the overall tier of electricity access for each user
(household, enterprise, education facility, or health facility) is calculated by applying the lowest tier
obtained in any of the attributes.

**CAPACITY (WHAT APPLIANCES CAN BE POWERED?)**


provide a certain amount of electricity to operate various appliances, ranging from a few watts for lightemitting diode lights and mobile phone chargers to several thousand watts for space heaters or air
conditioners. Capacity is measured in watts for the grid, mini-grids, and generators and in watt-hours
for rechargeable batteries, solar lanterns, solar lighting systems (SLSs), and solar home systems (SHSs).
It is often difficult to determine the Capacity of the system by simple observation. Thus, Capacity is
approximated either based on the electricity source or based on the appliances owned by the surveyed
entity (e.g., a household). Appliances are classified into tiers based on their power ratings. The Appliance
tier of a surveyed entity corresponds to the highest tier of all the appliances it owns. For example,
if a household owns several appliances, the highest-load appliance determines the Appliance tier
for the household and thus the Capacity tier. If a household does not own any appliance (in working
condition)—including lighting—the Capacity tier is 0. Households without any access to electricity (and
households using dry-cell batteries as the main electricity source) fall by default into Capacity tier 0.
All households are included in the calculation of this attribute. The tiers of various electricity sources
are outlined below.


**Grid** : Capacity is by default Tier 5.


**Mini-grid** : Capacity is Tier 5, unless the entity surveyed (e.g., household) experiences a load limit or
cannot power all its appliances, in which case the Capacity tier corresponds to the highest appliance
tier reached by the appliances (in working condition) owned by the entity.


**Generator and rechargeable battery** : Capacity corresponds to the highest Appliance tier reached by
the appliances (in working condition) owned by the household.


**Solar home system** : Capacity is by default Tier 2, unless the household owns appliances that reach an
Appliance tier above Tier 2, in which case the Capacity tier will correspond to the highest Appliance
tier reached by the appliances (in working condition) owned by the household.


**Solar lantern and solar lighting system** : Capacity tier is Tier 0 or Tier 1, depending on a score calculated

Once the raster data were acquired, they were imported into ArcMap, a geographic information system
(GIS) software. To narrow the focus to Burundi, the global raster data was clipped or delineated to match
the country’s administrative boundaries. The vector data, representing the provinces and “ _collines_ ” of
Burundi, was also downloaded and added into ArcMap (OCHA 2017). Adjustments were made to ensure
that the projection of both raster and vector data aligned seamlessly. Additionally, new columns, such
as ID_cty (numerical codes for provinces) and ID_admin (numerical codes for the lowest administrative
units), were added to the vector data set for processing in R using the spatial algorithm.


After preprocessing in ArcMap, the raster and vector data sets were exported for further processing
in R software. R is employed to implement an algorithm designed to generate PSUs. These PSUs are
delineated based on the administrative unit boundary (such as _colline_ or _commune_ ) used in the
administration vector data, with an average number of households of 241 per unit. However, to account
for areas with high population density, such as cities and urban regions, additional steps were taken.
In these areas, the raster data for areas with high population density were clipped and resampled
(changing the resolution of the raster data set) by “downsampling” [38] to ensure that the generated units
aligned more accurately with the projected population size, maintaining the representativeness of the
sampling process.


Generally, Burundi PSUs’ generation involved raster and vector data acquisition, preprocessing in ArcMap
or QGIS to align and enhance the data sets, and further algorithmic processing in R to generate PSUs.
The inclusion of population density information from the raster data and the adjustment of vector data
based on administrative boundaries contribute to a more accurate and representative sampling approach
for Burundi. However, this process can lead to data inaccuracies, particularly in densely populated
areas where resampling may cause the loss of pixel values. This issue is particularly pronounced in
urban areas with high population density, where pixels with both high and low values coexist. When
adjusting high-value pixels, low-value pixels are often downscaled, leading to data loss. Additionally,
the resampling process operates on cumulative pixels within the area of interest, resulting in most
PSUs having the target values while a few are negatively impacted.

**Step 3: Additional verification by the REGIDESO team**


The finalized list was sent to ABER and REGIDESO for verification using _colline_ names. Collines, the
second-smallest administrative unit (Hijmans 2015), were used as a reference point. [40] Given that an
EA can encompass multiple _sous-collines_, _collines_ were employed to facilitate a more straightforward
classification of the EAs by the REGIDESO and ABER teams.


By utilizing the coordinates coupled with the _colline_ data for each EA, the teams were able to verify
63 EAs that necessitated additional verification (37) or were incorrectly classified (26). The resulting
classification yielded 175 electrified EAs and 167 nonelectrified EAs. This represents a relatively high
number of electrified EAs in Burundi, where, given the low electrification rate of 11 percent, one would
anticipate a lower number of electrified EAs. The elevated number of electrified EAs can be attributed
to the high number of urban EAs (129/171) randomly selected from Bujumbura Mairie Province, which
is characterized by a high level of electrification.


**HOUSEHOLD LISTING**


Initially, the classification between electrified and nonelectrified EAs was conducted utilizing night light
satellite imagery (VIIRS DNB) [41] obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, [42]
resulting in the identification of 65 electrified EAs and 277 nonelectrified EAs. Subsequently, a more
rigorous process (as outlined in the previous section) was implemented to reclassify the EAs, yielding
a revised ratio of 174 electrified EAs (representing an additional 109 electrified EAs compared to the
initial 65 EAs) versus 168 nonelectrified EAs.


This large difference between the two methods can be explained by the limitations of satellite imagery,
especially in regions with low-quality electricity services and small EAs. Satellite images have a relatively
low resolution and can be affected by factors like cloud cover, moonlight, and missing data. Additionally,
satellite images may not be able to detect interior lighting, which can lead to an underestimation of
electrification, especially in areas with intermittent power supply. In the case of Bujumbura Mairie, where
many EAs are quite small, the low resolution of satellite images may not be able to accurately capture
the presence of electricity. Ultimately, using actual ground-based data on transformers, combined with
information from utility workers, provided a more accurate estimate of electrification in the region.
