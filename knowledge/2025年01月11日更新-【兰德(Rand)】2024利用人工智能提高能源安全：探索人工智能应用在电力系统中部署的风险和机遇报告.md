#### EXECUTIVE SUMMARY


**Background and context**


Electricity systems around the world are
under pressure due to aging infrastructure,
rising demand for electricity and the need to
decarbonise our energy supplies at pace. AI
applications help address these pressures
and increase overall energy security. For
example, AI applications can reduce peak
demand through demand response, improve
the efficiency of wind farms and facilitate
the integration of large numbers of electric
vehicles into the power grid. However, the
widespread deployment of AI applications
could also come with heightened cybersecurity
risks, the risk of unexplained or unexpected
actions, or supplier dependency and vendor
lock-in. The speed at which AI is developing
means many of these opportunities and risks
are not yet well understood.

This technical report accompanies the main
policy report titled ‘The Use of AI for Improving
Energy Security’ (RR-A2907- ). It describes the
methods, tools and results of our quantitative
exploration of the impact of AI-based
applications on the energy security of the
European power grid.


**Study objectives and research**
**approach**


The objective of this study was to assess the
current state of AI applications for the power
grid and their impact on various energy security
metrics. To do this, we used PyPSA-Eur, a
Python-based power system model, to explore
the extent to which different AI applications
can improve energy security under different AI



deployment scenarios. Our research focused
on the European energy system and the
short term (less than five years), assuming
a static power grid topology and considering
AI applications at deployment readiness level
(TRL 8 and 9).

We developed quantitative methods to evaluate
the performance of AI on energy security
on alternative scenarios of AI deployment
in the European power grid. To measure
energy security attributes for each scenario,
we examined four key dimensions of energy
security: availability (ensuring sufficient supply
reserve to meet demand), affordability (average
and marginal cost of electricity), accessibility
(determining whether electricity generation
is relying on imported fossil fuel) and
acceptability (evaluating the carbon emissions
of the electricity).

This technical report provides quantitative
evidence for the impact of AI on energy
security and translates the evidence into
policy insights on the role of AI in the power
grid. Our research also provides observations
and lessons learned on the advantages and
challenges of using open-source modelling for
research on power grids.


**Primary findings**


We evaluated the impact of three AI
applications across four scenarios: AI-driven
load reduction, AI-driven load shifting, AI-driven
wind wake steering, and all three applications
combined. Comparing these scenarios to a
baseline without AI applications, we found
that AI-driven load reduction provided benefits
across all energy security metrics, ranging

Our analysis found that each AI scenario improved at least one energy security metric, but
the magnitude varied and trade-offs between countries and different metrics were observed.
AI-supporting policies should align with the security metric of greater saliency. The impact
of AI applications may depend on energy market rules and market incentives. Policymakers
should consider AI’s role in current policy discussions on market restructuring or design.
Implementing different AI applications together could have effects on energy security.
Policymakers should recognise that AI’s impact on energy security is not necessarily additive
and carefully consider interactions between applications to avoid adverse impacts.

Load-reducing behind-the-meter (BTM) AI applications have significant positive impacts on all
four energy security metrics. Policymakers should incentivise the use of AI heating, ventilation
and air conditioning (HVAC) control technology for buildings, smart metering, virtual power
plants, and distributed energy resources to improve energy security.

## ENERGY SECURITY

Energy is an essential requirement of all
economic activity. Secure access to energy
has therefore always been a core government
concern. Because of its critical nature, energy
security has been well researched in academia,
government and the private sector. [8] However,
there is no consensus on the definition of
energy security.


**Definition of energy security**


In this technical report, we follow the ‘four As’
definition of energy security developed by the
Asia Pacific Energy Research Centre. [9] The four
As are availability, accessibility, affordability
and acceptability. We do not create a single
compounded index to measure ‘energy
security’ as identifying potential weights for
each attribute is out of scope. Instead, we
intend to measure the impact of AI on each
attribute and identify possible trade-offs. Each
attribute is defined below:

**•** **Availability** means that the energy required
to fulfil the needs of the economy and
society is available at all times.

**•** **Affordability** relates to the price of the
energy provided.

**•** **Accessibility** deals with sourcing and
transporting energy supplies, and the
geopolitical challenges this may entail.

**•** **Acceptability** deals with environmental
issues and debates around sustainability. [0]

## **AI IN E ERGY**

AI is an umbrella term for a wide variety of
statistical and algorithmic techniques. It can
be used to draw out trends from large volumes
of data, control processes, make decisions or
predictions, and interact with its environment
based on this data. One of the most widely
used forms of AI is machine learning (ML). ML
involves the use of algorithms and statistical
models to enable a system to improve its
performance on a specific task over time.
ML algorithms are designed to learn from
data, identify patterns and relationships and
make predictions or decisions without being
explicitly programmed to do so. This allows
ML systems to adapt and improve their
performance on a given task as they receive
more data and experiences.


**Potential of AI for energy systems**


In this study, we quantify the AI impact
on energy security using a power system
optimisation tool. There are great opportunities
for AI in the electric grid, where information
exchange is essential to achieving near
instantaneous production, delivery and
consumption. [4] However, the potential of AI
applications for power-system-wide energy
security benefits are largely unknown. Most
literature quantifies the impact at the facility
or AI application level, such as bill and energy
savings. There is limited understanding in the
literature of the extent to which AI can benefit
our energy system when broadly deployed.
This is partly due to the complexity of the
power system and uncertainties surrounding


4 Entezari et al. (2023).



both the development of AI and the power
systems themselves. Our study does not aim
to solve these uncertainties by projecting
or predicting the future system impact of AI
applications. Rather, we attempt to showcase
a modelling approach to quantitatively explore
AI applications’ impact and present the
magnitude and potential trade-offs of different
impacts. With the lessons learned from this
exploratory exercise, we identify caveated,
yet important, policy implications of AI
implementation in the energy system.


**AI applications for energy**


The electricity system consists of a plethora
of control elements. These are increasingly
connected to the internet, creating a network
of connected nodes, each of which generates
data that can be shared, collected and
analysed. There are major benefits to this
digitalisation, as measurements made by
individual components can be shared and
interpreted (e.g. by AI), leading to significant
insights into the operation of the electricity
system. Those insights may help improve the
efficiency of the system and reduce costs.
Real-time measurements can also be used to
pinpoint anomalies in the grid.

As AI technology has progressed, researchers
began investigating how AI techniques could
be applied to energy systems. Research on the
topic began to pick up in the early 2000s, but
there has been an explosion of research in the

**Modelling limitations**


There are some limitations to our approach
largely due to the following constraints on
computational resources and publicly available
data:

. We used a deterministic modelling

approach, rather than a stochastic
approach, due to the computational
capability of the solver [6] available to
us. This means our analysis was done
under a perfect foresight approach with
no uncertainty. AI implementations
that can reduce uncertainty – such as
improvements in forecasting accuracy –
were therefore not considered.

2. We used the open source PyPSA-Eur [7]
model to construct a European electricity
system that includes generation, storage,
demand and transmission infrastructures
from open data. Modelling the European
system requires the curation of large
amounts of data from different countries.
We limited our analysis to demand and
weather for the coldest week of the year
20 3, for which data had already been
curated by the developers. We did not have
the resources to curate any additional years’
demand/weather for our simulations.

3. We used the network topology provided by
the latest update from the developers (year
2020). We did not have resources available
to update the model topology ourselves.
Therefore, our simulation exposed the
European grid topology as from 2020 to the
coldest weather in 20 3 assuming different
AI deployments. We believe this exercise
is still useful for two reasons. First, 20 3



was an extremely cold winter. Second,
changes in power grid topology (e.g. new
transmission lines) take several years to
be implemented, and so we consider 2020
topology to be a good approximation to the
current set-up.

4. We focused on a short-term horizon, with
the configuration of the grid as given.
While there could be more opportunities
with AI to enhance the energy system
and lead to changes in configuration/
topology, a long-term timeframe introduces
more uncertainty and new infrastructure
buildouts. We limited new transmission
and capacity expansion in our modelling
and optimised dispatch of existing
resources only. Therefore, we considered
only AI applications at TRL 8 or 9 (rather
than earlier readiness levels).

5. Our model approach also assumed a
high level of central coordination when
optimising the European electric system
to minimise costs given the constraints
of meeting demand with supply. In
reality, the European electric market is
more ‘decentralised’ – meaning there is
no central place to bid and clear energy
dispatch. [8] European markets are covered
by several power exchanges that operate
with different market rules, products (i.e.
day-ahead, intra-day) and mechanisms (i.e.
capacity), hindering central coordination. [9]

Due to these complexities and limitations in
model representation, our study focused on
investigating changes – and the direction of
changes – from the benchmark, rather than the
model results themselves.

**Scenario 3 (S3): Wind wake**
**steering**


Assumptions and input parameters

In this scenario, we consider the IFOM impacts
AI-driven applications could produce – for
instance on the operation of wind energy
resources. Wind turbine wakes result in
significant energy loss during generation for
the downstream turbines due to reduced wind
speed. The magnitude of power loss due to
the wake effect varies depending on the layout
pattern and distance between turbines, but the
literature reports that it could be as much as
23%. [44] Wake steering is a control strategy that
misaligns wind turbines with wind direction,
deflecting the wakes away from downstream
turbines and improving the performance of wind



farms. Companies such as Vestas have tested
ML- and AI-based algorithms for optimal wake
steering in actual power generation, resulting
in performance improving by up to 3% and
energy production improving by 3% to 5%.
Meanwhile, the US National Renewable Energy
Laboratory (NREL) reports a 3% gain in annual
energy production from its simulation tool. [45]

We modified PyPSA-Eur parameters to
reflect 3% improvements in wind-farm output
from wake steering for both onshore and
offshore wind. PyPSA-Eur utilises a parameter
called a correction factor to account for the
wake effects and array efficiency. A default
correction factor value of 0.8855 was used
based on a relevant study. [46] We used a
correction factor of 0.9 2 to reflect our
assumption of a 3% improvement from AI/ML
wake steering technology.

## POLICY IMPLICATIONS AND FURTHER RESEARCH



Drawing on the findings and discussion,
we have identified a number of insights
for policymakers in Europe and beyond
when considering potential system-wide AI
applications in energy. We also propose several

**Policy insights**


Our analysis of energy security metrics offers
valuable insights into the performance of
different AI expansion scenarios compared
to a status quo benchmark. It underscores
the positive impact of strategies such as
AI-driven load reduction across multiple energy
metrics. However, it also emphasises the need
for careful consideration of the impact of AI
applications across different countries and
across different security metrics. We present
below a set of potential policy implications for
AI on the ENTSO-E grid.


Each of the AI scenarios leads to
improvement in at least one of the
energy security metrics, although the
magnitude varies between scenarios
different metrics. Our study implies that
some AI deployments generate tradeoffs between energy security metrics.
For instance, in S2 (load shifting), AI
implementation led to improvements
in availability and affordability but not
accessibility and acceptability. This implies
that polices supporting AI should align
with the security metric of greater saliency
(accessibility, availability, acceptability,
affordability) for the policymaker.

**Future research opportunities**


We identified several major opportunities that
can be built upon the research conducted using
PyPSA. The identified expansions are as follows:


**Expanding the time horizon to long-term future:** Expanding the time horizon
from short-term (<5 years) to long-term (>5 years) will allow for the inclusion of AI
technologies at a lower level of technological readiness than those considered in
this research.

**Expanding the scope to include other energy sectors:** Expanding the scope to
include other energy sectors such as primary fuel sources and transportation
would help to address a broad energy system question, such as navigating through
climate policy and energy system transition. The PyPSA tool already has these
features that can be adopted without extensive time or resources.

**Testing for a longer period time:** We were only able to assess the energy system for

intermittent renewable energy whose generation may vary and be uncertain based on
weather conditions.

**Allowing additional levels of granularity in the optimisation:** Given computational
constraints, the research was limited to a one-node-per-country for most countries.
This limitation was due to the use of a solver that, although available at no cost,
has limited power. Additional funds can be allocated to allow for additional levels of
granularity in the optimisation, which will help to assess more distributional impact
and better represent the actual system.

## OPEN-SOURCE MODEL DISCUSSION

One of the ancillary objectives of this research
was to gain experience with open-source
power flow modelling. In this section, we share
some of the insights the team gained from this
exercise that can guide future deployment of AI
in the electricity system.


**Benefits and challenges**


In recent years, open-source tools and open
data have become increasingly popular in
clean energy transition research, due to



improved computing capabilities and the need
for lower-cost entry solutions for non-industry
use cases, such as for researchers, policy
analysts and others.

PyPSA is a mature open-source tools and has
functions comparable with commercial and
proprietary tools. [5] It is intuitive and easy to
learn. However, during our use of PyPSA, we
encountered several issues that impeded our
progress in conducting research.



**Operating system issues with installation:** The first issue we encountered was
related to the installation process. The core function and its dependencies require a
Linux system and do not work with Windows. This presented a challenge for us, as
we were using Windows, and had to switch to Linux to use PyPSA. This resulted in
additional time and effort being expended on the installation process.

**De-bugging and error handling:** We found that de-bugging and handling errors
were not intuitive and required us to rely on open discussion forums for answers.
This process was unreliable as we sometimes received answers in a matter of days,
but at other times we did not receive any response at all. Having to wait to resolve
errors before we could continue our work slowed the progress of our research.

**Deviating from basic set-up:** Another issue we encountered was that deviating
from the basic set-up, such as choosing a different year to study, required additional
processes and runtime. We often faced errors that prevented us from completing
the run. As a result, we had to change the plan and limit the project scope to the
data and set-up available to us.

**Frequent updates:** We found that PyPSA had frequent updates, with a new version
release almost every month. When there was a major update to the tool, we had
to configure the tool again, as a significant amount of the workflow was modified
or merged with other functions. This resulted in additional time and effort spent
re-doing work we had already completed.

**Lessons learned**


Our experience using PyPSA revealed several
issues, such as difficulties with installation,
error handling and deviations from the basic
set-up, as well as frequent updates that
required us to redo work. Through our PyPSA
experience, we learned the inherent trade-offs
between using open-source and commercial
tools. While open-source tools like PyPSA offer
extensive capabilities and cost-effectiveness,
they can also present unique challenges
such as system-specific requirements, less

necessitate reconfiguration. Our experience
