# **Abstract**

Using a fair-value approach, the Congressional Budget Office estimates the cost of increasing the
United States’ financial commitment to the International Monetary Fund (IMF). Although
countries default much less often on the IMF than on other lenders, the fund still carries credit
risk. By design, the IMF lends to countries experiencing significant macroeconomic challenges.
Private markets often avoid those borrowers. Even when borrowing countries eventually repay
loans that fall into arrears, the IMF still experiences a financial burden. The fund passes some
costs of such defaults to members through a “burden sharing” mechanism that reduces the
income that the United States and other creditors receive on their contributions. In addition, the
United States has historically offered further support to help countries resolve their overdue IMF
debts. CBO estimates the U.S. cost under current law by incorporating average default costs and
market risk—which differs from the Office of Management and Budget’s assumption of
negligible risk. For the U.S. contribution’s permanent component, the net present value cost is
the discounted total of those annual costs in perpetuity. Results suggest that the fair-value cost
for the United States is about 1.36 percent of its commitment to the IMF. That finding represents
a one-time expense to cover the cost of the commitment in perpetuity. Sensitivity analyses show
that factors such as borrowers’ income levels and the choice of discount rate affect the estimates.
The IMF’s preferred creditor status, which effectively brings about a three- to four-notch credit
rating uplift, helps keep overall risk relatively low.

## **Introduction**

This paper describes how the Congressional Budget Office estimates the budgetary cost of the
United States’ commitment to contribute more financial resources to the International Monetary
Fund (IMF). The IMF is a global financial organization with 191 member countries, tasked with
promoting financial stability and addressing balance-of-payments crises. Member contributions
and lines of credit support the fund’s activities. As the IMF’s largest contributor, the United
States has a total current commitment of about $192 billion, including funds already contributed
and a standby commitment to supply more funds up to the total commitment when needed.


The IMF faces credit risk and passes costs on to member countries when countries borrowing
from the fund fall into arrears on their obligations. The IMF benefits from its status as a preferred
creditor. That is, borrowers typically make payments on IMF loans before any other obligations
to private lenders or to other countries. Sometimes, though, borrowers do not make their
scheduled payments on time, a situation referred to here as either a default or going into arrears.
Borrowers almost always eventually repay the IMF, often with help from other countries,
including the United States—that is why a “default” in the context of the IMF is akin to arrears
for a private borrower and lender. However, the fund still faces credit risk and passes the cost of
default on to members through a “burden sharing” mechanism, which reduces the remuneration
that IMF contributors receive. Throughout history, the United States has borne additional costs—
direct financial assistance and loans—to help countries settle their overdue debts with the IMF.


As required by statute, CBO projects the cost of the U.S. commitment to the IMF under current
law by using a fair-value approach, which includes the estimate of the compensation private
investors would require to take on the risk of that commitment. The agency’s approach differs
from that of the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), which uses an “exchange of assets”
view. That approach reflects an assumption of negligible risk for IMF commitments and ignores
that the cost of defaulting on IMF loans is passed through to the United States. CBO’s estimate
reflects the cost of U.S. participation in the General Resources Account (GRA), the main source
of financing for the fund’s lending activities and the part of the commitment that carries credit
risk. The agency’s estimate does not include the cost of other IMF activities, such as
concessional lending through the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT). U.S.
contributions to those other activities such as the PRGT are made through specific appropriations
and have totaled $122 million in the last 10 years. The IMF also has funded programs by selling
its gold holdings, an action that in the past has been undertaken with Congressional approval. [1]


In CBO’s estimates, the fair-value cost is about 1.36 percent of the funds that the United States
contributes or commits to contributing to the fund. That amount is equivalent to an up-front cost
that covers the commitment in perpetuity. (The U.S. commitment to the IMF through a quota is

**Background on the IMF**
Since the IMF was established in 1944, it has sought to promote financial stability among its 191
member countries, including the United States. Through its various lending programs, the fund
offers financial assistance to countries facing economic crises, particularly balance-of-payments
problems. Those programs offer countries financing at lower interest rates than what private
investors would charge, and the lending is conditional on implementing economic reforms to
address the causes of financial instability and restore economic confidence. Although those
reforms aim to improve borrowing nations’ economic performance, countries typically borrow
from the fund when facing difficult economic circumstances. By design, the IMF lends to
counterparties that often have no other source of credit.


The IMF’s main lending programs are funded through the GRA, financed mainly by member
countries’ quotas (financial commitments), and the PRGT, separately financed by donor
contributions. The GRA offers member nations nonconcessional lending, whereas the PRGT
offers low-income countries concessional loans with below-market interest rates and longer
repayment periods. PRGT loans do not rely on quota-based financing, and their cost structure
differs from that of GRA loans. The IMF also manages other lending programs, including the
Resilience and Sustainability Trust, which supports long-term structural reforms.


In 2009, the IMF shifted most lending to low-income countries from the GRA to the PRGT,
whereas the GRA remains the main financing mechanism for middle- and high-income
countries. That distinction is important for this paper’s analysis because borrowing countries
using the PRGT are riskier than those with GRA-funded loans. The analysis focuses on the GRA
because its lending performance determines how much compensation the United States receives
for contributing more resources to the IMF. However, sections discussing IMF financial risk,
arrears, and the overall structure of burden-sharing adjustments refer to PRGT lending.


The quota-based GRA system is the foundation of the IMF’s financial structure. Countries
deposit one-quarter of their quota with the fund and pledge the remaining three-quarters as a
promissory note. The quota is measured in special drawing rights (SDRs), the IMF’s unit of
account. The SDR’s value is based on a basket of five currencies: the U.S. dollar, euro, Chinese
renminbi, Japanese yen, and British pound sterling. As of February 17, 2026, the SDR is worth
about $1.377.

The IMF can increase its lending capacity by drawing on two supplementary credit lines, the

Although all member countries participate in the quota system, only some, including the United

The United States has been the fund’s largest contributor, with a total commitment as of
February 2026 of about $192 billion, consisting of $114 billion in quotas and $78 billion through
the NAB (see Figure 1). Those commitments have evolved. In response to the 2008 financial
crisis, the G-20 agreed to expand IMF resources, and in 2009 the United States authorized a

reduced the U.S. NAB obligation by roughly the same amount.


**Lending Arrangements.** The IMF offers several lending arrangements. GRA-financed StandBy Arrangements offer short-term financial support to countries facing immediate liquidity
needs and help stabilize their economies while their governments address fiscal and monetary
imbalances. For countries with deeper, structural problems, the Extended Fund Facility, also
under the GRA, offers longer-term support, allowing countries to tackle systemic weaknesses
through economic reforms.


7


For low-income countries, the PRGT-financed Extended Credit Facility, Stand-By Credit
Facility, and Rapid Credit Facility offer concessional lending with various policy conditions and
repayment flexibility. The Resilience and Sustainability Facility, financed separately through the
Resilience and Sustainability Trust, supports long-term structural reforms related to climate
change, public health, and other global challenges.


**Lending Conditions.** IMF lending is conditional on economic reforms for borrowing countries.
For example, countries may have to implement policies that address fiscal deficits, improve tax
collection, manage public expenditures, control inflation, and enhance governance. Those policy
conditions are meant to lead countries toward improved stability and growth, enabling them to
eventually meet their financial obligations. The IMF also offers technical assistance to improve
countries’ public financial management systems—such as budgeting and resource allocation—
and strengthen institutional frameworks to enhance governance and accountability.


**IMF’s Decision-Making Process.** The fund’s decision-making process is closely tied to its
financial structure. Voting power is based on the size of each member’s quota, the main
component of its financial commitment to the fund. With the largest quota, the United States
holds substantial voting power, including a veto over major IMF decisions, such as changes to
quotas or the authorization to sell the IMF’s gold reserves. Every five years, the fund reviews its
quota system to allocate contributions on the basis of a range of factors, including the size of
members’ economies. The most recent adjustment, agreed upon in 2010 and implemented in
2016, nearly doubled the IMF’s total quotas to about $656 billion, making more resources
available to lend. Each member government must ratify changes in the quota system. For the
United States, ratification involves enacting legislation to make the resources available.


**U.S. Exposure to IMF Credit Losses**
The IMF’s preferred creditor status enhances the fund’s ability to recover funds and reduces risk
of loss but does not eliminate that risk. [2] Many borrowing countries have missed scheduled
payments. For example, prolonged episodes of arrears have occurred in Sudan and Zimbabwe.


Under the burden-sharing mechanism, when a borrowing country defaults, the IMF recoups the
resulting cost in two ways. The fund reduces the remuneration it pays to contributing countries
and raises the interest rate paid for future borrowing. In some years, burden-sharing adjustments
have reduced the rate of remuneration to the United States by as much as 1 percentage point out
of a remuneration rate that has varied from zero to just over 9 percent since 1984. The largest
burden-sharing adjustment since 1984 occurred in July 1990, reducing remuneration rates by
nearly 2 percentage points (see Figure 2).

When countries in arrears eventually become current on IMF payments—usually with help from
the United States or other member nations—the fund reimburses the United States and other
members for the withheld remuneration, albeit without interest. Because those reimbursements
come years or sometimes decades after the burden sharing occurs, the United States suffers a
financial loss. The government must then borrow more from the public, resulting in an interest
cost that is not recovered when the fund reimburses the forgone remuneration without interest.


Adjustments to SDR rates paid to IMF creditor countries such as the United States reduced that
compensation by about one-tenth, from 3.2 percent on average since 1984 to 2.9 percent after the
adjustments. Thus, 10 percent of remuneration has historically been delayed and eventually
reimbursed without interest. With the value of money and lack of interest factored in, those
reimbursements lost about one-third of their value.


**Budgetary Treatment of the U.S. Commitment to the IMF**
The Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2009 (P.L. 111-32), requires CBO to calculate the
budgetary effect of the U.S. commitment to the IMF on a fair-value basis, as does the 2016


9


Consolidated Appropriations Act. [3] The fair-value approach uses market prices to measure the
cost of a government activity by estimating the compensation a private investor would require to
take on the risks borne by the taxpayer. Under fair-value budgeting, the agency estimates costs in
the budget as present values when the commitment is made rather than when cash flows occur.


**Fair-Value Approach.** The fair-value cost includes both the expected cost of default—the value
projected to occur on average—and the cost of market risk. The risk arises from shifts in
macroeconomic conditions, such as productivity and employment, and from changes in
expectations about those conditions. The government is exposed to market risk because, in a
weak economy, borrowers default on debt obligations more often, with lower recoveries from
borrowers. When the government extends credit, the market risk of those obligations is
effectively passed along to taxpayers and beneficiaries of government programs who, in
economic terms, are treated as investors and would view that risk as having a cost.


**Alternative Approaches.** OMB’s approach to budgeting for the IMF differs from that of CBO,
taking an exchange-of-assets view, under which the risk of loss on the U.S. commitment is
deemed negligible and no losses are recorded. When the United States buys SDRs from the IMF
with U.S. dollars, OMB treats that transaction as an exchange of assets of equal value. That
approach omits the cost to the United States of delayed payments (defaults) on IMF loans that is
passed through to the United States without compensation. Because OMB’s estimates serve as
official records of the deficit, CBO uses them to record the cost of commitments already made.
However, in CBO’s assessment, the risk of loss is not negligible, and the agency applies the fairvalue approach in its cost estimates of proposed legislation that would contribute more resources
to the fund.

government, with cash flows between the Treasury and the fund considered intragovernmental
cash flows. That approach focuses on the unlikely event of dissolving the IMF and distributing
its assets to members. [5] Because the United States does not effectively control the IMF in the

That alternative approach would have implications for what is reflected in the budget. Under
CBO’s current approach, the agency records IMF’s credit losses as costs because they are passed
on to the U.S. Treasury through burden sharing. Under the alternative approach, the United
States’ share of the IMF’s credit losses would be estimated as a cost regardless of whether the
losses result in burden-sharing adjustments to Treasury receipts. Under CBO’s current approach,
an appreciation in the value of the IMF’s gold holdings does not in itself represent budgetary
savings. Such appreciation would result in savings only if the gold caused the IMF to pause
burden sharing on the view that the fund has enough resources to sustain losses without it. (So
far, the IMF has not taken that view in response to recent increases in the value of gold.) Under
the alternative approach, a substantial increase in the market value of the fund’s gold holdings
would create savings for the budget.


**Current-Law Baseline.** By statute, CBO estimates the cost of proposed legislation under a
current-law measure under which the budgetary cost includes only measures specified in law and
excludes the effects of any future legislative changes. Past Congressional action has affected the
cost of the U.S. commitment to the IMF by authorizing more spending or allowing the sale of
IMF gold reserves to help countries resolve their arrears. Once countries address their arrears,
they reimburse the IMF, and the fund compensates the United States for prior burden-sharing
adjustments, albeit without interest. A current-law measure of the U.S commitment to the IMF
considers the cost in a situation in which the United States would offer no economic assistance to
IMF borrowers beyond what lawmakers had already authorized. That measure would rule out a
situation such as Sudan’s 2021 clearance of IMF arrears, in which the United States furnished a
bridge loan and waived reimbursement of burden-sharing losses under authority granted in the
Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 (P.L. 116-260). That assistance required new legislation
and is thus unavailable under current law. Because the United States has veto power over major
IMF decisions, the fund is presumed to operate within the limitations of Congressional
authorization. An implication is that lawmakers would need to enable the fund to sell some of its
gold reserves to cover losses or finance new initiatives. The IMF’s gold holdings are thus not
part of the analytic framework in this report.


**Macroeconomic Feedback.** The IMF’s activities may reduce the likelihood and severity of
global economic and financial crises and avoid their spilling over into the U.S. economy. Those
economic effects of the IMF’s activities have budgetary consequences beyond this paper’s scope.
CBO’s estimates for the fund have generally not included macroeconomic feedback, also known
as dynamic effects. Those effects might arise, for example, if contributing resources to the IMF
resulted in a stabler world economy, and that in turn led to increased U.S. economic growth that
fed back into the budget.

**Related Literature**
This paper updates CBO’s previous report on the cost of the U.S. commitment to the IMF,
drawing on research conducted by the U.S. Treasury, credit rating agencies, academic
researchers, and the IMF.


**CBO’s 2016 Publication.** The estimates in the agency’s 2016 report were based on assessments
about the potential for a catastrophic scenario for the IMF: one that could occur but had never
been observed. [6] This paper updates that approach with estimates based on actual experience with
IMF losses and the rates at which those losses were passed through to the United States. Though
the two approaches lead to similar estimates for the cost of the U.S. commitment to the fund,
CBO’s revised approach reflects the recognition that protracted arrears, though relatively
uncommon, are part of the fund’s normal operations. The IMF has procedures for addressing
arrears, and the United States incurs costs in such cases. This report estimates the budgetary
effects under current law.


**Reports From the U.S. Treasury.** Each year, the Treasury reports to lawmakers on the financial
effects of U.S. participation in the IMF, including how participating affects the U.S. Treasury’s
net interest costs. [7] That report also emphasizes the fund’s role in promoting global economic
stability and supporting U.S. economic interests. This paper builds on the Treasury’s findings by
incorporating a broader data set that examines trends further in the past, to include episodes of
protracted arrears and burden sharing. [8] By doing so, CBO’s analysis offers a complementary
perspective, focusing on realized costs and long-term financial risks associated with U.S.
participation in the IMF.

several multinational organizations that enjoy preferred creditor status. Others include the World
Bank and development banks such as the African Development Bank, which lend to low- and
middle-income countries to promote economic development. Borrowers have given those
organizations preferred treatment in repayment, even though no preference exists for them in
law. [9] Although credit rating agencies such as Fitch and Standard & Poor’s assign credit ratings to

The **lending rate** measures the share of IMF commitments that are on average lent out in a year
and thus subject to credit risk. The rate is the ratio of the dollar amount of IMF loans outstanding
divided by the total amount of commitments for all IMF members.


The **credit default swap (CDS) spread** measures the risk of the fund’s borrowers, without yet
considering the effect of the IMF’s preferred creditor status. CDSs insure investors against the
cost of default on countries’ sovereign bonds, and the spreads measure the premium paid for that
insurance. Thus, the CDS spread measures the cost of the fund’s exposure in a hypothetical
situation in which the IMF holds a portfolio of bonds for the same countries that borrow from it
through the GRA. However, because the cost is lower owing to the IMF’s status as a preferred
creditor, CBO applies an **adjustment for preferred creditor status** . Specifically, the agency
considers that the fund experiences a much lower default rate than a private creditor with the
same borrowers. Losses are smaller when borrowers default on the IMF than on other creditors,
and so the fund experiences larger recoveries. A default for the IMF is typically a prolonged
period in which a borrower does not make payments, as opposed to the meaning in the private
sector, in which a borrower does not pay a debt, such as with a home’s going into foreclosure
and being seized by the lender.


CBO applies two more adjustments to estimate the cost of the IMF’s commitments. The fund
tends to lend in situations in which the cost of market risk is relatively high. Therefore, the
agency applies an **adjustment for correlation between the lending rate and the CDS spread**
to capture the fact that the IMF lends in scenarios of higher-than-average risk. Risky situations
with a high CDS spread are precisely the kind for which countries tend to look to the IMF for
financing. A second adjustment, the **adjustment for U.S. burden share**, equals the U.S. share of
default costs in relation to the U.S. share of contributions to the IMF.

**Interest Rate Spread on Credit Default Swaps**
To estimate the risk of the IMF’s portfolio, CBO uses data from Capital IQ for CDSs and credit
ratings, and from the IMF’s financial reports for IMF lending. [14] CDSs insure creditors against
the cost of default. In a CDS contract, one party agrees to pay a regular fee to a second party that
acts as an insurer against the cost of default on a specific debt, such as a sovereign bond. When a
credit event such as a missed payment occurs on that bond, the party receiving the regular fee
must make a payment equal to the loss that occurs because of the default. That loss is calculated
as the par value of the debt minus its postdefault value.


CDS spreads—the annual fee paid for protection against the cost of default—capture the same
elements of cost that analysts often use yield spreads to measure. Yield spreads are the difference
between the interest rate on a bond and a risk-free benchmark—for example, the difference
between the interest rate on a corporate bond issued in U.S. dollars and the U.S. Treasury rate. In
other contexts, CBO uses yield spreads of corporate bonds to measure the cost of default of
commercial loans on a fair-value basis, measuring them as the rate charged to private borrowers
minus a benchmark rate that market participants perceive to be risk free. [15] Yield spreads
comprise three main components: a premium for the expected cost of default, a premium for the
cost of market risk, and a liquidity premium to compensate the bondholder for the risk of being
unable to sell the bond in a timely manner. CDS spreads serve a similar purpose and eliminate
the need to identify risk-free benchmarks across currencies and, usually, carry smaller liquidity
premiums because CDS contracts require no up-front investment.


CDS spreads have some advantages over yield spreads for evaluating the cost of IMF lending. First,
CDS spreads are a uniform measure of cost for borrowers in different currencies. Yield spreads are
available only for countries that borrow in a currency, such as the U.S. dollar or euro, for which it is
possible to identify a risk-free benchmark. Second, CDS spreads probably include a smaller liquidity
premium than bond yields because a party to a CDS contract does not need to make an up-front
investment that would raise concerns about selling that investment at a market price in a timely
manner. Finally, CDS spreads are not influenced by bond features such as call options that affect
their yield spread (because bond purchasers would require higher yields as compensation for the
presence of the call option). [16] The average CDS spread was 1,040 basis points from 2004 to 2024,
weighted in each year by the amount of lending to each country. [17]

Economic Support Fund, but only after lawmakers had appropriated new funds through
supplemental or annual legislation. [29] Because CBO calculates the budgetary effect with respect
to current law, for an evaluation of legislation that gives resources to the IMF, the agency’s
projections would consider neither the cost nor the effect of legislation authorizing future
assistance. The budgetary effect of any such action would be the domain of that future
legislation—and the effect of such future assistance would not reduce the budgetary effect of
additional resources for the IMF given under current law. As a result, this analysis of the U.S.
commitment to the IMF does not incorporate the cost of any other forms of future financial
assistance to countries that borrow from the fund.


The six categories for clearing of arrears serve as a framework for understanding the financial
exposures resulting from different strategies. Some cases may fit into more than one category. In
such instances, the agency assigned the case to the category representing the greatest financial
burden to the United States. CBO defines each category for clearing arrears as follows:


- **Forgiven.** The United States waives reimbursements that it would receive under IMF

burden-sharing arrangements as part of broader debt relief initiatives. Such decisions are
often aligned with international debt reduction efforts, including the Heavily Indebted Poor
Countries Initiative. For example, Sudan’s arrears were addressed through debt relief, with
the United States forgoing expected reimbursements to support the shared goal of easing the
countries’ overall debt burdens. Given the forfeiture of all funds otherwise due, a haircut of
1.0 indicates full financial exposure for the United States. In such cases, the United States has
typically required statutory authority to forgo reimbursements, as for Sudan in 2021 through
legislation authorizing the U.S. Treasury to waive amounts owed under the IMF’s burdensharing mechanism.


- **U.S. aid.** U.S. assistance, usually grants, is used directly to clear IMF arrears. Although the

fund may formally reimburse the United States for its burden-sharing contributions, the

- **IMF lending support.** The fund independently offers concessional financing through PRGT

Although not reducing the United States’ remuneration from the fund, that assistance reflects
a fiscal exposure through legislated contributions to the PRGT. In CBO’s estimation, a
haircut of 0.3 reflects that such future contributions would require new legislation and are
therefore not assumed to occur under current law.


- **U.S. ally support.** Countries clear their IMF arrears by using contributions from U.S. allies,

and the United States may concurrently offer aid or other support within a broader
cooperative framework. Although clearing arrears is credited to allies, the shared nature of
the overall aid package suggests a financial cost to the United States. For example, Bosnia
and Herzegovina’s IMF arrears were cleared in 1995 with a bridge loan from the Netherlands
at the same time that the U.S. gave substantial bilateral assistance and reconstruction support
as part of the international stabilization effort after the Dayton Peace Agreement. In such
cases, in which an ally pays off IMF loans in concert with other U.S. foreign assistance, CBO
assigns a haircut of 0.1 to reflect the likelihood that the ally’s payment of IMF arrears was
contingent on the United States’ bearing a cost through some other form of aid.


- **Self- and non–U.S. ally support.** Debtor countries independently resolve their arrears by

instance, Nicaragua resolved its IMF arrears during the 1980s without U.S. involvement,
amid strained relations between the two countries. A haircut of zero indicates no direct or

## **Adjustment for the U.S. Burden Share**


In addition to the adjustment for preferred creditor status, CBO adjusts the cost because the
United States covers a different proportion of the fund’s burden from what the U.S. commitment
to the fund implies. IMF burden sharing is split evenly into two components—one that affects
the United States and another that does not. The fund lowers remuneration rates to countries
contributing resources, such as the United States, and raises the interest rate that it charges
borrowing countries. The United States has historically covered about 14 percent of burden
sharing, whereas countries contributing resources to the IMF have historically covered
36 percent of the burden sharing and borrowing countries have covered the other 50 percent
(see Figure 10).

financial stress indicates that the U.S. share of IMF financing tends to rise when lending demand
is high because financing becomes more concentrated among a smaller set of strong creditor
countries, such as the United States. During such periods, the share of countries supplying
financing to the IMF shrinks, thereby increasing the U.S. share. Data from the IMF’s Financial

period after the global financial crisis, the euro-area crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic—and
declined as conditions normalized. [32]

NPV = _A_ ∑∞ _t_ =1 _Dt_,

factor that translates annual costs into an NPV factor, which under the agency’s projection of

**Uncertainty Beyond Model Parameters**
More uncertainty arises because events could unfold in ways that significantly diverge from
historical patterns:


 - **Reduced use of burden sharing.** The IMF could rely more on its reserve buffer (now at
its SDR 25 billion target) to absorb credit losses instead of invoking burden sharing. [35]


 - **Behavioral changes by the IMF.** The fund might respond to higher credit risk by
tightening lending terms, delaying arrangements, or limiting exposure—all measures that
would reduce potential losses.


 - **Catastrophic scenarios.** A large-scale wave of defaults or a weakening of the IMF’s
preferred creditor status, similar to the scenario considered in CBO’s 2016 report, could
substantially increase fiscal exposure.


Another source of uncertainty is the concentration of the IMF’s lending among a few countries.
As of early 2025, Argentina, Egypt, Pakistan, and Ukraine account for just over half of the
fund’s outstanding credit through the GRA. In the agency’s assessment, the risk of those four
borrowers combined is typical of the type of lending the IMF traditionally conducts. The
situation of those countries represents a broad mix of improving economic and financial
conditions and factors associated with arrears and generous U.S. assistance. Examples include
ongoing military conflicts, reliance on external support, and alignment with U.S. interests in
regions of geopolitical volatility. Although all four countries are now current on their IMF
obligations, some have experienced transitory arrears. The credit risk associated with those
borrowers is at least as high as the average across the fund’s historical lending portfolio and is
consistent with the parameters underlying CBO’s estimates.


That assessment is consistent with recent market-based measures of credit risk. From 2020 to
2024, the weighted average CDS spread across IMF borrowers was 1,777 basis points, compared
with 1,040 basis points over the 2004–2024 period—an increase of 71 percent. That difference
reflects, in part, the higher risk associated with the few countries that account for most recent
IMF lending. Applying that ratio to the central estimate yields a cost of 2.32 percent rather than
1.36 percent. That figure, shown in the fourth column of Table 8, represents an upper-bound
sensitivity based on recent lending conditions.
