3.2/ How to build AI capabilities


**The need for a systematic approach and firm foundations**

Any building designed to last must rest on a solid foundation, and AI is no exception to this

rule. When a company seeks to build AI capabilities, every element in the rAIse framework

must be addressed methodically to ensure that AI initiatives are both technically sound and

aligned with the organization's business goals. The 10 rAIse elements break down into three

categories. **L**


**1** **Define your "North Star":** Your company's vision for AI initiatives should articulate the

specific value AI will bring to the organization in line with overall business goals. That can

be anything from greater efficiency and productivity to a superior customer experience

or new revenue streams. Aligning this North Star with overall business goals keeps all AI

initiatives on track strategically.

**2** **Map out a data strategy and tech blueprint:** These two roadmaps lay a technical

foundation. Crucially, they must identify the critical data needed for your AI goals, along

with efficient methods for acquiring and integrating it. The technical architecture,

including data storage solutions and processing pipelines, must also be defined and the

appropriate AI tools must be selected bring the AI vision to life.

**3** **Stake out your operating model, including AI governance:** The AI operating model is

your command center to ensure smooth execution. It sets ethical guidelines and data

security measures rooted in governance and policies. But it also addresses the human

aspect by fostering collaboration, identifying talent needs and upskilling your workforce.

Lastly, the model encourages partnerships, both internally and with external experts, to

leverage different areas of knowledge and resources.


By this stage, it is important to look at where and how the AI operating model should be

anchored within the organization. Basically, there are three options:

   - As part of an existing department, the AI team sparks innovation by generating ideas and

identifying high-impact AI use cases for different business units. It also spearheads internal

AI education through upskilling initiatives and stays abreast of market trends to ensure

that the organization remains informed and competitive in an ever-changing world.

   - As a separate unit with a limited scope, the AI team works closely with business units and

stays tuned to market trends, co-creating daring AI ideas and tackling high-complexity

projects. Being free from existing AI team structures allows this unit to pursue ambitious

"moonshot" proofs of concept that push the boundaries of what is possible.

   - As a separate unit and owner of the AI agenda, the AI team becomes the organization's

AI powerhouse. It collaborates with business units and tracks market trends to generate

impactful AI ideas. It prioritizes and manages the entire AI project backlog, ensuring

alignment with business goals. It also transforms existing AI teams into a well-oiled

"factory model," maximizing efficiency and delivering on the company's AI vision.


As with the AI readiness score in the previous chapter, knowing where you stand – where you

want to anchor your AI operating model within the organization – gives clarity and visibility

about what skills are needed, how to communicate the target structures and what practical

measures are best suited to making this model work.

3.3/ How to implement AI solutions


**GenAI is not a one-size-fits-all solution – Choosing just the right fit tools to fit your**

**unique challenges is critical**

While AI holds immense potential for virtually all businesses, a combination of technical and

non-technical hurdles can make implementation difficult in practice. In preparing this

report, Roland Berger spoke to Professor Ulrich Gnewuch from the University of Passau

about three key factors that drive business value and ensure successful AI implementation.

These factors are flexible implementation strategies, a well-designed system of change

management and the intelligent choice of the right AI solutions.


**Implementation strategies**

Unlike traditional IT, AI projects require a more flexible implementation strategy to bridge the

gap to the existing system landscape and infrastructure, manage crucial data and ensure

continuous improvement. It is important to emphasize the central role played by data

because, in contrast to classical IT, more time is spent on providing and preparing data, but

also on constantly incorporating new data. Since AI works with probabilities, an iterative and

incremental approach is needed for the system to learn and improve.


Forming cross-functional teams with developers, data protection officers and business

representatives is also recommended. Right from the start of each project, everyone

assigned to it needs to be aware that it must work productively at the end. All stakeholders

who will work with and benefit from the system must therefore be factored in from day one.

Selecting the right tools based on experience and resources is equally crucial, and outside

help too can be invaluable: since many smaller organizations will simply not have sufficient

resources to build the infrastructure themselves, it makes sense to use third-party solutions

that can already map it out with full data protection compliance.


**Change management**

Staff buy-in is crucial for successful AI adoption, so plain talking to employee representatives

is vital: "Businesses should transparently communicate their reasoning, the consequences

and who will be affected by an AI solution. That way, the company maintains sovereignty

over interpretation." Though challenging in the case of GenAI, one helpful method to

**Selecting the right type of AI solution**

AI use cases can demonstrably improve business metrics. Automating customer interactions

with AI chatbots, for example, can significantly improve efficiency and cut costs. That said,

the right solution for the problem at hand must be selected with great care. Predictive AI

tools are the option of choice to address well-defined problems with a focus on efficiency.

These tools excel at tasks with low resource intensity (data and computational power) and

provide understandable and explainable results. Conversely, generative AI (GenAI) tools

should be considered for tasks requiring creative content generation across different

formats. While powerful, these tools require high-quality data and significant computational

resources. The investment in data is thus heavier, and there is less control over output than in

the case of predictive tools. **M**

3.4/ How to govern AI


**AI governance should not be an afterthought – It must be front and**

**center from the outset!**

Strong IT governance and clear regulation are urgent imperatives. The Tay chatbot incident

and the COMPAS case are only two of the more high-profile occurrences that underscore why

this topic is so vital. Precisely because many companies are still in the early stages of

deployment, good AI governance – which should be as dynamic, innovative and creative as

AI itself – should ideally be put in place to steer AI before the technology is scaled up.


**The EU takes the lead in AI legislation**

The European Union's AI Act – the world's first comprehensive legal framework for AI – will

come into force in 2026 with the stated aim of fostering trustworthy AI. The act prescribes

clear rules for AI developers, deployers and others regarding specific uses of AI. It seeks to

ensure that AI systems respect fundamental rights, safety and ethical principles, besides

addressing risks associated with powerful and impactful AI models. A distinction is drawn

between low-risk categories of applications (think translation tools and chatbots) and high
risk applications (such as those for use in healthcare and insurance).

3.5/ How to measure AI success


**Making sure your "new toy" delivers the goods**

If AI is to be a "results business" like any other, it must deliver tangible business value. But how

do you measure precisely what AI adds to your business? Metrics will naturally vary from

industry to industry. Healthcare organizations want to track issues such as patient outcomes

and the efficiency of treatment, while financial service providers are more interested in fraud

detection rates and risk mitigation. Retailers will want to measure sales growth and customer

engagement, whereas manufacturing companies look more closely at reducing downtime

and improving quality.


It is therefore vital to define what success means before trying to measure the impact of an

AI project. Above all, it is important to align the metrics you choose with your overall business

goals – and to avoid "vanity metrics" that have no real-world impact. Besides helping you

monitor progress toward your goals, defining metrics in this way also nurtures expectations

within the organization and keeps people focused on the same goals.


However your organization measures AI success, it must ultimately affect the bottom line.

Typically, establishing this link will not be straightforward, though, so individual IT components

may need to be linked explicitly to business value streams.


Not all AI benefits will show up immediately in profit and loss: AI-powered interactions may

boost customer satisfaction and thus impact profitability in the long term without immediately

pushing up sales. In more complex ecosystems, it can be even harder to isolate the specific

impact of AI on a given metric. Clearly defined use cases can nevertheless make it easier to

track and measure the financial contribution of AI. Similarly, thorough cost-benefit analysis

ahead of implementation can help by estimating both potential cost savings and revenue

gains associated with the AI solution. Another useful approach is to choose metrics that

effectively capture the impact of AI on relevant profit-and-loss components and track them

over time to calculate the return on investment.

Once a mere support function, IT has today become a strategic imperative. The rise of tech

giants such as Apple, Amazon and Microsoft underscores the transformative power of

information technology. However, as businesses embrace the latest IT trends, it's crucial to

do so with a discerning eye and a clear understanding of their potential impact. On top of the

six key IT trends sketched at the beginning of this report – AI disruption, green IT, cyber
