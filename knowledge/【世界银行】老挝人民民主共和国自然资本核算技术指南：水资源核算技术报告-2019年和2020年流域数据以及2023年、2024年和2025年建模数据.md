# Context

In the context of Lao PDR, measuring and valuing natural capital, particularly in the water
and forest sectors, is crucial for informed decision-making and prioritizing investments.
The Lao Statistics Bureau (LSB) has developed a Master Plan for the Development of
Environment Statistics (2022- 2026), aiming to create and publish several natural capital
accounts (NCAs) and integrate natural capital into national investment and policy

planning. These NCAs include material flow, physical energy flow, air emission, water,
forest, land, ecosystem, and environmental taxes. The LSB has sought support from the
World Bank for this initiative.


The World Bank is working with the LSB to compile accounts on water and forest sectors.
The accounts to be compiled include forest extent accounts, forest ecosystem services

and benefits, water supply and use, and water resources.


This technical report summarises the method and results for accounting for water

resource assets. It includes:


1) An overview of Natural Capital Accounting
2) The general approach to accounting for water resource assets, including high level

observations from this exploratory work to compile a water resource asset
account
3) Exploratory methods and results for each component of the account, across three
different broad approaches:

a. Reported statistics from the Water Resource Asset Account

b. Accounts compiled using available data using a tabular solution
c. Accounts compiled using global data


Accompanying reports will provide the methods and results for the other accounting
themes.

# Introduction to Natural Capital Accounting

Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 10


#### **Overview**

Natural capital accounting is about organising data so we can better manage the

environment, the economy and society. At its core, natural capital accounting records the
stock of the environment (our natural assets) and the flows between the environment
and the economy and society. This information helps public and private decision-makers
understand and manage their dependencies and impacts on nature.


Central to natural capital accounting is the use of a standard approach to measurement.

This includes a common framework, concepts, definitions, methods and data. A
standardised approach supports coherence across ecological, economic and social
domains, and enables comparisons across sectors, locations and time. This, in turn,
supports evidence-based decision making for national and regional policies at the
environment–economy nexus.





Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 11


#### **The System of Environmental-Economic Accounting**

Public-sector approaches to natural capital accounting are governed by the System of
Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA). The SEEA is an internationally accepted
framework for incorporating nature into national accounting systems and includes two
major publications: the SEEA Central Framework (CF) and SEEA Ecosystem Accounting
(EA). These frameworks originated from the need to extend the System of National
Accounts – the accounting system used to compile economic figures such as GDP – to

better understand the relationship between the stock of natural capital and economic
activity.









Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 12


#### **Implementing Natural Capital Accounting**

Despite its conceptual richness, the SEEA does not prescribe methods and data for
individual accounts. It is the role of the account compiler to interpret the SEEA framework,
to collect and organise data, and to make transformations to the data where appropriate
to align with the SEEA framework.


The generalised approach to account compilation consists of 3 broad steps: data

collection, transformation of available data into analysis ready data, and preparation of
outputs such as Natural Capital Accounts from account ready data. [1] The choices for
account compilation, which are described in more detail in appendix A, are made based
on available data, resources and capacity.


The choices made when compiling data affect confidence in the outputs compiled. Figure

1 summarises the relationship between method/data tier and confidence in outputs. A
tiered approach based on data quality and methodological / transformation complexity,

provides a structured and simple way to assess confidence levels in early phases of
measurement [2] :


- Tier 1 outputs reflect low confidence and are often used for awareness-raising and

high-level narratives.


- Tier 2 outputs achieve medium confidence, supporting scenario analysis and regional
planning.


- Tier 3 outputs yield high confidence, enabling detailed project-level assessments and

regulatory compliance.

# Summary of results

#### **Policy connections**

Water is a valuable resource with a range of connection to economic and social activity in

all countries. In Lao PDR the economic activities of agriculture, mining and electricity
production are all expected to increase, and each has a high dependency on water, as well

as different impacts on downstream ecological health.


Beyond economic activities, there is ongoing need to facilitate access to suitable quality

water for households and to manage risks associated with, for example, flooding,
occurrences of which are expected to be increasing due to climate change. In Lao PDR
there is an additional challenge of securing water supply during the dry season, which are

becoming less predictable.


All these issues indicate that robust information about water resources and the

connection to usage by economic units including households can underpin a range of
polices. The water resource asset accounts presented in this report provide an initial step
towards this information set.

#### **Key findings**

Although a range of compilation approaches were developed and tested in the NCA work,

compilation of complete water resource asset accounts at basin level on monthly
frequency were not possible. Nonetheless, there are strong grounds for further work in
this area, as well as combining expertise on water resources with statistical expertise on
accounting.


Based on the yearly accounts for Numnguem, Sekong and Nam Ou Basins, it is evident

that substantial variations exist, such that measurement of water resources at national
scale is not useful for policy analysis. For example, total inflows across the three basins
are substantial, but their scale varies considerably. The Numnguem Basin records the
smallest total inflows (42,465 million m³), driven overwhelmingly by rainfall (41,790 mil
m³), with only minor upstream contributions. In contrast, the Sekong Basin, which receives

a larger total inflow of 60,743 mil m³, has inflows more evenly split between upstream
tributary inputs (28,817 mil m³) and rainfall (31,926 mil m³). The Nam Ou Basin shows the

largest hydrological inputs, with total inflows of 97,037 mil m³, reflecting extensive
upstream contributions of 57,327 mil m³, combined with rainfall inflows of 39,710 mil m³.


Variations are also evident in the monthly provincial accounts. Figure 2 shows total

monthly rainfall volumes (converted to mil m [3] ) by provincial group. All regions display a
consistent annual cycle, with sharp peaks during the wet season and near-zero rainfall
volumes during the dry season months. The South records consistently the largest
volumes of rainfall, reflecting both higher rainfall and larger spatial extent of southern
provinces.


The Central and North-Central Highlands groups show intermediate rainfall volumes,
while the Northwest generally records the lowest monthly totals. Across the time series,

wet-season peaks vary considerably from year to year, indicating substantial interannual
variability in monsoon strength and timing.

#### **Findings from the accounting process**


The report describes results and methods for three different approaches when
accounting for water resources:


- Yearly Water Resource Asset Accounts for 3 Basins – relying on reported data from
water resource reports.


- Monthly Water Resource Asset Accounts for Provinces – relying on data sourced by
the LSB on different hydrological variables.


- Monthly Water Resource Asset Accounts for all Basins – relying on global spatial data
layers and geospatial processing techniques to estimate water balance components

within hydrologically defined boundaries.


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 17


A range of findings emerged in testing the implementation of these three approaches:


- Scale matters - Basin-scale accounts are more suitable for hydrological interpretation
and forest–water nexus analysis than province-based accounts.


- Temporal resolution influences usefulness - Monthly accounts provide substantially
greater insight into seasonality, peak demand, and variability, than annual summaries.


- Data coherence is critical - Rainfall, evapotranspiration, runoff, abstractions, and
storage must be methodologically aligned to maintain water balance consistency.


- Water asset accounts require information on stocks (the volume of water stored), but
for rivers and streams the most common measurements are flows (discharge), not
standing volumes. As a result, representing river and stream water as a stock is
conceptually difficult and would require additional assumptions and data (e.g.,

channel geometry and water level–volume relationships). Stocks are more naturally

represented for lakes, reservoirs, and groundwater, but these also require dedicated
monitoring data that are currently limited. Strengthening hydrometric and

groundwater data systems would therefore be necessary to support more complete
stock-based water accounts.


No single data source is sufficient. The most robust long-term solution is likely a blend of:


- national observation data (e.g., gauges, abstractions),


- spatial datasets to fill gaps, and


- process-based hydrological models calibrated to local conditions.


Process-based basin water balance models (e.g., SWAT or similar) offer clear analytical

advantages, particularly for simulating rainfall–runoff relationships, groundwater
contributions, river routing, and land-use impacts. These models would significantly

strengthen alignment with the forest account and allow explicit analysis of how changes
in land cover influence water availability. However, implementing and maintaining such
models requires sustained technical capacity, hydrological expertise, calibration data, and

institutional coordination.


It is also important to note that substantial basin-scale water balance modelling work has
previously been undertaken in Lao PDR through World Bank supported studies, including
technical assessments led by Deltares. This work provides an important foundation and

demonstrates the feasibility of applying calibrated hydrological modelling approaches in

the Lao context. However, the extent to which these modelling approaches can be
institutionalised and implemented on an ongoing basis, including regular updating, recalibration, and routine production of accounting-ready outputs, remains unclear. Future
water accounting efforts should therefore build on the recommendations from prior
work, while also considering the institutional arrangements and resourcing required for
sustained implementation.

#### **Strategic considerations for LSB and Government**

The work undertaken in this report highlights that future development of water resource

asset accounts will require institutional choices.


If the objective is to establish a regular, sustainable production system within the LSB, the
approach must match available technical capacity, resourcing, and long-term
maintenance capability. While desirable, a fully calibrated process-based hydrological
modelling is unlikely to be feasible as an ongoing production method, unless:


- a dedicated technical unit is established within government,


- relevant agencies commit to producing regular model outputs, or


- the LSB invests in building specialised hydrological modelling expertise.


Alternatively, spatial reanalysis-based approaches (as demonstrated in approach 3) offer

a practical and scalable pathway for routine production, while still enabling basin-level

analysis and monthly resolution.


Accordingly, the choice going forward is not simply methodological, but institutional.
Government will need to determine:


- the intended purpose of the water accounts (statistical reporting vs. basin planning
tool),


- the acceptable trade-off between methodological sophistication and operational
feasibility, and


- the level of investment in technical capacity required to support more advanced
modelling approaches.


This report demonstrates what is achievable under current conditions and provides a
foundation for informed decision-making about the future direction of water resource
asset accounting in Lao PDR.

# The Water System in Lao PDR

Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 20


The water system is an intricate and dynamic network, in which water continually moves
between environmental assets such as the atmosphere, oceans, and inland water bodies.
Its availability within inland systems is governed by hydrological processes—including
rainfall, evapotranspiration, infiltration and over longer time frames, geological change.
These processes operate across and beyond jurisdictional boundaries, meaning that

water flows can affect rights and access well outside the place where they originate.


Human activities interact closely with this natural cycle. Economic entities alter
hydrological processes through water abstraction, return flows, land-use change (e.g
deforestation, urban expansion, construction of reservoirs), and the release of pollutants.
Key industries—including energy, agriculture, forestry, and mining—extract water for

diverse purposes, while within the economy, water is distributed between sectors and
households. After use, water is often returned to the environment in altered volume or

quality, and in some cases, it is traded across borders as an economic commodity.


In Lao PDR, this global picture takes on a distinct form shaped by its geography and
climate. The landlocked, mountainous nation is defined by its rivers and valleys, most of

which fall within the Mekong River Basin. The Mekong and its many tributaries underpin
the country’s ecology, economy, and links to neighbouring states. Lao PDR experiences
high rainfall under a monsoonal climate, with pronounced wet and dry seasons that bring
alternating periods of abundance and scarcity. Mountainous terrain and fertile river
valleys channel this water into inland water systems, wetlands, and agricultural lands.


Water is central to Laos’s development strategy, particularly through hydropower. The
expansion of dams and reservoirs has earned the country the title of “Battery of Southeast

Asia,” generating electricity for domestic consumption and export. However, these
changes alter natural flow regimes, with implications for fisheries, sediment transport,
and downstream ecosystems. Agriculture remains the largest water user, supporting rice

cultivation, plantations, livestock production, and inland fisheries. Many rural
communities depend directly on natural flow patterns, making them highly sensitive to
changes in water availability, timing, or quality.


Access to safe drinking water is generally strong in urban centres but can be less reliable
in rural areas, where contamination and seasonal shortages pose greater risks. As a

riparian state, Laos shares its major water resources with neighbouring countries, and
many rivers cross or form international boundaries. Effective water management
therefore requires both national policies and regional cooperation, such as through the
Mekong River Commission.

# General approach to water resource accounting


#### **Account description**

The water resource asset account is a physical account that presents data on the stock

and flows of water resources, including:


- The opening stock of water across different water resource types


- Additions to, and reductions from, the stock over the accounting period


- The closing stock of water across the same water resource categories.


This account is described in the SEEA-Water framework, which provides an integrated
approach to water monitoring by bringing together water-related statistics across sectors
into a coherent information system.


SEEA-Water is an internationally agreed methodological standard that supports
implementation of the SEEA Central Framework. Both frameworks apply the same

accounting concepts, with SEEA-Water providing additional guidance on their application

to water statistics.

#### **Considerations for account compilation**


The Water resource asset account can be compiled using a range of approaches,
depending on:


- Data availability


- Resources and technical capacity of the compilation team


- National priorities and intended policy use.


Because motivations for natural capital accounting differ between countries and

institutional contexts, the level of detail required in an account also varies. While decision
relevance and precision are critical, there is an important trade-off between spatial and
temporal resolution of data, its geographic coverage, and cost and feasibility.


The primary objective of this program of work was to build the capacity of the Lao

Statistics Bureau (LSB) to implement water accounts. Accordingly, the focus was not on
modelling every flow with maximum precision, but rather on compiling the accounts using
available data and aligning methods with the current capabilities of the LSB.


Improvements in data and capability will need to be considered when compiling furture
water resource accounts, to maintain alignment with best practice in an evolving space.


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 24


#### **Account scope**

Water resource asset accounts can take different forms, but they typically include:


- Rows representing opening and closing stocks, as well as additions and reductions
(e.g., rainfall, evapotranspiration)


- Columns distinguishing different types of water resources or stocks (e.g., reservoirs,
rivers, lakes), and in some cases separating environmental processes from economic

activities (e.g., abstractions and return flows)





Table 1 presents a shell account for this NCA work with colour-coded structure to identify
the priorities for measurement. A few observations are made:


- Measuring opening and closing stocks directly is often difficult. In practice, changes in
storage may be inferred from observed additions and reductions, which can still
support robust policy assessment.


- Variables included are precipitation; evapotranspiration; inflows to, and outflows
from, the basin; changes in total water storage; return flows and abstractions.


- Variables excluded are absolute opening and closing stocks of water, and all variables
that depend on detailed groundwater dynamics (e.g. groundwater stocks, recharge

and groundwater abstractions).


- Entries for specific water bodies or stock types (such as artificial reservoirs, lakes,

rivers, and streams) may be included where data are available or where policy
relevance requires greater detail.


This account was originally scoped to support intra-year (monthly) decision-making at the
basin level. This document records efforts taken to deliver this scope.

#### **Approach adaptation and evolution**

As discussed, the account was originally scoped as three separate basin-level accounts,
compiled monthly over three years, using local data. This would have resulted in 108
individual accounts in total. The initial approach involved the LSB leading compilation,
drawing on data from relevant national institutions.


While this ambitious scoping was intended to test the feasibility of producing information
at a decision-relevant scale, it became clear during implementation that to complement

the approach implemented by LSB, an additional step would be to develop a nationalscale basin level water resource account, using available global datasets. This approach
was led by the consultant to support capacity-building within LSB and enable ongoing

account development. The key difference in the approach is that global data can be
processed easily for multiple catchments, without additional overhead for different
methods and approaches for an individual basin. Furthermore, national scale basin level

accounts were compiled to demonstrate the potential measurement options.

##### **Opportunities for improvement**


1. Strengthening institutional collaboration


The compilation process highlights a clear opportunity to strengthen collaboration
between the Lao Statistics Bureau (LSB) and the Department of Water Resources (DWR)
to support routine production of water accounts. A more formalised joint approach—
where DWR provides core hydrological datasets and LSB leads SEEA-aligned
compilation—would improve efficiency, consistency, and long-term sustainability. This

workflows for ongoing account updates.

monitoring systems and improving access to more timely datasets could support
seasonal or monthly accounts in priority basins, increasing the accounts’ relevance for
operational planning and policy decisions.

basin water outcomes. Future improvements could focus on better integrating land cover
and forest data into basin-level water balances, and developing indicators that support
watershed protection, climate resilience planning, and cross-sector coordination.

#### **Assessment of confidence for raw data and methods**

##### **Rainfall**

Overall, the analysis-ready rainfall data underpinning the monthly provincial account are
categorised as medium confidence and are appropriate for aggregate-level decision
making, particularly for understanding seasonal variability and relative differences
between provinces.


The rainfall data are derived from national rainfall station datasets and are best classified
as tier 2-3, reflecting official monitoring data:


- Station-based rainfall observations are generally reliable where monitoring networks
are well maintained.


- Confidence depends on the number, distribution, and completeness of stations

within each province.


- Uncertainty increases where:


  - station density is low,

  - stations are unevenly distributed (e.g., concentrated in lowland areas),

  - or time series contain gaps.


Rainfall variability in Lao PDR is strongly influenced by elevation and monsoon dynamics,
meaning that representativeness can vary significantly within a province.


The method for compiling rainfall volumes is classified as tier 2.

- Averaging station rainfall and converting from millimetres to volumetric rainfall using

provincial area is transparent and reproducible.


- The approach is consistent with common practice where spatially gridded rainfall

products are not available.


- However, it assumes that the station average is representative of the entire province,

which may not fully capture spatial heterogeneity.


The method is appropriate for exploratory and aggregate accounting, but precision

would improve with spatial rainfall surfaces and basin-based delineation.

##### **Evapotranspiration**


Overall, the evapotranspiration (ET) estimates are categorised as medium confidence,
suitable for exploratory and aggregate analysis, but subject to greater modelling
uncertainty than rainfall.


The ET calculation draws on multiple tabular inputs, including:


- Temperature data (ID3),


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 43


- Rainfall data (ID2),


- Crop area statistics (rice),


- Crop coefficients (Kc),


- Latitude parameters.


These inputs are best characterised as tier 2–3, reflecting official climate observations
and administrative agricultural statistics combined with standard agronomic parameters.


- Temperature data are generally stable and reliable, though subject to the same
station coverage limitations as rainfall.


- Rice area data may be affected by reporting lags, definitional inconsistencies (e.g.,
planted vs harvested area), and incomplete capture of informal production.


- Crop coefficients and effective rainfall parameters are drawn from standard guidance

and may not fully reflect local crop varieties or irrigation practices.


The evapotranspiration method is classified as tier 2 and should be interpreted as

indicative of seasonal water demand rather than precise measures of actual water
consumption.


- The Hargreaves equation provides a practical and transparent approach where full
meteorological data (required for Penman–Monteith) are unavailable.


- It is suitable for monthly estimation in data-constrained environments.


- However, it is less robust than the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith method and may
introduce bias under certain climatic conditions.


Additional uncertainty arises in estimating actual evapotranspiration (ET ₐ ), which
depends on assumptions regarding water availability:


- Effective rainfall is estimated using standard FAO approaches.


- Irrigation and soil moisture constraints are not covered due to limited data.


- For rice systems in particular, water availability is strongly influenced by irrigation
management and paddy storage, which are not explicitly modelled.

#### **Opportunities for improvement**

##### **Rainfall**

An important next step is to strengthen the spatial representation of rainfall by assessing
alternative rainfall layers alongside station-based provincial averages. This would involve
comparing candidate gridded rainfall products (e.g., satellite- and reanalysis-derived
datasets) against observed station data to evaluate bias, seasonal consistency, and
performance in areas with sparse coverage or complex terrain. Establishing a consistent,
validated rainfall input would improve comparability across provinces and support future
basin-scale accounts.


Separate technical work has been undertaken by Deltares to assess rainfall datasets and
identify recommended approaches. Any future development of water resource accounts
should build on and align with the recommendations made in that assessment to ensure

methodological consistency and avoid duplication of effort.

##### **Evapotranspiration**


A key next step is to strengthen evapotranspiration estimation by assessing alternative

ET methods and input datasets beyond the current temperature-based Hargreaves
approach. This should include comparing Hargreaves outputs against Penman–Monteith
(FAO-56) estimates where sufficient meteorological inputs exist, and/or against credible
gridded ET and climate products, to quantify bias, seasonal behaviour, and sensitivity of
monthly water demand estimates. Establishing consistent assumptions for crop
calendars and crop coefficients (Kc)—and improving representation of irrigation and
effective rainfall for rice—would further increase the interpretability of results for
seasonal water stress and the forest–water nexus. The test approach here has been to

focus on rice, however this could be extended in the future as seen valuable.


Separate technical work has been undertaken by Deltares on evapotranspiration

datasets and recommended approaches. Any future development of water resource

accounts should pick up and align with those recommendations to ensure consistency
and avoid duplication of effort.

##### **Abstractions and returns by economic units**


In concept, the abstractions and returns by economic units in the water resources asset
account is the same as those entries in the water physical supply and use table (PSUT). In
practice, the difference is that the water PSUT is most commonly compiled at national
level and for an annual frequency. Therefore, in order to compile estimates relevant for

the water resources asset account for a province and on a monthly frequency, methods
are needed to transform the national level annual data. The development of these


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 45


methods was not completed in this NCA work but a range of suggestions for progressing
this area of measurement have been identified.


Methods and data sources that might be considered include:


- Surveys of the use and returns of water by economic units at provincial level covering
both businesses and households. This might be conducted by geo-coding the existing
data from current household and enterprise surveys.


- For irrigation and livestock, which are likely to be major water users, the LECS survey

might be very relevant. Relevant data is likely to also be available from the agricultural
census.


- Allocating national totals using estimates of economic activity in different provinces
for example using measures of production or employment. To accommodate
differences across provinces, some estimates of water consumption rates per unit of

production could be incorporated.


- Population data will be relevant in considering household water use.


- Monthly allocations could consider changes in production over time and the links to
rainfall patterns. Even though the water resources asset account does not present

data by industry, it would be relevant to undertake estimates by industry such that
variations in the patterns of use and returns can be appropriately integrated.

##### **In and out transfers**


Transfers should not be assessed at the provincial scale, as provinces are not
hydrologically bounded and river flows crossing provincial borders are primarily natural
movements within a basin system. Meaningful assessment of upstream and downstream

flows can only occur when accounts are compiled at the river basin scale.


At the basin scale, an important opportunity for improvement is to explicitly quantify

surface water inflows from upstream basins and outflows to downstream basins, using
observed or modelled discharge at basin boundaries. However, this will require
addressing key data constraints. River flow monitoring remains relatively sparse in many

areas, and gauging stations are not consistently located at basin outlets or boundary
points. As a result, challenges remain in reliably apportioning observed river flows to

specific basins, particularly where tributary systems are complex or where station
catchments do not align with accounting boundaries. Strengthening the river gauging
network and/or integrating hydrological modelling would be important steps toward
incorporating upstream and downstream flows into future basin-scale accounts.

### **Appendix B: Water balance models**

Water balance models (WBM) are a common tool for compiling information on water

across different spatial and temporal scales. They are process-based, gridded hydrologic
models that simulate the land-surface components of the global water cycle (see Figure
27). These models are built on a set of equations that describe how water moves through
and interacts with various environments and importantly, they are catchment facing, land
management matters a lot.


**Figure 27. Schematic representation of the water balance model (WBM)**























_Source: (Grogan et al., 2022)_



The WBM represents all major land surface components of the hydrologic cycle, and

tracks fluxes and balances between the atmosphere, above-ground water storages (e.g.,
snowpack), soil, vegetation, groundwater, and runoff. A digitized river network connects
each grid cell to the next, enabling simulation of flow through river systems. The WBM
includes domestic and industrial water requirements and use, agricultural water
requirements and use (irrigation and livestock), and hydro-infrastructure (dams and inter
basin transfers). WBM can be run for any region and any spatial resolution, given available

input data at the appropriate scale.


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 70


At their core, the equations follow the principle of conservation of mass — water cannot
be created or destroyed, only moved or stored in different places. A simple version looks
like this:

Δ𝑆= Inflows           - Outflows

These models typically work across spatial units –a grid cell, a hydrological response unit,
or a catchment. The principles are the same, such that for any spatial unit, the change in
storage can be expressed more fully as:


Δ𝑆 = 𝑃 − 𝐸𝑇 − 𝑄 ± 𝐺


Where:


- **ΔS** = change in water stored in soil, groundwater, or surface water


- **P** = precipitation (rain, snow, hail)


- **ET** = evapotranspiration (evaporation from water bodies and soil, plus plant
transpiration)


- **Q** = runoff or streamflow leaving the location


- **G** = net groundwater exchange (inflow or outflow)


In detailed models, these terms are broken down further using equations that describe:


- Infiltration – how fast water soaks into soil, often using versions of the _Green–Ampt_ or
_Horton_ infiltration equations.


- Runoff generation – how rainfall exceeding infiltration capacity becomes surface flow,
governed by terrain slope and soil properties.


- Evapotranspiration – often calculated with the _Penman–Monteith equation_, which links
temperature, humidity, wind, and solar radiation.


- Groundwater flow – based on _Darcy’s Law_, which relates flow to the slope of the water
table and the permeability of subsurface materials.


While the mathematics behind these can be complex, the key idea is that every drop of
water is tracked as it moves between the atmosphere, land surface, rivers, lakes, and

aquifers. By summing these exchanges over time and across space, the model produces
the inflows, outflows, and storage changes needed to fill in the water asset account.


Ideally, a water balance model would be run monthly to produce timely and consistent

estimates. However, this requires time, resources, and technical capacity that are not
currently available within the Lao Statistical Office. The immediate approach is therefore
to develop shortcuts—methods that can deliver equivalent monthly outputs without
depending on another agency to run the full model.

### **Appendix C: Variable descriptions**

##### **Precipitation**

In a WBM, precipitation (P) is a core daily input (mm/day). It represents all water falling
from the atmosphere to the land surface, mainly as rain in tropical climates.


Precipitation is a forcing variable in water balance model, meaning it is an external driver
supplied to the model rather than something the model calculates internally. It provides
the primary water input to the system and sets the pace for hydrologic processes such as
infiltration, runoff, and evapotranspiration. Other forcing variables that are important for
the model, but are not included in the water resource account, may include:


- Air temperature – controls evapotranspiration rates and influences plant growth.


- Solar radiation – affects energy balance and evaporation.


- Wind speed – influences evaporation and transpiration rates.


- Relative humidity – affects the evaporative demand of the atmosphere.


Precipitation enters the model’s water balance as the primary source term. Each day, P is
added to the surface water pool, where it is partitioned into:


- Infiltration – water moving into the soil profile


- Surface runoff – water flowing over the land into streams/rivers


- Evaporation and transpiration (ET) – losses back to the atmosphere


How P is split between these pathways depends on land cover, soil properties,

topography, and the model’s hydrologic equations.


Precipitation is also a variable outputted by a water balance model and can be used

immediately to populate account entries.


Technical Report Accounting for Water resources | 72


##### **Evapotranspiration**

Evapotranspiration (ET) represents the combined loss of water to the atmosphere

through plant transpiration and soil or surface evaporation. It is a central component of
the water balance, as it links climate, vegetation, and water availability.


Two related concepts are important to distinguish:


- Potential evapotranspiration (PET) refers to the atmospheric demand for water
assuming unlimited availability at the surface (i.e., well-watered conditions). PET is

thus climate driven.


- Reference evapotranspiration (ET ₀ ) is a standardized version of PET, defined for a
hypothetical reference crop (a short, well-watered grass surface). ET ₀ depends only on
weather conditions (temperature, radiation, wind, humidity) and provides a common
benchmark across sites.


Building on this:


- crop evapotranspiration (ETc) adjusts ET ₀ for specific land covers or crops by applying
a crop coefficient (Kc), reflecting differences in plant physiology and growth stage.


- Finally, actual evapotranspiration (ETa) is the water actually consumed, which may be
less than ETc if soil moisture or water supply is limited. ETa thus represents the
realised water loss, constrained by hydrological and management conditions.


In water balance models, ET is usually estimated for each sub-basin or land cover type
based on climate data (e.g. temperature, solar radiation, humidity, wind) and vegetation
characteristics.

### **Appendix D: Land surface models**

Land surface models (LSMs) are models of the land component of the climate system,

developed primarily to represent how the land surface exchanges water and energy with

the atmosphere. They simulate processes such as rainfall interception by vegetation,

infiltration into soils, soil moisture storage, evaporation and plant transpiration (often

through resistance- or Penman–Monteith–type formulations), snow accumulation and

melt, and the partitioning of rainfall into evapotranspiration and runoff. Most LSMs

operate on a gridded spatial framework (from regional to global scales) and are commonly

driven by meteorological forcing (observations or reanalysis) to produce spatially

complete monthly or daily estimates of variables like evapotranspiration, soil moisture,

and surface runoff.


A defining feature of LSMs is that they solve a coupled water balance and surface energy

balance, meaning that evapotranspiration and other fluxes are constrained by both

moisture availability and atmospheric energy demand. Because they are designed to

interact with atmospheric models, LSMs focus strongly on producing internally consistent

surface fluxes across large areas, and they often include parameterisations to represent

sub-grid variability in soils and vegetation. Many modern LSMs can also include human

influences in simplified forms (e.g., irrigation demand or land cover change), though the

degree of water management representation varies widely by model and configuration.


In contrast, water balance models used in basin management and accounting (including

catchment models such as SWAT, or gridded hydrological WBMs) are typically oriented

toward catchment water accounting and river outcomes, with a primary focus on runoff

generation, streamflow, and storage changes within hydrologically defined units. While

both model types can produce core terms like precipitation, evapotranspiration, and

runoff, LSMs are fundamentally designed around land–atmosphere coupling and energy

constraints, whereas water balance models are generally designed to represent

hydrological flows and water resource dynamics within river basins, often with more

explicit treatment of routing and, in some cases, water infrastructure and management.
