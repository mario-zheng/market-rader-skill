### **Executive Summary**

**Tourism offers significant opportunities for women’s**
**participation, entrepreneurship, and leadership**
**compared to other sectors, but there are challenges**
**to address.** Jobs in tourism are more likely to be low
paid, businesses tend to be smaller, informal, and less
well-capitalized, and present risks such as vulnerability
to exploitation, particularly in low-income countries
like The Gambia. The United Nations (UN) Global
Report on Women and Tourism highlights that women
are nearly twice as likely to be employers in the
tourism sector as in any other industry, attributing this
to the sector’s characteristics such as low entry
barriers and flexible work arrangements. However,
despite these opportunities, challenges persist.
Women in the tourism sector often face exploitation,
trafficking, and harassment due to the sector’s
predominantly informal structure. The risks are
exacerbated by the lack of formal protections and the
vulnerability of women in low-income settings within
the industry.


**The Gender Assessment of the Tourism Sector in The**
**Gambia documents gender disparities and key issues**
**hindering women’s participation and earnings in**
**tourism** **through** **the** **lens** **of** **employment,**
**entrepreneurship, and leadership.** The assessment
also examines cross-cutting factors such as policy and
institutional environment, education, and social
norms, which impact women’s economic opportunities
in the sector and influence their decision to enter and
remain in the sector. The findings are based on
comprehensive desk research, semi-structured
interviews, and key informant interviews conducted in
person in The Gambia in May 2023.


**Despite** **women** **showing** **strong** **interest** **in**
**hospitality, reflected in higher enrollment rates than**
**men in tourism and hospitality institutes, this is not**
**translating into higher employment shares due to a**
**combination of key supply- and demand-side barriers.**
Gender employment disparities in Gambian tourism
reveal significant gaps favoring male workers over
female workers, though the gap narrows for part-time
roles. Women constitute 35 percent of full-time
employees in the tourism sector, compared to 65
percent for men, resulting in a gender gap of 29
percentage points. Women are more likely to be hired



for part-time or seasonal roles and often occupy
positions such as receptionists, waitresses,
housekeeping staff, and some managerial roles in
Human Resources (HR), finance, and marketing.
Women face barriers including lack of skills, restrictive
gender norms, limited information about lucrative
tourism jobs, and discriminatory hiring practices,
which contribute to their lower participation rates.
There is still a societal stigma associated with women
working in hotels, exacerbated by safety concerns in
late hours and gender norms.


**In tourism entrepreneurial activities, most women-**
**owned** **businesses** **face** **challenges** **in** **skills**
**development, securing finance, accessing tourism**
**segments and markets, and are further limited by a**
**lack of infrastructure and testing labs.** Predominantly,
women engage in entrepreneurial activities as craft
sellers, beach fruit vendors, tour operators, restaurant
owners, and indirectly contribute to the tourism sector
by supplying vegetables, food, and through catering,
beauty, skincare, and fashion businesses. While gender
disaggregated data is not available on tourism business
ownership in The Gambia, in general, women-owned
businesses are predominantly micro: 83 percent of
women-led businesses are solo ventures, compared to
63 percent of male-led businesses. Moreover, less than
1 percent of female-led businesses can be classified as
medium or large, highlighting the dearth of prominent
female business role models in the sector. Earnings are
also lower, with median monthly profit for female-led
businesses at 3000 GMD, compared to 5000 GMD for
male-led businesses. The limited diversity in the
tourism sector, also limits entrepreneurship
opportunities, with most women entrepreneurs
primarily engaged in souvenirs, fruit sellers, and
produce providers. One of the key issues facing
women tourism entrepreneurs is the lack of skills,
including in handcraft skills that tends to limit women
vendors to selling crafts made elsewhere, but also in
business management such as financial literacy skills.
The challenge also lies in the limited variety of markets
and tourism segments available to showcase local
products, as well as the weak connections to hotels,
supermarkets, and online platforms. Additionally, lack
of market infrastructure and testing labs also limits
outlets where women can sell more innovative
Gambian-made items and consumption goods.

### **Introduction**

**The travel and tourism sector has demonstrated a**
**greater** **capacity** **to** **offer** **women** **increased**
**employment opportunities in comparison to other**
**sectors, thereby presenting a significant potential for**
**women’s** **advancement** **within** **the** **economy.**
According to the UN Global Report on Women and
Tourism by World Tourism Organization, tourism
offers superior opportunities for women’s
participation in the workforce, women’s
entrepreneurship, and women’s leadership than other
sectors of the economy. For example, the report found
that women are nearly twice as likely to be employers
in the tourism sector than in any other industry
(UNWTO, 2010). Women are also more likely to be
involved in tourism due to the unique characteristics of
the tourism sector. In general, the barriers to entry are
low, particularly in terms of skill and educational
requirements and capital needs for entrepreneurial
initiatives. There is less emphasis on formal education
and training and greater emphasis on interpersonal
and hospitality skills. There are increased options for
entrepreneurship that do not require heavy start-up
financing. Moreover, the nature of the work can be
flexible in terms of work hours, as well as part-time or
full-time employment.

### **Methodology**

**2.1 Framework**


**The Gender Gap Analysis examines women’s**
**participation in tourism through three lenses:**
**Employment, Entrepreneurship and Leadership.**
Employment and Entrepreneurship are seen as
principal objectives for increasing women’s
participation and earnings in tourism. They are treated
separately as the stakeholders and interventions can
be very different than that of men. For example, how
to encourage hotels to hire more women staff requires
a different intervention than how to encourage hotels
to procure from women-owned enterprises. The third
pillar, Leadership is seen both as an enabling factor
that can support bringing about the necessary policies



to support women’s employment and
entrepreneurship, but it is also a key outcome on its
own. While assessing the status of women in
leadership roles within tourism and more broadly,
women’s presence in broader decision-making
mechanisms is also taken into consideration.


**The** **Gambian Tourism** **Ecosystem** **Assessment**
**examines the enabling environment for women’s**
**economic participation through three cross-cutting**
**areas: policy and institutional environment, tourism**
**education and social norms.** These are explored as the
key external factors contributing to women’s
outcomes in employment, entrepreneurship, and
leadership.

**2.2 Data collection**


**The first step in the assessment was desk research.**
The World Bank Group’s Gender Data Portal, The
United Nations Development Programme’s (UNDP)
Gender Development Index, World Economic Forum
Global Gender Gap Report, and Women, Business, and
the Law provided useful initial background information
on how The Gambia was performing on gender
equality. The National Tourism Strategy, National
Gender Strategy, and the World Bank Country
Partnership Framework provided an understanding of
the broader policy landscape of the country. The
assessment also noted descriptive statistics on gender
gaps shared in country surveys or in other surveys such
as World Bank Enterprise Surveys.

**Recent** **research** **conducted** **in** **The** **Gambia**
**contributed invaluable insights to the gender**
**assessment of the tourism sector.** Some of these
resources include The Gambia Poverty and Gender
Assessment 2022, Policies for Sustainable and Inclusive
Private Sector-led Growth: Achieving Sustainable and
Inclusive Growth, The Gambia and the Project
Appraisal Document of the Tourism Diversification and
Resilience in The Gambia (P177179) among others.

**Following the desk research, the assessment**
**undertook a mapping of the stakeholders with a few**
**important considerations.** First, the authors identified
several actors in the five economic activities that
generate revenues and income in the tourism sector –
transport, accommodations, excursions, food and
beverage, and shopping. Depending on the size of each
sub-economic activity and the conditions in the field,
the authors interviewed several stakeholders to reflect
diversity of experience and opinion. In addition,
relevant stakeholder suggestions by the Project
Implementation Unit of the current Gambia Tourism
and Resilience project were included in the list. Finally,
the assessment tried to ensure a balance of female and
male participants in the stakeholder meetings.

**Key informant interviews and focus group discussions**
**were conducted in The Gambia in May 2023.** The team



carried out 33 individual interviews, including 23 with
key informants, and organized a focus group involving
eight women entrepreneurs from various industries
within the tourism value chain, facilitated by the
Gambia Women’s Chamber of Commerce. Key
informants included representatives from industry
associations; heads of training institutes; managers of
direct tourism service providers in accommodation,
tour excursion and restaurants; enterprise support
organizations (ESOs); development partners; NGOs;
and a former policy maker (Table 2). Key informant
interviews took around 30 minutes to one hour. In
addition, the authors held ten informal individual
interviews with hotel staff, craft sellers, fruit sellers,
and tour guides.

**The in-person assessment also resulted in the**
**collection of data from tourism organizations, though**
**finding sex-disaggregated data was a challenge**
**throughout the assessment.** The team also uncovered
further data collected by the Gambia Tourism Board
and the GTHI during the in-person assessment. This
additional information filled some gaps in data and
provided a complementary analysis, but more data will
need to be systematically collected to shed light on the
issues women face in the tourism industry as well as
any progress made going forward.

**The assessment’s focus was primarily on urban**
**tourism centers, though it investigated community-**
**based tourism in rural areas which carries a potential**
**for The Gambian tourism and women’s economic**
**opportunities.** The in-person consultations mostly
took place around urbanized centers such as
Serrakunda and Banjul, except for a few ecolodges
located near the south-western coastal area where site
visits and interviews with workers were conducted.
Even though the assessment did not conduct in-depth
interviews in villages, interviews with vendors around
Kunta Kinteh Island and with community-based
tourism actors such as Institute of Travel and Tourism
in The Gambia were undertaken. The assessment
recognizes the key linkages, especially agricultural,
between the rural economy and the tourism
ecosystem. Where necessary, the analysis tried to fill
in the gap with the study, Barriers to Economic
Empowerment of Rural Women in The Gambia, which
has a strong focus on rural women and findings from
relevant key informant interviews.


### **The Gambia Context**

**3.1 Tourism in The Gambia**


**Situated on the western coast of Africa, The Gambia,**
**with a population of 2.4 million, spans along the**
**banks of the Gambia River, providing it with a unique**
**geography and diverse ecosystems.** Bordered by
Senegal on three sides, The Gambia is the smallest
country on the mainland continent but draw tourists
from around the world. One of the primary sources of
tourism in The Gambia is its coastline, stretching for
approximately 80 kilometers along the Atlantic Ocean.
The Gambia is mainly a sun and sea tourism destination
with two main seasons: high season from November to
April ( _dry season_ ) and low season from May to October
( _rainy season_ ).


**Tourism is an important contributor to The Gambia’s**
**GDP and a vital source of jobs; however the sector**
**lags its competitors due to fewer arrivals, shorter**
**stays, and lower tourism expenditures** . These
challenges mainly result from limited diversification in
source markets, distribution channels, and product
offering and leads to high seasonality, modest spillover
effects, and susceptibility to climate risks and shocks.
Pre-pandemic, tourism was estimated to directly
contribution between 12 and 16 percent of GDP,
supporting over 42,000 jobs directly and an additional
40,000 jobs indirectly (UNDP, 2020). It generated an
annual US$ 85 million in foreign exchange earnings,
making it the country’s number one foreign exchange
earner (World Bank, 2022c). However, the stark
disparity in seasonal variations, with low-season
passenger arrivals comprising only about 40 percent of
those in the high season, remains a critical concern
impacting both employment rates and earnings within
the industry.


**The Gambia attracts tourists mostly from a few select**
**markets in Europe but is trying to expand its reach to**
**other countries** . According to The Gambia Tourism
Board, the United Kingdom is the lead market and
British visitors constitute 17 percent of all visitors and
42 percent of visitors coming from traditional markets.
It is followed by Netherlands (8 percent of all visitors
and 19 percent of visitors from traditional markets),
Spain, Germany, Belgium, Sweden, Denmark, Norway,
and Finland. According to The Gambia Tourism Board



Arrival Statistics in 2022, international arrivals by air
were only at 77.5 percent of pre-COVID levels (Gambia
Tourism Board, 2022).


**The Gambia possesses a rich asset base but has barely**
**diversified away from ‘sun and sea’ tourism into other**
**areas such as eco-tourism and cultural tourism.**
According to the World Economic Forum Travel and
Tourism Competitiveness Report, The Gambia ranks 61
out of 136 countries when it comes to the total number
of known fauna species and 54 on the attractiveness of
natural assets index (World Economic Forum, 2017). It
is also among the countries with the highest number of
United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural
Organization (UNESCO) sites in the Sub-Saharan
region, with two cultural heritage sites (Stone Circles of
Senegambia and Kunta Kinteh Island), and one
intangible expression of culture (the “Roots” Festival,
commemorating the experiences of the descendants
of African slaves who were taken from the region
during the transatlantic slave trade). Nevertheless,
many tourist sites are currently in a state of disrepair
and are poorly maintained, requiring substantial
renovation and improvements.


**Climate change and coastal erosion have a direct**
**impact on cultural products and goods in the country.**
Kunta Kinteh Island, a World Heritage Site, is shrinking
in size annually, with part of it now submerged in the
ocean, endangering its integrity and tourism value. The
infrastructure challenges due to climate change and
low maintenance are some of the key issues tackled by
the World Bank’s Tourism Diversification and
Resilience in The Gambia project. Moreover,
considering that over half of the nation’s population
resides in urban regions and around 80 percent of the
urban population is situated within a 20 km range of
the Atlantic coast, the anticipated increase in sea levels
presents a significant peril to the local population,
including stakeholders ranging from fishers to hotels
across the spectrum of the tourism supply chain.


**The COVID-19 pandemic contributed to massive**
**downsizes in personnel and closures of activity within**
**the sector, also disproportionately impacting women** .
The accommodation sector was the most affected with
about 94 percent of establishments reducing staff level
(The Gambia Bureau of Statistics, 2020). In 2020, there
was a 62 percent drop in air arrivals over 2019.
According to World Travel & Tourism Council 2022,
tourism’s contribution to GDP sharply fell to 7 percent
in 2020 from 15 percent in 2019. However, the sector


is showing signs of recovery as income from tourism
has increased considerably from an estimated US$58.7
million in 2021 to US$184.1 million in 2022 (Gambia
Tourism Board, 2022). Women, who are
predominantly employed in service roles, faced
heightened vulnerability to income insecurity during
the pandemic. The impact on women in the tourism
industry was exacerbated by the substantial number
engaged in informal employment. When women in the
tourism sector operate within informal businesses or
as self-employed workers in the informal sector, they
generally have low savings and no monetary support,
especially compared to those in formal jobs. These
issues are commonly linked to such informal work
arrangements, rendering them more susceptible to
economic shocks and crises (International Labour
Organization, 2022).


**3.2 Status of Women in The Gambia**


**The gender gaps in Gambia are substantial.** According
to the 2023 World Economic Forum Global Gender Gap
Report, the country ranks 119th out of 146 countries in
gender equality. In the UNDP Gender Inequality Index,
which analyzes three dimensions—reproductive
health, empowerment, and the labor market—Gambia
ranks 153rd out of 170 countries. And more broadly, in
the UNDP Human Development Index, which measures
progress in basic dimensions of human development,
Gambia ranks 174 out of 191 countries and is placed in
the low human development category.


**Women and girls in Gambia experience a complex**
**web of challenges.** According to World Bank Gender
Data Portal, only 51 percent of women aged 15 and
above can read and write compared to 62.5 percent of
men. The gap in adult literacy between men and
women is larger than the gap in the Sub-Saharan Africa
(SSA) average. Child marriage remains prevalent,
disrupting education, endangering young mothers, and
perpetuating poverty and gender inequality. Despite
the criminalization of female genital mutilation (FGM)
in The Gambia, the practice remains prevalent. About
50 of percent girls aged 0-14 years have undergone
FGM, and this rate goes up to 75 per cent of women
and girls, aged 15-49, who have undergone some form
of FGM (United Nations Population Fund, 2013).


**Women’s roles are often delineated by traditional**
**gender norms, impacting their access to education,**
**employment opportunities, and decision-making**
**power** . In The Gambia, traditional gender roles often



prescribe distinct responsibilities for men and women,
reinforcing the perception that women should
primarily be responsible for domestic duties and
caregiving, while men are expected to take on roles
that involve decision-making and economic activities.
Starting from a young age, girls are exposed to the
notion that boys are the ones meant to be leaders,
which persist as they grow older, with institutions and
communities further reinforcing this message (UN,
2022).


**Intrahousehold dynamics exert a significant influence**
**on the outcomes of women’s empowerment efforts.**
While enhancing the access to economic
opportunities, the distribution of resources,
responsibilities, and decision-making power within a
household can profoundly impact a woman’s ability to
fully participate in and benefit from these
opportunities. According to The Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD)
Development Centre’s Social Institutions and Gender
Index (SIGI), The Gambia experiences notable levels of
discrimination within households, highlighting
significant power imbalances among family members.
When it comes to who decides how household money
is spent, one survey reveals that men are considerably
more likely than women to say they make such
decisions themselves (61 percent vs. 44 percent). More
women than men say they decide jointly with their
spouse (28 percent vs. 10 percent) (Afrobarometer,
2023). These dynamics could also yield far-reaching
outcomes. Given that men are regarded as household
leaders, women might face a higher likelihood of job
displacement during off-peak periods, owing to the
perception that men are responsible for family
support. This situation might already be in effect since
women often engage in part-time or seasonal
employment.


**Women lack access to paid work and labor market**
**opportunities are particularly limited for young**
**women.** In The Gambia, the labor force participation
rate (proportion of the population ages 15 and older
that is economically active) among females is 57.3
percent and among males is 65.8 percent for 2022
(World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2022). Women are
3.7-times more likely to be unpaid helpers in family
businesses, and 26 percent more likely to be engaged
in subsistence agriculture (World Bank, 2022c). Most
women are in vulnerable employment, which means
they are less likely to have formal work arrangements,


social protection, and safety nets to guard against
economic shocks; thus, they are more likely to fall into
poverty. Vulnerable employment among women is
81.3 percent and among men is 62.7 percent in The
Gambia for 2019 (World Bank Gender Data Portal,
2019).


**The level of women's involvement in decision-making**
**and leadership roles in The Gambia continues to be**
**limited.** Women in The Gambia ranks 132nd out of 146
countries on the political empowerment of women
according to the 2023 World Economic Forum Global
Gender Gap Report. Women's roles are often
delineated by traditional gender norms, impacting
their access to education, employment opportunities,
and decision-making power. Like many places
worldwide, gender-based violence (GBV) is a
significant concern in The Gambia, with one in four

**In the domain of self-employment, where a**
**significant number of women are involved, women**
**face difficulties in opening bank accounts and**
**accessing formal credit due to underlying constraints**
**which disadvantage women.** According to Global
Findex Database, 33 percent of Gambia’s population
above age 15 has an account at a financial institution
or through a mobile money provider. In 2022, 38
percent of men had an account while 28 percent of
women did so, almost 10 percentage point gender gap
in account ownership (World Bank Global Findex
Database, 2021). In terms of accessing credit, women
lack collateral to secure loans. For example, only 9
percent of women own a land, compared to 31 percent
of men, and only 16.8 percent of women, compared to
40.6 percent of men, owned a dwelling either alone or
jointly (World Bank Gender Data Portal). Distance to
bank branches is also more of an impediment for
women than for men, as women have lower mobility,
more responsibilities for caring for children, and lack
means of transportation – only 2 percent of women in
one study owned a means of transport versus 58
percent of men (UNCDF, 2019).


**3.3 Tourism Segments in The Gambia: Potential for**
**Women's Participation**


**The beach—referred to as 'sun and sea'—is by far the**
**most popular tourism attraction, and heavily reliant**
**on International Tour Operators (ITOs) who bring in**



**50–70 percent of all tourists.** This sub-segment
remains the main attraction for about 80 percent of
tourists and allows ITOs to control 70 percent of all air
access and international marketing, as well as
contribute 85 percent of all hotel revenues (World
Bank Group, 2022d). This assessment analyzes
women’s participation in the hotel workforce and the
role of women-led businesses in the hotel value chains
in the gender gap analysis section.


**The diversification of the tourism offering in The**
**Gambia, especially** _**conference tourism, eco-tourism,**_
_**and community-based tourism**_ **could lead to**
**promising economic outcomes for women, both in**
**terms of improving women’s employment and**
**women-led** **MSMEs** **accessing** **new** **markets,**
**contributing** **to** **inclusive** **economic** **growth**
**opportunities.** Due to limited diversification,
distribution channels, and product offerings, Gambia
currently lags behind its competitors. The
diversification carries new possibilities for women’s
economic involvement in the sector while also
broadening the sector and boosting the economy.


**For example, conference tourism is an emerging area**
**that could bring in international visitors and shorten**
**the low season** . With the opening of a state-of-the-art
convention center in August 2020, Gambia has seen an
influx of local, regional, and international conferences
being hosted there, and demand is expected to
continue growing. According to the Gambia Tourism
Board Arrivals Report, Business and Conference
visitors were up by 3 percent in 2022. Conference
tourism can also serve to entice attendees to extend
their stays, or return, either by themselves or with
their families, provided they have exposure to
attractions and experiences that make it desirable to
return. Conference tourism boosts accommodation
stays, creating additional demand for hospitality
needs. And although the conference center itself
currently has limited staff, there are several direct and
indirect jobs that feed into the conferences, including
event planning, translators, catering, technology and
audio-visual services, marketing and promotion,
entertainment, retail and gift bag preparation, etc. This
expansion of employment opportunities in tourism for
women extends beyond conventional roles, like those
in hotels and restaurants, which are often subject to
stigma. More importantly, the growth of other tourism
segments, such as conference tourism, can help
detract from The Gambia’s lingering sex tourism


reputation and build a tourism segment that is more
professionally oriented and more inviting for women
to participate. Box II highlights how the Kigali



Convention Center became one of Africa’s top
conference destinations, providing insights into best
practices The Gambia could also adopt.







**Eco-tourism is a relatively new but growing segment.**
Spread further upstream of Banjul, the ecolodges offer
quiet retreats in eco-friendly lodging and surroundings.
Eco-tourism offers more environmentally and socially
sustainable practices as they often hire and source
from local women, providing one of the few
employment opportunities in these remote
communities. According to the Rapid Assessment
conducted in the beginning of COVID by the Gambia
Bureau of Statistics, women constitute 43.5 percent of
the ecolodge workforce, higher than hotels where
women make up 30 percent of the workforce.
However, compared to hotels, eco-lodges still
represent a much smaller portion of employment
opportunities. Ecolodges—which are also smaller in
scale—only account for 4.5 percent of the
accommodation establishments in the country,
whereas hotels constitute 28.3 percent [1] .


**Eco-tourism combines well with community-based**
**tourism,** **creating** **additional** **employment**
**opportunities for women, such as birdwatching tours,**
**cooking demos, etc.** This is line with various studies
which show that women could be psychologically,
socially, economically, and politically empowered by
their participation in ecotourism ventures (Samal and
Dash, 2023). Ninki Nakka trail, which offers
community-based tourism in rural communities along



the Gambia River, was designed to promote travel
during the low season, between May and October. Its
focus is on sustainable tourism to benefit host
communities, businesses and the environment.
Gambia Tourism Board has a code of conduct for Ninki
Nakka Trail tour operators—both domestic and
international—including a condition to be an ethical
business with decent employment practices, and to
maintain a policy on gender equality and decent
working conditions.


**Research shows that developing nature-based and**
**sustainable tourism could also help attract high-value**
**tourism.** Recent World Bank consumer research on
three key European outbound markets (Germany,
Sweden, United Kingdom), which are also important
for the Gambian tourism, identified five market
segments amongst travelers (World Bank, 2022b).
Three of these segments, which account for 66 percent
of respondents, focus on nature-based and sustainable
tourism and are the highest spending of all traveler
types. These tourists actively seek distinctive
experiences, aspire to connect with local communities
and enterprises, view travel as a means of education,
and are committed to sustainable practices. Attracting
more of this tourist segments by changing the
marketing strategy and increasing the supply of active,

**On the darker side, unfortunately, The Gambia carries**
**the stigma of being a destination for sex tourism, of**
**both male and female sex workers** . Particularly
notable is its reputation as a hotspot for European
women seeking male companionship, though the
reverse is also prevalent. The Gambia’s reputation as a
sex tourism destination impacts how workers, both
female and male, interact with tourists and
hotels/restaurants and other actors supporting either
tacitly or explicitly this type of tourism. Locally, the
term “bumster” refers to a beach boy, typically a young
man who is a member of the host community who
frequents beaches and hotel areas to engage with
tourists. They offer various tourist activities and
excursions, alongside providing sexual or 'romantic'
services in exchange for monetary gain or other
benefits. Unfortunately, The Gambia's association with
bumsters acts as a deterrent to the growth of the
tourism sector, as highlighted consistently by 70
percent of surveyed tourists (World Bank, 2019b).


**Sex tourism impacts women’s participation and**
**vulnerability in tourism at many levels** . Bumsters
often push for front-facing jobs, such as waiters or
bartenders, to maximize interaction with guests. Not
only does this tend to push women from those jobs,
but also the ill-repute of these kind of workers
prevents women from pursuing them, despite the
higher income potential from tips. Hence, women are
often relegated to lower-paying "behind-the-scenes"
roles like housekeeping, exposing them to potential
predatory behaviors from guests. With some hotels
willing to turn a blind eye or even encourage sex
tourism, this reinforces the stigma of the sector and
lowers women’s participation in it. Senegal, like The

_**Employment issues**_

**Women show strong willingness to participate in the**
**hospitality industry, given their enrollment rates in**
**the tourism and hospitality institute are higher than**
**men, but this is not translating to employment in the**



**hotel industry, due to a combination of key supply**
**and demand-side barriers.** Female labor force
participation remains low due to lack of skills, assets
and networks, time-based constraints, limited
mobility, gender discrimination in hiring and
promotion, and restrictive gender norms (Halim,
O’Sullivan and Sahay, 2023). Key informant interviews
suggest that women are switching to alternative jobs
or self-employment due to an interplay of factors.

**Within the tourism and hospitality sector, women**
**often work restricted hours, potentially leading to a**
**bias in favor of male candidates during the hiring**
**process.** Most businesses have a policy of not hiring
women for shifts after 4 p.m. Though this is not a legal
requirement, it is a de facto practice due to a variety of
cited reasons, including safety and gender norms.
Consequently, this practice may result in a preference
for hiring men who can work both day and night shifts.
Interviews with ecolodges point to better hiring
practices of women, but even in eco-tourism, Gambian
women still face challenges in working late.

**Tourism businesses may be favoring women for part-**
**time rather than full-time work, partly due to**
**maternity policy.** Employers are legally obligated to
provide a 6-month paid maternity leave for full time
female employees, while paid paternity leave is
available up to 10 days. Some interviews suggest that
this disparity leads to a preference for hiring women
for part-time positions or favoring male candidates. In
contrast, in countries like São Tomé and Príncipe,
where social security covers the leave, this bias is
eliminated. The Women, Business and the Law 2023
report lists ‘laws affecting women's work after having
children’ as one area for improvement to improve legal
equality for women in The Gambia. Offering only leave
for mothers, especially above a threshold of 30 weeks,
can be correlated with fewer women in the workforce
(Del Rey, Kyriacou and Silva, 2021) [6] . Studies also show
that a smaller gap between mothers’ and fathers’ leave
is associated with a higher female labor force
participation rate.

**4.2 Gender disparity in Entrepreneurship**


**The MSMEs play a key role in the economy as wage**
**opportunities are scarce in The Gambia.** MSMEs in The
Gambia contribute about 20 percent of GDP and
employ approximately 60 percent of the urban labor
force. Nine in ten businesses are micro businesses, i.e.,
either 1-person businesses or family businesses (World
Bank, 2022c). Moreover, they constitute a crucial
source of work for Gambian youth (18–35 years), who
own more than 40 percent of MSMEs. Approximately,
one in six women and one in four men aged 15 to 64 is
the owner of her/his own business.

**Women own smaller businesses compared to men,**
**employ less people, and have lower profits than male-**
**led businesses in The Gambia.** According to The
Gambia Poverty & Gender Assessment 2022, 83.4
percent of female-led businesses were solo
entrepreneurs compared to 63 percent of male-led
businesses. Nearly 2 percent of female-run businesses
were small, versus 12 percent of male-run businesses.
Less than 1 percent of female-run businesses are
medium or large [7] . The same assessment shows that
median monthly profit was 3,000 GMD for a female-led
business compared to 5,000 GMD for a male-run
business, demonstrating a significant profit gap.

**Among firms which have five or more employees,**
**female** **participation** **in** **ownership** **and** **top**
**management is very low compared to the Sub-**
**Saharan Africa average.** According to World Bank
Enterprise Survey in 2018, 17.9 percent of firms in the
services sector had female participation in ownership
compared to 27.5 percent of firms in SSA. Only 13.1
percent of firms had majority female ownership
compared to 14.1 percent of firms in SSA.

**As the literature shows, gender gap in business**
**performance is attributed to the gender-related**
**limitations that women encounter when initiating,**
**maintaining, and expanding their businesses.** In SubSaharan Africa, women are more likely to be
entrepreneurs than men, and yet their businesses earn
only two-thirds the profits of their male-owned
counterparts (World Bank, 2019a). The significant
gender gap in earnings is not a result of inherent
variations in the skills of male and female
entrepreneurs. Instead, there are multiple underlying
contextual constraints such as legal discrimination,



social norms, and risk of GBV; endowments such as
education/skills, confidence/risk preferences, finance
and assets, and networks and information; and, finally,
household-level constraints such as care and allocation
of factors of production (World Bank, 2019a).

**Increased revenue of tourism related MSMEs is a key**
**country priority as also indicated in the Country**
**Partnership Framework.** The MSMEs within the
sectors of accommodation, food and beverage, and
travel and tours have exhibited a higher level of
economic activity. Notably, over 40 percent of these
MSMEs have reported annual earnings surpassing
GMD 300,000 prior to the onset of the COVID-19
pandemic [8] . Nevertheless, the presence of exclusive
agreements between international tour operators
(ITOs), accommodation providers, and ground tour
operators has limited the potential for numerous
MSMEs to partake in the existing tourism value chain [9] .
Furthermore, the impacts of COVID-19 have rendered
many businesses in the tourism sector susceptible to
concurrent shocks in both supply and demand.

**Among the tourism entrepreneurial activities, women**
**are commonly involved as crafts sellers, beach fruit**
**sellers, tour operators and restaurant owners and**
**contribute indirectly to the tourism sector as**
**vegetable and food suppliers, with their catering,**
**beauty and skin care, and fashion businesses.**
Economic activities in the tourism industry are often
intertwined with other sectors. Transport sector is
heavily male dominated. There is large share of women
participation in the crafts market, however mainly for
beaded jewelry, cloth and baskets production. In
beauty, there is an increasing number of women
entrepreneurs who use local plants/trees of the
country to make oils, such as baobab oil for skin care
and other uses.

_**Key Entrepreneurship Issues**_

_**Skills**_ . The focus group participants raised the fact that
most women do not actually have the basic skills to
make crafts such as sewing, dyeing, weaving—skills
that would allow them to enhance product



diversification and quality. Some craftswomen also
lack the financial and literacy skills to be able to
understand business contracts or to use mobile
money. In addition, other basic skills women need is
literacy support, including financial literacy to
understand business contracts. Digital skills are also
needed to understand how to use mobile money and
social media for new ideas, techniques, and marketing.

_**Access to Networks and Exposure.**_ Even though there
are enabling organizations in the entrepreneurship
ecosystem (Box V), the limited presence of
entrepreneur networks and exposure hinders the
potential for Gambian women entrepreneurs to in the
tourism sector. Firstly, without robust (and funded)
networks, women may lack access to critical resources
such as mentorship, training programs, and financial
support, hindering their ability to start or grow their
businesses. Limited exposure means their offerings
may remain unnoticed by potential customers,
resulting in low sales and profitability. Moreover,
without connections to industry stakeholders and
policymakers, women entrepreneurs may struggle to
navigate regulatory hurdles or advocate for their
interests within the tourism sector. This lack of
visibility also perpetuates gender disparities, as
women's contributions to the tourism industry may go
unrecognized, further marginalizing them from
economic opportunities. In addition, despite its huge
diaspore, there is lack of sufficient connections to the
Gambian diaspora which could provide new ideas and
market opportunities. Increased exposure on national
and international platforms would allow women
entrepreneurs to attract a broader audience of tourists
(across the different segments), diversify their
customer base, and generate sustainable income.
Networking can also facilitate collaboration among
women-owned businesses, enabling them to create
unique tourism experiences and packages that
showcase the richness of Gambian culture and
heritage.


_**Access to Markets.**_ While it is important to enhance
the skills of women entrepreneurs, several key
informant interviews raised a fundamental question:
"What should women diversify their products into?"
The challenge lies in the limited variety of markets and
tourism segments available to showcase local
products, as well as the weak connections to hotels,
supermarkets, and online platforms. From a tourist's
perspective, it is exceedingly challenging to discover
these businesses. Hotels seldom feature local
products, lack on-site stores, and do not incorporate
local items in guest rooms, such as soaps, bedding, or
décor accessories. Food sellers and businesses on
beauty products have a difficult time selling to
supermarkets. One participant expressed her
frustration, having made numerous attempts without
receiving any feedback, leaving her uncertain about
what improvements to make. By not featuring locallyproduced products, hotels and shops hinder the
opportunity for developing a “Gambian brand” tourism
identity, similar to São Tomé and Príncipe’s chocolate
products, or Ghana’s shea butter products. Moreover,
vendor space is an issue as stalls in the crafts centers
are already allocated and rarely change “ownership”,
as a result entry of new participants is rare in the crafts
center, which impedes the introduction of new craft
ideas and innovative competition to build a more
robust and diverse crafts sector.

_**Access to Finance.**_ Financing is an issue for all micro,
small and medium enterprises. According to the World
Bank’s Enterprise Survey in The Gambia, 72 percent of
small and 40 percent of medium-size firms cite access
to finance as a major constraint to their growth. High
collateral requirements, high interest rates and
difficult documentation requirements are some of the
underlying barriers noted by the entrepreneurs.
However, women are often more disadvantaged due
to underlying gender gaps such as in collateral, which
is needed to secure loans at the banks. For example,
in 2020, 40.6 percent of men owned a dwelling either



alone or jointly, compared with just 16.8 percent for
women. Women have lower literacy rates and face
informational barriers to submit loan applications. In
addition to bank loans, there is some grant availability,
but women lack information and support to apply.
They mostly rely on microfinance and informal
channels such as “osusu”, a money pooling system
among a group of individuals, which is particularly
popular among small female business owners to access
money without interest.

**4.3 Leadership and Decision-Making**

**The underrepresentation of women in decision-**
**making and leadership roles in The Gambia has**
**profound implications for the tourism sector,**
**particularly in terms of perpetuating sexual stigma**
**and hindering women's economic participation.**
Women’s participation in decision-making and
leadership remains low in The Gambia. While the
United Nations at the 1995 Beijing Conference on
[Women set a gender quota of 30 percent for women](https://www.un.org/womenwatch/daw/beijing/platform/decision.htm)
[representation in decision-making processes, the](https://www.un.org/womenwatch/daw/beijing/platform/decision.htm)
actual representation of women in leadership roles
falls far below this standard. Women constitute only
8.6 percent of the parliament while 91 percent of the
parliamentarians is men, ranking The Gambia 168 out
of 186 countries worldwide. This falls behind the SubSaharan Africa average as well as the world average [10] .
At the executive level, only three of the 22 Ministers in
the current Cabinet are women, at the local level eight
out of 120 councilors are female, and among
traditional council leadership only 13 of the 1,887
Alkali [11] are female. Women represent 57 percent of
the registered voters but are not often on the ballot
and are likely to participate as party organizers and
mobilizers, rather than as candidates. Often, when a
woman and a man want to run for the same political
party, the woman is asked to step aside to make room
for the man (UN, 2022). The scarcity of women in

prioritizing and implementing legislative reforms and

**As of this writing, none of the leading tourism**

**women, though women have presence at other**

opportunities for women’s leadership than other
sectors of the economy, none of the key public tourism
players such as Gambia Tourism Board, GTHI, and
NCAC are currently headed by women. On the private
sector side, female representation has been better.
There is some female presence among General

hotels are led by women. Aside from GMs, women are
often managers in back-office positions such as
accounting and sales. Additionally, the Gambia Hotel

the shortage of women in leadership roles within
tourism associations and hotels complicates the
prioritization and implementation of systemic changes

Nevertheless, change is feasible and ongoing. One
female General Manager interviewed, emphasized

**to keeping women from leadership positions.** In The
Gambia, about three-quarters (74 percent) of citizens
say women should have the same chance as men to
run for political office. Especially economically betteroff citizens (86 percent) and those with post-secondary

this view. However, 60 percent of Gambians think that
if a woman runs for a public office, she will be likely
criticized or harassed by others in the community, and
40 percent think she will probably face problems with
her family (Afrobarometer, 2023). Cultural phenomena
have created the stereotype ‘impression that men are
superior, and women are inferior’ and a ‘socialization
process [that] has also led men and women to perceive
men as leaders and women as supporters.’ These
perceptions are alleged to “put women in the private
sphere as wives, mothers and daughters and men in
the public domain of decision-making”, as well as the
economic and political arena.

**Key informant interviews in The Gambia suggest that**
**the presence of more women in upper hotel**
**management helps other women staff feel more**
**confident in approaching them with more sensitive**
**issues, e.g., harassment.** The presence of women in
these influential roles not only serves as a source of
inspiration for other female employees but also
creates a more inclusive and approachable
atmosphere within the workplace. More effectively, all
tourism businesses should have explicit policies and
training against SEA/SH, with clear measures for
sanctions, a safe and confidential grievance
mechanism, access to GBV services, and a safe
whistleblower policy.


### **Cross-Cutting Issues**

**The Tourism Ecosystem Assessment examined how**
**The Gambia is impacted and influenced by the**
**country's** **policy** **and** **institutional** **framework,**
**educational endeavors, and social norms, including**
**the challenging issue of GBV.** Within this framework,
various stakeholders including governmental bodies,
educational institutions, and civil society organizations
play pivotal roles in shaping the trajectory of the
tourism sector and addressing pertinent genderrelated issues. Current legislation is wanting in terms
of better protecting women in the sector. In parallel,
initiatives in tourism education seek to equip women
with the necessary skills for meaningful participation
and leadership roles within the industry. However,
entrenched social norms continue to pose barriers,
reinforcing traditional gender roles and perpetuating
inequalities. Moreover, the pervasive issue of GBV
underscores the urgent need for comprehensive
interventions and support structures within the
tourism sector. This overview delves into the
interconnected dynamics of policy, education, social
norms, and GBV, shedding light on the challenges and
opportunities for promoting gender equity and
inclusivity in The Gambia's tourism landscape.


**5.1 Policy and Institutional Context**


**In The Gambia, institutions involved in the**
**formulation and implementation of tourism policy**
**play a vital role in promoting the country's tourism**
**sector and ensuring sustainable development.** Four
key institutions include Ministry of Tourism and
Culture, Gambia Tourism Board, National Council for
Arts and Culture, Gambia Hotel Association along with
various private sector entities. On the gender side, key
institutions, such as the Ministry of Gender, Children
and Social Welfare, play a pivotal role in formulating
and implementing gender-responsive policies and key
policy documents such as the Gender and
Empowerment Strategy guide the priorities on gender
issues. The gender strategy is currently being revised
for the 2023-2033 period and has a strong list of action



items for the elimination of all forms of sexual and
GBV.


**Stronger legislation and rigorous implementation of**
**safety protocols are imperative to cultivate a secure**
**and empowering working environment for women in**
**the tourism industry, fostering their participation and**
**advancement** **free** **from** **harassment** **and**
**discrimination** . The WBL report highlights that The
Gambia needs improvement enacting legislation
protecting women from sexual harassment and
adopting criminal or civil penalties for sexual
harassment in employment. According to WBL, which
examines the legal and regulatory environment faced
by women in various countries in relation to their
economic participation, The Gambia scores 69.4 out of
100, which is lower than the regional average observed
across Sub-Saharan Africa (74). Gambia’s lowest scores
are within laws concerning workplace, where it scores
50 out 100, which is also lower than SSA average (81.8).
The analysis shows that there is currently no legislation
on sexual harassment in employment or no criminal
penalties or civil remedies for sexual harassment in
employment. And more broadly, there is no legislation
on domestic violence, femicide or comprehensive
legislation on sexual harassment. In addition to
legislative frameworks, in the WBL 2024 report added
new indicators on supportive frameworks, here
Gambia scored a paltry 16.7 out of 100, lower than the
SSA average of 24.5, doing particularly poorly on
supportive interventions to ensure women's safety.
The report recommended that The Gambia consider
developing comprehensive mechanisms to address
violence against women, putting in place special
procedures for cases of sexual harassment, designating
a government entity responsible for the monitoring
and implementation of national services, plans and
programs addressing violence against women,
providing an annual budgetary allocation towards
violence against women risk mitigation and prevention
and response programs (World Bank, 2024) [12] .

**Many women in The Gambia have limited access to**

suggests that more and better-quality investments in

**In the enabling business environment, there is high**
**prevalence of informality, high tax rates and complex**
**business regulations (World Bank, 2019b).** Most firms
in The Gambia are informal (70 percent of MSMEs),
with women-owned businesses more likely to be
smaller and informal. The key regulatory bodies,
statutory requirements and associated conditions
governing the operations of tourism MSMEs are the
Gambia Tourism Board, Gambia Chamber of
Commerce and Industry (GCCI) and to some extent the
Gambia Investment & Export Promotion Agency
(GIEPA). For women-led businesses, Gambia Women’s
Chamber of Commerce support firms to formalize and
grow.



**The 2018 Enterprise Survey identified access to**
**finance as the main business environment obstacle**
**facing firms in The Gambia.** Limited financial history
and collateral, coupled with an informal operational
structure, pose significant barriers to securing loans
from traditional financial institutions. The absence of
robust credit information systems further compounds
this issue, making it difficult for lenders to accurately
assess the creditworthiness of MSMEs. Additionally,
high interest rates, stringent lending requirements,
and a lack of tailored financial products for the diverse
needs of these enterprises hinder their ability to obtain
necessary funding. As a result, many Gambians still rely
on informal channels such as family members and
savings groups to access finance. Efforts to increase
firm productivity and growth will also need to focus on
improving access to finance, the biggest perceived
constraint to doing business in The Gambia.


**Most actors in the tourism ecosystem collect very**
**limited sex-disaggregated data, which poses a**
**significant impediment to recognizing the challenges**
**encountered by women and developing sectoral**
**targeted interventions.** As UNTWO points out, there
appears to be a strong correlation between gendersensitive policies on the one hand, and women’s ability
to thrive in tourism employment and leadership, on
the other. However, the first step is to understand the
key challenges women face to formulate interventions.
There are opportunities for key organizations such as
Gambia Tourism Board, GTHI to collect targeted data
to document the gender disparities, identify
opportunity areas and track progress.


**5.2 Tourism Education**


**In line with global trends, women are enrolled more**
**than men in the tourism and hospitality school with**


**the gender gap declining in the more advanced**
**programs.** The GTHI is the main actor in the country to
train youth for the tourism sector and the majority of
the student body is female. GTHI offers foundation,
certificate, and diploma programs in three areas - room
division, food and beverage, and professional cooking.
According to GTHI data, professional cooking is the
most popular program, and women often establish
catering businesses after. Participation in the diploma
program, which also provides managerial training, is
comparatively small but the gender gap in
participation is also much smaller compared to other
lower degree programs.


**Hotels choose most of their workforce from GTHI, but**
**it primarily serves the “workforce” level on**
**hospitality, but the tourism aspect is underexplored.**
The Institute of Travel and Tourism of The Gambia
‘ITTOG’ is another institute in the tourism ecosystem,
offering courses on travel and tourism. However, there
are untapped prospects to expand school offerings to
encompass subjects like eco-tourism and events
management, addressing the growing facets of the
tourism industry.


**As the United Nations World Tourism Organization**
**(UNWTO) points out, while women are the majority**
**of students in formal tourism education, they largely**
**lack the high-level and soft skills training needed to**
**ensure their career progression, pointing to an**
**opportunity for new tourism curriculum development**
**in The Gambia.** There is a large body of literature which
shows the impact of training programs which goes
beyond business training and encompasses soft skills,
network building and other key skills, alongside the
high-level training. Currently, there is a lack of new
curriculum development and investment in managerial
programs, which encompass soft skills training which
could benefit female students. As UNWTO report
points out, management strategies in the hospitality
industry have been found to lack a focus on women as
human capital, as reflected in women’s limited
representation among management and senior
positions in the tourism sector – potentially
underscored by few women participating in high-level
training (World Tourism Organization, 2019).


**Key informant interviews reveal gaps in a few key**
**areas such as sex-disaggregated data collection,**
**sensitization of youth earlier in the education system**
**and high-costs of tourism education.** Notably, The
GTHI currently ceases alumni tracking after six months,



presenting an opportunity for enhanced data
collection and an imperative for monitoring students’
post-work placement and program effectiveness.
Furthermore, discussions with hotel managers
underscore the importance of sensitizing students
about tourism at an earlier stage, possibly during
secondary education, and crafting curricula that can
foster the development of youth, thereby augmenting
their future human capital and economic prospects.
Lastly, the gender imbalance in hotel hiring practices,
which predominantly favor men, leads to
dissatisfaction among women, especially given that
hospitality training is often self-funded and, without
subsequent job placements, may be perceived as an
expenditure with little return on investment.


**5.3 Social Norms**


**Social norms in The Gambia significantly impact**
**women's economic participation in the tourism**
**sector,** **especially** **considering** **the** **country's**
**reputation for sex tourism.** The pervasive stigma
associated with sex tourism perpetuates harmful
stereotypes about the illicit nature of those working in
it and discourages families from encouraging or
supporting their daughters or wives from joining it,
even for innocuous hotel administrative jobs, such as
accountancy. As a result, the gender employment gap
in tourism is quite significant at 28 percent (Table 3),
and much higher than the labor force participation gap
of 8 percent (World Bank Gender Data Portal, 2022).


**Although the stigma is still a barrier, attitudes have**
**seen growing acceptance for women in the tourism**
**and hospitality sector.** Social acceptability of jobs
within the tourism and hospitality sector remains a
barrier in women’s participation in the sector.
However, the remarkably high levels of female student
enrollment in tourism institutes signal an amelioration
in attitudes and viewpoints. Additionally, the
interviews suggest that women are increasingly staying
on to work after getting married and having children in
the sector, whereas that was less the case before.


**The sector is also starting to change to be more**
**women-friendly, though there is still need for**
**systemic change.** Some hotels interviewed provide
night transport for their employees, which then allows
them to hire women for evening positions in hotel
restaurants/bars, which are normally occupied by
men. Hotel associations and industry leaders should
also be pushing their members to make the industry


more women-friendly. While some establishments
have policies, such as code of conduct, to protect staff
against harassment, these larger hotels are in the
minority. Having a code of conduct requires regular
training, enforcement, and implementing grievance
mechanisms, which are not costs that currently have a
high priority. To encourage uptake, some of these
costs, such as training, could be shared with the public
sector and/or other tourism establishments, or
through the implementation of a more systemic
grievance reporting and redress.


**Engaging communities, and particularly men and local**
**leaders, could be key to overcome the stigma and**
**address** **restrictive** **social** **norms.** Effectively
addressing these entrenched social norms necessitates
a multifaceted approach that involves engaging
various stakeholders, including men, communities,
religious leaders, and women elders. By fostering
dialogue and promoting awareness, these efforts can
challenge existing biases and promote gender equality
within the tourism sector. Addressing restrictive social
norms and stigma requires a comprehensive effort that
involves changing attitudes, behaviors, and
perceptions within communities. In the context of
tourism, where gender roles and expectations can
influence various aspects of the industry, involving
men, women, and boys in behavior change initiatives
can have a transformative impact. There are several
World Bank projects which focus on behaviour change
and addressing social norms, involving women, men,
and boys, which could also be critical for any tourism
intervention. By leveraging these experiences,
interventions designed to promote gender equity and
inclusivity within the Gambia's tourism sector, could
ultimately foster a more vibrant and sustainable
industry.


_**Gender-Based Violence (GBV)**_


**GBV in the travel and tourism sector is embedded**
**within the broader societal frameworks.** Gambians
rank GBV as the second-most important women’srights issue that the government and society must
address. Additionally, as per the expert opinion survey
conducted by WBL 2024, 44 percent of respondents
indicated that no women are exempt from
experiencing GBV. This is similar to the world average
but lower than some higher income countries in the
region, such as Kenya, where the Intimate Partner
Violence (IPV) rate is 38 percent. According to the
2019-20 Demographic and Health Survey, 9 percent of



women between ages 15 to 49 in The Gambia have
ever experienced sexual violence, while almost 40
percent of ever-married women have been subjected
to either physical, sexual or emotional violence by their
current or most recent partners. The survey further
found that about 51 percent of women and 35 percent
of men in The Gambia agree that wife battering is
acceptable if a wife burns the food; argues with her
husband; goes out without telling her husband;
neglects the children; or refuses to have sexual
relations with her husband.


**Within the tourism industry, sex trafficking and sexual**
**exploitation are still prevalent and affects both**
**women and girls as well as men and boys.** Women in
hotels can also face harassment from both colleagues
as well as guests. The Ministry of Gender, Children and
Social Welfare, the Ministry of Health and Civil Society
Organizations have been working to improve the
existing GBV response structures and ensure that
information about GBV and care services to support
survivors are available and accessible. The agency has
been investing in strengthening the existing
mechanism, including supporting the establishment of
a National GBV Helpline 1313, construction and
renovation of One-Stop Centres in major health
facilities across the country, among others. However,
there is still need for dedicated attention to the
offences in the tourism industry. For example, there is
no dedicated hotline for these kind of offences within
the tourism sector.


**Norms both impact the prevention of GBV and the**
**uptake of GBV services.** There is limited availability
and reliability of GBV response services, combined
with significant social and cultural barriers to reporting
GBV, is reflected in low service seeking behavior by
survivors. In The Gambia, almost two-thirds (63
percent) of respondents consider it likely that a woman
who reports being a victim of rape, domestic violence,
or other GBV will be criticized, harassed, or shamed by
others in the community, including 44 percent who say
this is “very likely”. There is also a strong social
consideration called “maslaha” to not report
harassers, so as not to get them fired and hurt their
family.
