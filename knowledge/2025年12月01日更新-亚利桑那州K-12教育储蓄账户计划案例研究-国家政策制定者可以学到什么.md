## **About This Report**

In this report, we examine Arizona’s kindergarten through grade 12 (K–12) Education Savings
Account (ESA) program, known as Empowerment Scholarship Accounts. Arizona’s ESA policy is the
oldest of the 19 states that now have ESAs. We explore Arizona’s ESA program’s growth, who uses
the ESA program under different eligibility schemes, how much the ESA program costs, how families
use ESA funds, and the private education sector’s responses to the ESA policy. Using the insights
gained from the case study in Arizona, we offer key recommendations tied to each thematic chapter of
the report for other states considering implementing ESA policies or expanding existing ESA policies.

## **Summary**

#### **Issue**

Kindergarten through grade 12 (K–12) Education Savings Accounts (ESAs) are state-managed
accounts that give families with school-aged children public funds to spend on their child’s education if
they opt not to enroll them in public school. As of the 2025–2026 school year, 19 states have ESA
programs, up from four in 2019. However, there is very little empirical research on these programs.
Arizona has the longest-standing ESA program—the Empowerment Scholarship Account
program—which first enrolled students in the 2011–2012 school year and has seen multiple eligibility
expansions over the past 14 years. The various iterations of Arizona’s ESA policy offer an opportunity
for insights about how ESA policy design relates to use of ESA programs in practice. Learning from
Arizona is particularly important in light of national policies that will further expand ESAs. Beginning
in the 2026–2027 school year, the national school choice tax credit established in the One Big
Beautiful Bill Act will give states the option to offer tax credits to taxpayers that contribute to
scholarship organizations supporting families paying for private educational expenses (Pub. L. 119-21,
2025).

#### **Approach**


In this report, we examine Arizona’s K–12 ESA policy along the following dimensions:


  - how eligibility policies have shaped enrollment in the ESA program over time (Chapter 2)

  - to what extent students with disabilities use ESAs (Chapter 3)

  - which families and students use ESAs under the universal policy (Chapter 4)

  - how much the ESA program costs and how families use ESA funds (Chapter 5)

  - how private educational service providers have responded to the ESA policy (Chapter 6).


We use publicly available information on Arizona’s ESA program from quarterly reports released
by the Arizona Department of Education (ADE) since 2021, along with other sources of public data
(including the American Community Survey, Stanford Education Data Archive, Private School
Universe Survey, and other reports released by ADE), to document trends and conduct descriptive
regression analyses.

Using the insights gained from the case study in Arizona, we offer recommendations for other
states considering implementing ESA policies or expanding existing ESA policies. The primary
limitations of this report relate to data availability: The data that ADE reports on ESA program
participation are aggregated, and the exact tables included in the ADE quarterly reports change over
time. These two features of the primary source of available program data shape the analyses that we
were able to do and the inferences that we were able to draw about the program.

#### **Key Findings**


  - Prior to universal eligibility, Arizona’s ESA program grew from serving 144 students in the
2011–2012 school year to almost 12,000 students in the 2021–2022 school year. The
universal ESA policy expansion in 2022–2023 led to a further sevenfold increase in program
participation to nearly 90,000 students in 2024–2025. Existing privately educated students—
including private school and homeschooled students—make up an estimated 71 percent of
universal enrollees.

  - After adjusting for inflation, Arizona’s spending on ESAs has gone from $2.2 million in the
2011–2012 school year to $886 million in 2024–2025. This represents roughly 10 percent of
the state’s Department of Education budget of about $9 billion (Arizona Auditor General,
2025).

  - Approximately 7 percent of Arizona’s school-aged population uses ESAs as of the 2024–2025
school year. Growing ESA usage could fundamentally change how Arizona uses public dollars
for education, with implications for public schools that can expect to continue losing
enrollment.

  - Prior to Arizona’s ESA eligibility expansion, ESAs were targeted toward specific student
groups. Students with disabilities were the largest user group, making up 60 percent of ESA
users in the 2021–2022 school year. Since universal eligibility was implemented, students with
disabilities now make up 18 percent of ESA users. In the 2024–2025 school year, spending on
universal enrollees overtook spending for students with disabilities.

  - ESA participants are not currently representative of Arizona’s student population: ESA users
tend to come from school districts that have higher achievement levels, serve students from
more-affluent backgrounds, and have larger White populations, on average.

  - Families spend roughly 60 percent of ESA dollars on private school tuition, but a growing
portion goes toward other education services, such as curriculum materials, tutoring, and
other educational services, consistent with an unbundled model of education.

  - Arizona’s ESA permits rollovers, allowing families to accumulate ESA funds over time. As of
the 2023–2024 school year, 28 percent of ESA funds that have been awarded have not been
spent.

  - Arizona’s ESA program has induced growth in the education marketplace: The number of
vendors on Arizona’s online education marketplace, ClassWallet, increased from 1,339 in
2021 to 6,091 by 2024—more than quadrupling over the period—and the estimated number
of private schools in Arizona has increased by 14 percent (from 451 to 515) since 2022.

  - Since the universal ESA policy was implemented in the 2022–2023 school year, private
elementary school and high school tuition rates have grown by 12 percent and 5 percent,
respectively, although these increases are not definitively in response to universal ESAs.

#### **Recommendations for Other States Considering ESAs**


  - States should consider what goals they are trying to achieve by implementing an ESA policy
and design their ESA program accordingly. If the goal is to provide alternative educational


v


opportunities for students who do not have access to appropriate public education options
(e.g., students with disabilities whose local public schools might not be able to support special
learning needs), then a targeted program might be advisable. If the goal is to allow public funds
to follow students, regardless of whether or not they are already enrolled in private school,
then universal ESA programs might be fitting.

- States implementing universal ESA programs should conduct targeted outreach to families
who could benefit but might otherwise be less likely to learn about and use ESAs. Families of
children with disabilities or low-income families in underperforming school districts might
benefit yet might not enroll without outreach.

- States implementing universal ESA programs should budget their programs with the
expectation that a significant share of first adopters will likely be students already enrolled in
private schools or homeschooled.

- To increase predictability of state budgets, states should consider implementing a rule that
requires families to spend their ESA allocations within each calendar year rather than roll
funds over from one year to the next.

- States should monitor whether private schools raise tuition in response to ESA programs and
consider policies to safeguard against tuition hikes.

- States with ESA programs should consider using digital wallets and online platforms to
reduce the administrative burden of running ESA programs, streamline the process for
families making purchases with their ESA funds, and reduce the opportunity for fraudulent
spending.

- States should implement regular reporting on ESA financing and student outcomes to ensure
that programs remain transparent. Documentation of program targets and associated
outcomes allows all stakeholders—from parents to educational leaders, policymakers, and
researchers—to identify areas of strength and opportunities for improvement.

## **Policy Background on Arizona’s** **Empowerment Scholarship Accounts**


Arizona has the longest-standing kindergarten through grade 12 (K–12) Education Savings
Account (ESA) policy in the country. Arizona’s Empowerment Scholarship Accounts give families
with school-aged children public funds to spend on their child’s education if they opt not to enroll
them in Arizona’s public schools. [1] The program was introduced in 2011, with the first students
enrolling in the 2011–2012 school year (fiscal year [FY] 2012). It has seen numerous policy
expansions since. The multiple iterations of the ESA program create a unique opportunity to explore
different ESA policy designs, many of which mirror approaches used by other states implementing
ESA programs. Since 2021, Arizona has released quarterly reports on the program, making it one of
the more-transparent programs in existence. Although there are no student outcome data included in
these publicly available reports, the aggregated data on program participation, usage, and costs over
time allow for a useful case study that other states can learn from.

#### **Student Eligibility and Expansion**


When the ESA program was first established in 2011, it was a targeted program made available to
students with disabilities. Families of students eligible for supports under the Individuals with
Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) and those who qualify for Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of
1973 were eligible to open an ESA (Arizona Revised Statutes, Title 15, Section 15-2401, 2011). The
ESA program therefore allowed students with special educational needs to seek out alternative
education opportunities outside the public school system to ensure that they received an appropriate
education. Families could receive up to 90 percent of the funding that states would have allocated to
their local public school district through the ESA (Arizona Revised Statutes, Title 15, Section 152402, 2011).
For the next ten years, eligibility to open an ESA expanded to additional student populations but
was still targeted to only some groups of students. These changing policies are shown in Figure 1.1. In
2012, the Arizona State Legislature expanded eligibility for ESAs to students attending schools that
had school quality ratings of D or F—those deemed low by the state accountability system. Students

#### **Budget and Operations**


Arizona’s ESA program has evolved in terms of its budget and operations as it has expanded. As
of 2025, Arizona families apply online and are eligible for up to 90 percent of the funding that states
would have allocated to a recipient’s public school district (Arizona Revised Statutes, Title 15, Section
15-2402, 2024). In addition, as of 2025, the program awarded $886 million in ESA funds for
students, representing roughly 10 percent of the $9 billion that Arizona budgeted for pre-K–12
education (State of Arizona, 2024). Once ADE approves their application, families can access ESA
funds through a digital wallet and make purchases automatically through an online marketplace of
approved education vendors. [2] Families are not required to use all funds each year, and unused funds
roll over from one year to the next (Arizona Revised Statutes, Title 15, Section 15-2402, Subsection
H, 2024; see also Chapter 5). Arizona will approve “any reasonable education-related expenses,” such
as tuition for private schools, textbooks, curriculum materials for homeschooling, tutoring or teaching
services, extracurricular activities, and other supplemental educational materials (ADE, undated-e).

#### **Arizona’s ESA Policy in Context**


K–12 ESAs exist within the backdrop of a larger school choice policy ecosystem that has evolved
since the 1990s. Broadly speaking, there are two types of school choice programs. One allows students
to move between publicly funded schools (e.g., charter schools, magnet schools, interdistrict or
intradistrict open enrollment). The other directs public funding to private schools, often including
parochial schools (e.g., school vouchers, tax-credit scholarships, ESAs). ESAs are the newest and most
flexible school choice policy. Although most families spend their allocated ESA funding on private
school tuition, ESA funding can also be used on other educational expenses outside private schools.
Thus, ESAs can support the unbundled education model, in which parents select resources and services
from different providers to meet their students’ tuition and nontuition educational needs; in contrast,
school vouchers typically require that public funds be spent on private school tuition at approved
schools.

School choice programs—in all forms—are on the rise nationally. Nearly one in five public school
students attend a chosen public school instead of their neighborhood-assigned school (National
Center for Education Statistics [NCES], 2022). Enrollment in charter schools nearly doubled from

## **How Does Eligibility Shape** **Enrollment?**


Arizona’s ESA program has grown from serving 144 students in 2012 to serving nearly 88,000
students—approximately 7 percent of the state’s school-aged population—in 2025. Figure 2.1 shows
ESA enrollment over time, with each dashed vertical line indicating a year when eligibility for the
program was expanded to additional groups of students. For the first 11 years of the program, prior to
universal eligibility, enrollment grew steadily to roughly 12,000 students as eligibility criteria gradually
expanded access to ESAs to more students. The universal policy, which first enrolled students in the
2022–2023 school year, led to a noticeable departure from a steady growth trend: 49,000 new
students used ESAs in 2023, nearly quintupling the number of participants in a single year. Two years
into the universal policy, Arizona’s ESA program has grown sevenfold relative to enrollment before
universal eligibility, and it continues on an upward trajectory. Seventy-four percent of the 84,000 ESA
users in 2025 were not eligible before universal eligibility was implemented. Increases in ESA
enrollment have occurred amid a declining school-aged population in Arizona, from 1.15 million in
2019–2020 to 1.12 million in 2024–2025 (NCES, undated-b), suggesting that the growth reflects
shifts away from other school options into the ESA program rather than general increases in the
student population overall.

In every year before universal eligibility, students with disabilities were the majority of ESA
recipients. In 2021, the first year that enrollment data by all eligibility categories was released, 59
percent of ESA recipients were students with disabilities, 15 percent were siblings of current or former
recipients with a disability, and 11 percent were children in active-duty military families. The
remaining eligibility categories made up less than 5 percent of enrollment. Universal eligibility created
a marked shift in the composition of the ESA program, with universal enrollees making up almost 80
percent of enrollees in 2025. The rate of enrollment growth is also much greater for students enrolled
under universal eligibility than for students in all other eligibility categories, suggesting that these
enrollment differences might continue to widen.

#### **ESA Enrollment Versus Charter School Enrollment in Arizona**


It might be helpful to think about potential growth in ESA participation by assessing how other
school choice programs that are available to all families in Arizona have evolved over time. As a

#### **How Families Use ESA Funds**


Families spend most of their allocated ESA funding on tuition, although they spend a significant
portion on a variety of unbundled education services. K–12 ESA programs are often compared with
voucher systems, which, like ESAs, allow families to spend public dollars on private education.
However, voucher policies typically stipulate that funds be used to pay for only private school tuition
at approved schools. ESAs allow families additional flexibility for educational spending beyond private
school tuition, allowing parents to customize educational experiences for their children based on their
learning needs. Proponents have argued that the opportunity to unbundle education through flexible
spending is one of the potential benefits of ESAs, although to what extent this occurs in practice has
been an open question.

Although most Arizona ESA recipients use the ESA funds for private school tuition—similar to a
school voucher system—a substantial share of Arizona ESA recipients allocate them to other eligible
uses, as shown in Figure 5.3. In fall 2024, families spent nearly 60 percent ($120 million in 2025
dollars) of ESA awards on tuition-related expenses (shaded in blue); however, the remaining 40

of ESAs. Within tuition-related expenses, most families (86 percent) spent ESA funds on tuition,

## **How Have Private Education** **Providers Responded to ESAs?**


Arizona’s universal ESA expansion in 2022 created conditions to observe how education markets
respond to large-scale increases in publicly funded private school choice. The new funding could foster
a competitive marketplace in which families can vote with their feet for education services. The
flexibility afforded to families regarding how to use ESA funds creates an opportunity not just to open
new schools but for providers to offer specific educational services that can flourish following the
unbundled model of education described in Chapter 5.

However, the large influx of funding could also lead existing providers to raise prices to capture
additional funding—without facing immediate competitive pressure to improve quality. In a study of
the impact of Iowa and Nebraska ESA programs, researchers found that tuition increased between 10
and 25 percent depending on level of eligibility and grade (Fontana and Jennings, 2024). Other
research on K–12 school vouchers suggests that voucher funds have led to 5- to 10-percent increases
in private school tuition (Harris and Olivier, 2025). This hypothesis has also been raised and
empirically studied in the higher education sector, where the concern is that institutions eligible for
federal aid could raise tuition above the cost of education to capture profits (Bennett, 1987; Cellini
and Goldin, 2014; Cooper, 2025).

We use publicly available data on education vendors using Arizona’s online marketplace, private
schools, and private school tuitions in Arizona to explore these various potential responses by private
education service providers to ESAs in Arizona.

#### **Growth in the Education Vendor Marketplace**


Arizona’s ESA program has created a growing marketplace of education vendors. Arizona uses
ClassWallet, a platform that offers an online marketplace for ESA transactions that several other
states also use, including Indiana, Missouri, New Hampshire, and North Carolina (ClassWallet,
2024). The platform allows families to purchase educational services directly from approved vendors,
streamlining the payment process compared with earlier reimbursement systems used in voucher
programs. The number of ESA-approved ClassWallet vendors has grown from 1,339 active vendors
in the 2020–2021 school year to more than 6,000 vendors in 2023–2024—a more than fourfold
increase in just three years. Upticks in vendor count align with major program expansions, including
the universal ESA rollout in 2022, with the number of ClassWallet vendors nearly doubling from

to the increase in demand. [10]

The growth of the vendor marketplace enables families to purchase individual educational services
rather than bundled school offerings. For example, according to ClassWallet approved expense data,
spending was concentrated in three main categories: Supplemental materials accounted for
approximately 39 percent of total expenditures, followed by computer hardware and technological devices
at 30 percent and curriculum at 19 percent (ADE, undated-f). This spending breakdown illustrates
how the ESA program allows families to combine services from multiple providers rather than

## **Discussion and Concluding** **Recommendations**


With the longest-standing ESA program, Arizona has been a pioneer in the ESA policy space for
more than a decade. Arizona’s program has undergone a series of expansions, making it a helpful case
study for other states considering introducing or expanding their ESA programs.

Perhaps the single-largest ESA policy choice facing states is whether to target it to only certain
categories of students or, as Arizona has done, to open eligibility to all. Universal eligibility in Arizona
has caused significant increases in family uptake, ESA vendors, and state costs. To help states navigate
this decision, we outline different goals for ESA programs matched with the program design that
would be suitable to achieve such goals (Table 7.1). We also provide considerations that states should
make when implementing either of these types of programs, acknowledging that program specifics can
vary.

In addition to the recommendations that we have made throughout this report, a final overarching
recommendation relates to data collection, infrastructure, and transparency. This report was made
possible because Arizona releases regular reports with aggregated information on program
participation and other aspects of the ESA program. We recommend continuing and expanding this
sort of data transparency in the ESA policy landscape for three reasons.


  - **High-quality information for families spurs higher-quality schools.** Effective market
solutions require low-cost access to high-quality information. For school choice policies to
yield the potential benefits of market competition, such as lower costs and higher-quality
schooling, families need to be able to make informed choices about the education options
available to them. Access to reliable and comparable information on education service
providers—including private schools and those offering specific services—across a variety of
metrics is critical to this process, allowing families to effectively vote with their feet and design
educational experiences that benefit their children.

  - **Transparency drives improvement.** Accountability is critical to the success of any
educational program. Documentation of program targets and associated outcomes allows all
stakeholders—from parents to educational leaders, policymakers, and researchers—to identify
areas of strength and opportunities for improvement. Transparency is also a basic necessity of
government programs to ensure that programs are making efficient use of public monies.

  - **Public access to user-friendly data encourages best practices.** The goal of this report was to
leverage the insights gained from Arizona’s ESA policy to share best practices with other
states considering similar policies. This sort of learning across contexts is made possible
because Arizona releases regular reports. Such learning could be enhanced and multiplied if
more states followed similar practices, creating opportunities for states to learn from each
other, experiment with new and innovative policy designs, and share best practices. For this to
be most effective, reporting should be regular, use consistent metrics, and be presented in
analyst-friendly formats. This reporting should include comparable student outcomes for ESA
and non-ESA students to allow better understanding of the impacts of these policies on
students. Ideally, states would invest in data infrastructure—as is the norm for public K–12
administrative data systems—to create longitudinal datasets that would allow for the richest
insights about program impacts.

## **Data and Methods**


This report uses data from multiple sources. The primary data sources are quarterly reports
published by ADE. Throughout the ESA program’s history, Arizona has produced various publicly
available reports and audits that track and assess the program. Since 2021, Arizona has released
quarterly reports as required by statute. The reports provide various aggregated data on program
participation and other program features, including enrollment counts broken down by eligibility
category, grade level, disability type, award amount, and local school district. These regular reports
provide the bulk of the data used in this report. We digitized and linked data across reports, allowing
us to explore trends over time. Additionally, we used a 2016 Performance Audit conducted by the
Auditor General and its accompanying follow-up report released 24 months later (Arizona Auditor
General, 2016; Prothero, 2018). We also used a special audit report conducted in 2020 and two
follow-up reports released 36 and 48 months later (Arizona Auditor General, 2020; Arizona Auditor
General, 2023; Arizona Auditor General, 2024). For the reporting categories that are consistent over
time, we have built time series datasets that allow us to track changes to the ESA program over time.
For example, the table that reports ESA program participation over time has been included in every
report since 2021.

Some of the tables have changed or have had definitions adjusted over time, making comparisons
over time infeasible. For example, between 2021 and 2024, the reports included information on which
school districts ESA students were enrolled in prior to opening an ESA. Prior to the universal
eligibility expansion, almost all students had to be enrolled in public schools before opening ESAs
(pre-K and kindergarten ESA enrollees were an exception), so this table was well populated. It
included both traditional public school districts and public charter schools (which are not a part of any
school district). In 2024, a new table was added to the report that showed the school districts where
ESA students reside even if they had not enrolled in public school before. This list includes only
traditional public school districts because charter schools do not have geographic boundaries. In 2025,
the districts where ESA students reside was removed and replaced with zip codes where ESA students
reside. Although these changes might seem minor, they make comparisons over time regarding where
students using ESAs live and insights about who ESA users are challenging.

In addition, we collected information about the ESA program from other non-ESA–specific
publicly available sources, including Arizona’s state budgets and special education reports. We also
linked data from other public sources, including the PSS and the Stanford Education Data Archive.

In general, to make the best use of the available data, we have used two rules to guide our approach
to using and displaying the data: (1) where possible, use the most recent data available and (2) make
comparisons only between data points that have been created using the same definitions. For example,
in Chapter 4, to better understand who uses ESAs, we explore the characteristics of districts where
there are a lot of ESA participants. In Table 4.1, we use data from the most recent available report,


36


which was 2024 Q4, to analyze the characteristics of districts where students reside. In Figure 4.3, we
made comparisons over time, looking at how the districts where ESA students were enrolled changed
before and after universal eligibility. We make this comparison using districts where students were
enrolled because the districts where students reside was not available in the pre–universal eligibility
period. This limitation leads us to focus our analysis on a smaller subsample of districts, but it is
necessary to ensure that we are making apples-to-apples comparisons over time.

#### **Inflation Adjustment**


Throughout the report, dollar amounts are converted to 2025 dollars using the Federal Reserve
Bank of Minneapolis Inflation Calculator (Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, undated). We use
annual inflation multipliers, which we apply according to the year that the numerical data come from.
For example, award amounts reported in the FY 2021 Q1 ESA quarterly report are from July–
September 2020. Therefore, we use the 2020 inflation multiplier to convert these values into 2025
dollars.

of residence.
