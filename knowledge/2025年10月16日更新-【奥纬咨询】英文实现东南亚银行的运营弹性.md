Operational resilience is the capability of a bank to continue delivering its critical

operations and services during periods of disruption, according to The Basel Committee

on Banking Supervision.


Achieving this has become both increasingly challenging and vital. Banks today face

a volatile environment marked by escalating cyber threats, technology interdependencies,

complex third-party relationships, and potential global disruptions, from pandemics

to large-scale technology incidents.


In this context, resilience cannot be treated merely as a compliance obligation. It must

be a strategic priority for boards and C-suite executives seeking to protect customers,

safeguard reputation, maintain competitiveness, ensure long-term viability, and uphold

financial system stability.


Since the Basel Committee published its Principles for Operational Resilience in 2021,

regulators worldwide have introduced frameworks with the shared goal of protecting

customers and preserving financial stability.


Drawing on global experience, Oliver Wyman has supported more than 15 banks across

North America, Europe (including preparations for the Digital Operational Resilience Act

[DORA]), and Australia. We expect Southeast Asian banks to follow a similar path as regional

regulators formalize their own frameworks, integrating business continuity, IT risk, and

third-party risk management into a comprehensive, harmonized approach.


This report draws on extensive conversations with regulators, boards, and C-suite executives

across Southeast Asia. It distils their most pressing concerns, challenges, and insights into

five critical priorities to help banks meet regulatory expectations, embed resilience at the

heart of operations, and strengthen their ability to protect customers and financial stability.

#### PRIORITY 1 **CLARIFY WHAT OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE MEANS —** **AND HOW IT COMPLEMENTS, RATHER THAN REPLACES,** **BUSINESS CONTINUITY MANAGEMENT**


Operational resilience is a capability within a bank’s non-financial risk management

framework. It is not neatly tied to any particular risk event; [1] instead, it represents the

capability to deliver critical business services — a narrowly defined set of essential services

     - through severe but plausible disruptions. These are typically events lasting several days,

such as a third-party provider failure, a natural disaster, or a prolonged system outage.


Crucially, operational resilience does not replace business continuity management but rather

complements it. However, the boundaries between the two often require clarification,

especially because many regulatory frameworks integrate both topics into a single set

of requirements. Examples include:

      - The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority’s (APRA) CPS 230, which combines
resilience and business continuity management in its Prudential Standard on Operational
Risk Management

      - Monetary Authority of Singapore’s (MAS) Guidelines on Business Continuity Management,
which already incorporate operational resilience concepts such as critical business
services and customer impact

      - Bank Negara Malaysia’s (BNM) Policy Document on Business Continuity Management,
which also adopts many resilience principles within its broader approach

#### PRIORITY 2 **DEFINE CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AND SET** **IMPACT TOLERANCES**

##### **DEFINING CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AT THE FIRST LEVEL**


A bank’s operational resilience journey begins with defining and aligning on critical business

services — the essential services delivered to customers and the wider financial system.

Every subsequent decision about resilience, from investments to impact tolerances, flows

from this definition.


The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) defines critical business services

as those that, if disrupted beyond agreed tolerance levels, would cause material harm

to customers or the financial system. While regulators leave institutions to determine which

services qualify, they offer guidance through “expected lists.” For example, BNM highlights

services such as ATM and cash deposit access, online and mobile banking, payment card

services, call center support, and payment authorization, clearing, and settlement.


Not all important services qualify as “critical” under operational resilience. Distinguishing

between the two helps prioritize efforts effectively. Common examples include:

**•** **Know Your Customer (KYC) onboarding** : Key for compliance but short-term disruptions
rarely affect customers broadly.

**•** **Internal payroll** : Vital for employee morale but delays of several days rarely
threaten stability.

**•** **Payments system** : Foundational technology, but resilience focuses on how critical
services rely on it, not on the system’s resilience itself, which falls under business
continuity management frameworks.

##### **REFINING CRITICAL BUSINESS SERVICES AT THE SECOND LEVEL**


After drafting an initial list, banks often refine definitions to focus on where customer

impact occurs. Without this step, services risk being defined too broadly, ambitions become

unrealistic, and resilience investments spread too thin.


For example, “settlement of payment transactions” sounds straightforward, but requires

precision. Does it include all or only large value payments? Domestic and international?

All channels or only selected ones? These clarifications are essential for credible, achievable

impact tolerances.

##### **DEFINING IMPACT TOLERANCES**


Once critical business services are defined, banks must set impact tolerances — thresholds

for how much disruption can occur before causing material harm to customers. How these

tolerances are then achieved depends on the capabilities of the bank.


Two examples illustrate this practice. Monzo [2], a UK-based digital bank, built a fully

independent stand-in platform on a separate provider, ensuring uninterrupted

24/7 payments.


By contrast, a US brick-and-mortar bank [3] set a flexible tolerance. In a severe but plausible

scenario, it processes 85% of payments above $500,000 and 85% of smaller priority

payments received before 4pm by day’s end, using manual backups and reconciliation

procedures that supplement automated workflows.

#### PRIORITY 3 **MANAGE THIRD- AND FOURTH-PARTY RISKS AMID GROWING** **RELIANCE ON CLOUD PROVIDERS**


Banks now operate within highly interconnected ecosystems, relying on a complex network

of external vendors, suppliers, and service providers to deliver critical operations. These

dependencies are especially pronounced with cloud providers and other technology

platforms that underpin digital banking services.


Operational resilience takes a holistic view of this landscape: banks must be able to deliver

critical business services regardless of the type of disruption and whether delivery relies

on third or even fourth parties. Achieving this requires a structured approach to managing

external dependencies, ensuring that resilience extends well beyond the bank’s own

systems and processes.

Through our work with leading institutions, we have observed four strategies banks are

using to manage these dependencies effectively:


**1. Strengthen due diligence and contracting**

When a critical business service depends on a third-party provider, that provider must itself

be resilient. For smaller vendors, banks often conduct their own assessments, while for

large cloud providers — such as AWS, Microsoft, or Google — they may rely on the provider’s

attestations and published certifications. Due diligence goes hand in hand with contractual

alignment: where necessary, banks require updates to existing agreements to ensure

resilience expectations are explicitly built into vendor obligations.


**2. Minimize concentration risks**

Overreliance on a single third party can create significant vulnerability. Banks must

avoid situations where one provider is responsible for both primary delivery of a critical

business service and the associated stand-in or backup processes. Diversification

is critical to maintaining continuity in the event of disruption.


**3. Understand fourth-party risk**

Risks do not stop with third parties. Banks are increasingly exposed to fourth-party providers

     - the suppliers and platforms their vendors depend on. For example, multiple providers

may rely on the same underlying technology platform, creating hidden concentration risks.

To manage this, banks are increasingly requiring disclosure of key fourth-party relationships

and incorporating these dependencies into their resilience planning.


**4. Involve third parties in scenario testing**


Testing severe but plausible scenarios is central to demonstrating operational resilience.

Banks are increasingly inviting third parties to participate in these exercises to validate their

ability to deliver critical business services under stress and to identify weaknesses across

the end-to-end delivery chain.

#### PRIORITY 4 **NAVIGATE THE OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE JOURNEY**


A typical operational resilience journey takes two to three years to transition into a business
as-usual capability. Banks in Southeast Asia, however, start with several advantages. Many

core concepts are already embedded in existing regulations, such as business continuity

management, IT risk management, and outsourcing frameworks. Institutions can also learn

from jurisdictions where operational resilience standards are more mature, including

North America, Europe, and Australia. There is also strong awareness and support from

boards and the C-suite.

Building an effective operational resilience program, however, is not easy. It demands

new skillsets, closer integration of risk, IT, and business functions, and a cultural shift

from “operational resilience is IT’s responsibility” to “operational resilience is everyone’s

responsibility.” It also requires fundamental changes to how the organization operates.


From our work with banks, five key lessons stand out:


**1. Deliver business value.** Treating operational resilience purely as a compliance exercise

is a missed opportunity. Programs should achieve business objectives as well as risk

management ambitions.


**2. Build strong foundations.** Establish a clear framework and align on critical business

services. Institutions that rush these steps often face costly setbacks.

**3. Start with a pilot.** Focus first on one or two critical business services to test the approach,

adjust early, and build the organizational muscles needed to scale.


**4. Integrate operational resilience into the technology roadmap.** Embedding it into

architecture design, system upgrades, and vendor selection ensures sustainability without

requiring immediate transformation.


**5. Make resilience part of the culture.** When viewed as a strategic enabler rather than

a compliance “tick-the-box” exercise, operational resilience strengthens market position,

protects reputation, and safeguards long-term viability.

#### PRIORITY 5 **ESTABLISH CLEAR OWNERSHIP AND GOVERNANCE**


Operational resilience is a bank-wide responsibility, typically initiated and overseen by

the group board. Boards play an active role in shaping the bank’s resilience ambitions —

challenging priorities, approving plans, and making key decisions such as defining critical

business services and setting impact tolerance levels.


While the group board sets direction, the group risk function usually drives the early stages

of the journey. This includes establishing frameworks and policies, integrating them into

the broader non-financial risk management strategy, facilitating the identification of critical

business services and impact tolerances, and running and scaling the program, including

scenario testing.


However, accountability always rests with the business. Ownership of each critical business

service — and its corresponding impact tolerance — should sit with a senior executive

or executive minus one, even if parts of the service are delivered by other divisions. This

ensures clear, end-to-end accountability for resilience outcomes.


In banks with a mature Three Lines of Defense framework, some institutions also appoint

a Chief Resilience Officer, often within group operations. This role typically coordinates

operational resilience activities across the enterprise, while group risk focuses on advisory

and assurance responsibilities.

#### **CONCLUSION**


The urgency for Southeast Asian banks to accelerate their operational resilience agenda

has never been greater. While many institutions are laying the groundwork by developing

policies and frameworks, the rapidly evolving threat landscape and heightened regulatory

scrutiny demand swift and decisive action.


Some leading banks have already made progress by identifying their critical business

services, but these efforts must now be broadened and deepened across the region

to mitigate escalating operational risks. Regulators are moving quickly to formalize

resilience requirements and intensify supervisory oversight, particularly for domestically

or regionally systemically important financial institutions. Failure to act decisively risks not

only regulatory penalties but also severe operational and reputational damage, undermining

both stability and customer trust. The time to embed resilience at the core of banking

operations is now — delays will only increase exposure to disruptive events and threaten

the future viability of institutions in this dynamic, high-stakes environment.


Meeting this challenge requires a collective commitment from all stakeholders — not only

to comply with regulatory expectations but to position operational resilience as a core

component of strategic success. Embracing this journey will safeguard institutional stability,

bolster customer confidence, and ensure long-term sustainability in an increasingly

volatile environment.
