**The Scope and Importance**
**of Self-Service**

When self-service first gained traction,
the focus was largely on analytics, giving
business users easier access to data and
visualization tools. Today, self-service
extends across the entire data and analytics
life cycle. Automation is central to this
shift, with low-code and no-code platforms
enabling citizen developers to create and
manage data processes, improve quality, and
ensure integrity—all critical to analytics—
without heavy IT involvement. By expanding
self-service, organizations can build a trusted
foundation that empowers more people to
work confidently with data.


Organizations continue to prioritize selfservice analytics as a way to accelerate
decision-making and reduce IT bottlenecks.
This trend is important to those
organizations that want to move beyond
spreadsheets and static dashboards to build
an analytics culture. This is one in which
insights are not confined to a centralized
team, but instead are embedded in
everyday workflows across departments.
Self-service can reduce bottlenecks,
accelerate time to insight, and foster greater
collaboration between business and IT, with
IT enabling and governing the environment
in the background.


Today, self-service is evolving beyond
simple access and visualization to include
more advanced capabilities. Solutions are
now emerging that surface automated
insights, guide users through the process
of building predictive models, and embed
machine learning within intuitive interfaces.



Generative AI is further accelerating
this shift by providing natural language
interfaces for data—allowing users to ask
questions, explore trends, and generate
summaries without needing deep technical
skills. These innovations lower the barrier
to entry for advanced analytics and might
close the gap between data access and
meaningful, self-service insight. In fact, the
integration of generative AI into BI and AI
tools may become the force that propels
self-service to enterprise-wide capability.


Benefits of self-service include:

**•** **Greater agility and collaboration.**
Extending self-service across the data
life cycle, supported by automation
and low-code tools, creates a shared
understanding across departments,
aligning goals and improving
communication. Organizations that
embrace this culture are more agile and
better equipped to adapt to dynamic
market conditions.

**•** **Improved decision-making.** Enabling
business users to directly access data
and analytics tools improves their ability
to make real-time, data-driven decisions.
This removes dependencies on IT or
centralized data teams, accelerating
insight generation. Self-service makes
sense since business users are closest to
the questions that matter. Empowering
them to ask follow-up questions, perform
exploratory analysis, and test hypotheses
leads to better decisions. For example,
marketing professionals may need to
quickly assess and experiment with
customer experience metrics; they require

fast access to analytics to make timely
improvements that can’t always wait for IT.

**• Employee empowerment.** Access to
self-service analytics supports continuous
learning, skill development, and greater
employee engagement across technical
and non-technical roles. In TDWI research,
we’ve seen that respondents believe
empowering more users with analytics
capabilities is important to increasing data
value and BI success. IT teams also benefit
by shifting from repetitive dashboard
creation to more strategic, advanced tasks
such as machine learning. Business users
in functions such as sales and marketing
gain autonomy to analyze and act on data
independently. This creates mutual benefit
and improves the overall organizational
data culture.





This isn’t to say that everyone in the
organization should analyze their data.
Some employees may not have an analytics
mindset. They may be overburdened with
other responsibilities or simply don’t see
the value of using data for decisions in
their particular job. The goal of self-service
should be to ensure that those who need
data to drive strategic or operational
decisions are empowered to use it.


For years in TDWI research, we’ve seen that
self-service analytics is a top priority for
organizations. Yet many companies seem
to struggle to democratize their analytics
efforts. As we will see, there can be
numerous reasons for this, and, of course,
self-service capabilities continue to evolve.
This “State Of” analysis illustrates where
there are still gaps today and provides

**The Overall State of**
**Self-Service Maturity**

There are a number of interrelated factors
that inform the current state of self-service.
Self-service is not simply a matter of using
a visual analytics tool against a data set or
even using generative AI to get answers from
data in a natural language way. It involves
people, processes, and technologies. It
involves being able to access data in a selfservice manner, analyze it, and trust the data.
It requires data literacy, i.e., users’ ability
to understand and interact with data and
analytics and communicate the results to
achieve business goals.


In the survey for this report, we looked at
five different factors for self-service success:
organizational culture, data management
tools and processes, infrastructure, analytics
enablement, and governance for data and
analytics. The survey answers were measured



on a 5-point scale from least mature to most
mature practices.


The results indicate that organizations are
progressing steadily toward enabling selfservice analytics capabilities, with an overall
average maturity score of 3.31.


The areas in which the most respondents’
practices were the most mature include
strong collaboration between business and
IT teams (mean score of 3.8), broad tool
access (3.7), active leadership support (3.7),
and a relatively high level of user trust in
the data (3.7), all of which are important for
successful self-service initiatives.


However, the data also highlights several
areas that require further development.
Notably, the actual adoption of advanced
analytics tools remains limited (mean
2.8 maturity score), and only a small
proportion of business users are regularly
engaging in self-service analytics, which
can suggest untapped potential in
expanding user enablement. Additionally,

**Culture for Self-Service**

Organizational culture is critical for moving
forward with self-service. Building that
culture often requires leadership that actively
supports self-service and collaboration
between business and IT. A strong culture



can result in a large number of users making
data-driven decisions and business users
engaging in self-service analytics.

area of self-service maturity has the overall
highest average across the five areas, and
it is significantly higher than the lowest

**The State of Data Maturity**
**for Self-Service**

Organizational culture is clearly important
for self-service maturity, but success
requires more. The data infrastructure
needs to be in place. Users need to be
able to ingest, find, and consume data



for analytics. The data needs to be easily
accessible and easy to understand. It
can’t be fragmented throughout the
organization. In other words, data
management and infrastructure can’t be a
hindrance.


Survey results suggest that organizations
are, on average, operating at a moderate
level of capability across several important
areas of data maturity. Respondents’
maturity scores averaged from 3 to 3.7
across questions on automated data
integration pipelines, data quality practices,
metadata management, and trust in data.
The lowest scoring question was about
the use of a metadata-driven data catalog,
which scored a 3, and the highest scoring
question was about data trust (3.7). Also
high were maturity scores about using
automated data integration pipelines (3.6).


Automated data integration pipelines
play an important role in delivering
timely, consistent, and reliable data from
various source systems to downstream
users. These pipelines can eliminate
manual data handling, reduce errors, and
support near-real-time updates, which
are especially important in fast-paced
business environments. Automation also
frees up technical resources, allowing data
engineers and IT to focus on optimization
and governance rather than repetitive ETL
tasks. When these pipelines are robust
and well-managed, they enable business
users to access current and relevant
data for decision-making, which is a key
foundational requirement for any selfservice analytics initiative.

Similarly, data quality processes ensure that
the data being used is accurate, complete,
consistent, and reliable. Poor data quality
can lead to flawed analyses and incorrect
decisions—the old garbage in, garbage out
issue. Poor data quality can also ultimately
lead to a lack of confidence in self-service
platforms and a decrease in usage. In this
survey, the use of automated tools for data
quality management such as monitoring
and observability tools was not yet
widespread (Figure 4).


However, there appears to be a strong
conceptual link between data quality
practices and trust in the data. While these
are asked as separate items in the survey,
organizations reporting higher maturity in
data quality tools also tend to report higher
trust in their data. It is possible that when
users believe in the integrity of their data,
they are more likely to engage in self-service
analytics. We have anecdotal evidence that
once trust is breached, individuals are less
likely to believe in their data.


As mentioned, with the advent of generative
AI, we see organizations wanting to analyze
their unstructured data (such as call center
notes or trouble tickets) as well as their
structured data. However, according to
TDWI surveys, organizations likely trust their
unstructured data less than their structured



data. [2] They are not used to managing this
data in a way that supports analytics. There
may be different data quality metrics for
unstructured data than structured data.
For instance, plausibility might be a new
metric in unstructured documents. Likewise,
metrics such as accuracy might mean
something different in unstructured text
documents than in structured databases.
That means organizations will need to
create, redefine, and refine certain metrics.


Yet redefining data-quality metrics does little
good unless those definitions, and the data
they qualify, are systematically captured,
cataloged, and governed. This ties closely
with metadata management, which provides
users with context about the data—what it
means, how it was collected, when it was last
updated, and who is responsible for it.


Metadata systems, such as data catalogs
(which scored low in average maturity here),
improve data discoverability and help
with transparency, allowing users to make
informed decisions about data use. They
provide visibility to the data. This is in line
with what TDWI has seen in other surveys;
data catalogs are becoming more of a
priority for organizations. If organizations
cannot find and use data for self-service
easily, then they most likely won’t adopt it.

**Building Data Literacy**

How do organizations build data literacy?
As mentioned, data literacy involves not
only understanding the data, but the ability
to think critically about it, ask questions
of it, gain reasonable insights from it, and
communicate those insights. While selfservice tools can be easy to use, business
analysts need to have critical thinking
skills and knowledge of the business to
be successful. They need to know how to
question the data and the outputs. For
example, if a tool surfaces an insight, the
user needs to be able to think critically
about whether that insight makes sense
in the context of their business. This is
especially important given that generative
AI systems are prone to hallucinations. That
means that the business user cannot just
accept the response at face value.


Some organizations build data literacy
by creating data literacy enablement
teams (they go by different names such as
data fluency teams to suggest a broader
understanding). These teams first assess the
literacy of the organization. They then create
programs geared to different personas in the
organization. In some companies training is
mandatory; in others it is optional (to provide
empowerment). The programs help train
users on the tools, but they should do more
than this. They should provide information
on governance (see below). They should help



people think critically by providing them with
scenarios using real company data, geared
to their persona, that the individual (or team)
takes from start to finish. They should be
engaging, so people want to participate in
them. Of course, sometimes this is easier
said than done.


Literacy enablement teams should also
measure their progress towards their goals.
They should outline metrics and KPIs that
are important for literacy and then measure
them. That may include a portfolio of metrics
ranging from using the tools to ROI impact.


**The State of Governance for**
**Self-Service**

In TDWI surveys, we routinely see that
data governance is a top priority for
organizations, especially as they try to
modernize their environments. TDWI
defines data governance as the practice of
ensuring that business data remains fit for
use. It focuses on the people, processes,
policies, rules, and regulations for achieving
specific goals for a managed data resource
and building trust in the data.


Data governance is obviously important for
self-service analytics. Data quality, policies
that build trust, and compliance guidelines
will need to be in place for structured as
well as unstructured data types. One option
for enforcing validation rules, approval
workflows, and audit trails is an advanced
automation platform, which can ensure that
business process automation aligns with
data governance policies. This can improve
governance and reduce cycle time. When
this level of process automation is tightly
coupled with data integrity frameworks, it

based on consistent and trusted data, which
is foundational for successful self-service.

adapting governance practices to keep
pace with rapidly evolving technologies.
As the use of AI in analytics expands,
organizations will need to prioritize
developing governance frameworks that
address not just traditional data use but
also the risks and opportunities presented
by advanced tools like generative AI.


**Considerations and Best Practices**
**for Increasing Self-Service Maturity**

Organizations have laid some of the
groundwork for self-service analytics, from
increasing cultural readiness to expanding
tool access and training. However, unlocking
the full potential of self-service requires
deeper maturity across infrastructure,
governance, and enablement dimensions.
Below are key considerations and best
practices to guide further progress:

**Gain top-down executive support to**
**build the culture.** Executive sponsorship is
foundational. Leaders must do more than
endorse self-service, they must actively
model data-driven decision-making,
fund literacy programs, and promote
collaboration between IT and business
users. Culture maturity scores were the
highest in this study, yet gaps remain in
actual adoption, suggesting that leadership
support needs to translate into more
consistent communication, engagement,



and enablement across departments.
It will be important to establish a crossdepartmental steering committee led by
senior business and IT leaders to drive
alignment, remove adoption barriers, and
prioritize funding and initiatives for data
democratization.

**Establish a solid data foundation.**
Without trusted, accessible, and welldocumented data, self-service efforts will
stall. This includes solid data integration
pipelines, data quality monitoring, and
clear metadata through tools such as data
catalogs. As we’ve seen in this report, while
trust in data is relatively strong, maturity in
metadata and quality tools is lagging. That
can be a problem waiting to happen.


Self-service users should have intuitive
access to metadata such as data
definitions, lineage, ownership, and quality
status. Embedding this information directly
in BI tools or data catalogs can eliminate
ambiguity and help users make informed
decisions faster. One best practice is to
invest in metadata-driven platforms and
data observability tools to monitor quality
and provide transparency. Ensure selfservice tools are tightly integrated with
trusted, governed data sets to minimize
risks of misinterpretation. And don’t forget
about the unstructured data.

**Invest in persona-based training and**
**support.** Training must go beyond tool
use to include data literacy and critical
thinking. Literacy programs should
be tailored to different roles such as
executives, analysts, and frontline staff, so
each group receives relevant context and
use-case-based guidance.

Organizations should consider forming a
dedicated data literacy enablement team
to assess current skills, design learning
journeys, and measure improvement
over time. Include interactive, scenariobased training and integrate literacy into
onboarding and performance metrics.
Additionally, some organizations will
implement formal change management
frameworks such as ADKAR or the Kotter
8-step method. If this is the case, understand
that change doesn’t happen linearly.

**Expand tool diversity and advanced**
**analytics access.** While visual analytics tools
are widely available, fewer organizations
offer access to a variety of tools, including
low-code/no-code platforms for data
creation and data quality management,
integrity solutions that ensure trusted
information, and generative AI front ends
for natural language exploration. Encourage
adoption by providing training and support
programs along with the ability to perform
more advanced analytics tasks.

**Build in guardrails and governance**
**from the start.** Data governance maturity
is uneven, especially around emerging
technologies like generative AI. A
fragmented governance strategy can
erode trust and introduce compliance risks.
Organizations need to ensure policies span
both structured and unstructured data and
evolve to include responsible AI governance.


Build governance into self-service tools
with embedded controls like data lineage
visibility, usage auditing, and access
policies. Consider investing in data
integrity suites or tools that support endto-end lineage, validation, and enrichment,



especially in environments where data has
been migrated from legacy systems or
spread across disparate platforms.


Use AI governance frameworks like the NIST
AI Risk Management Framework or OECD’s
AI Principles as starting points for responsible
use. We see organizations taking a number of
tools and developing their own frameworks
from them that fit their organizations.

**Foster collaboration between IT and**
**business.** Collaboration between business
and IT scored among the highest maturity
areas in the survey. However, to sustain
momentum, IT must evolve from a
gatekeeper role to an enabler. Business
teams, in turn, must take responsibility for
understanding and using data effectively.


Establish a federated model where IT
manages shared services (e.g., security,
access, infrastructure) and empowers business
domains to manage their own analytics
initiatives with support and oversight.

###### Automated data quality of governance rules.


**Leverage automation to scale self-service**
**and ensure data integrity.** Automation
should extend beyond data movement
into business-critical workflows that
influence the quality and consistency of
enterprise data. Automating high-volume,

data-intensive processes (e.g., in ERP
systems) can reduce errors, streamline
approvals, and improve data governance.
Organizations should explore automation
frameworks that allow business users to
execute mass updates or validate changes
within governed environments. This not
only improves efficiency but also ensures
that analytics are built on accurate, timely,
and trustworthy data, ultimately making
self-service more scalable and sustainable.

**Use automation to strengthen governance**
**and scale responsibly.** Automated data
quality checks, validations, and stewardship
processes can reduce human error and
ensure consistent application of governance
rules. Consider leveraging intelligent
automation tools that can recognize and
correct anomalies in data pipelines or alert
teams when data drift occurs.

**Measure and iterate.** Self-service maturity
is a journey. Organizations must define
success metrics such as adoption rates,
time-to-insight, and data quality issues
reported and use them to continuously
improve. It will be important to track selfservice analytics KPIs such as percent
of business users engaging with tools,
frequency of usage, and business outcomes
tied to analytics (e.g., campaign success,
operational efficiency). Combine this with
qualitative feedback loops.

**Prepare for responsible AI.** As generative
AI becomes embedded in analytics
workflows, organizations must plan for
ethical, legal, and compliance implications.
Responsible AI goes beyond accuracy and
bias, it includes transparency, explainability,



and accountability. Some organizations
begin by cataloging AI use cases,
identifying risks, and establishing a review
board or responsible AI task force. There
are numerous frameworks available to use
as a starting point.


**Research Methodology**

This State of Self-Service Report examines
the current state of self-service and how
mature organizations are in their efforts.
It highlights critical areas for self-service
success: organizational culture, data
management, infrastructure, analytics
enablement, and governance for data
and analytics. It examines challenges
organizations are facing and provides
considerations and best practices for
moving forward.


For this study, TDWI examined several
surveys that we run throughout the year,
although the primary data for this report
comes from the 2025 TDWI State of SelfService survey. Over 200 respondents from
various industries and company sizes have
participated in the survey to date, and 215
completed responses are reflected in the
figures in this report. The answers to the
questions for the report were arranged on a
scale for 1-5, with 1 being the least mature
and 5 being the most mature. Therefore,
the scores represented in this report are
also on this scale. Average scores are
shown. Medians track with averages,
except where noted in the report.
