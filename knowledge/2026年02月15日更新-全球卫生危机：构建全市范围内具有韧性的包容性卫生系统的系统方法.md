#### 1. Introduction

The increasing impacts of climate change on urban
areas demand comprehensive citywide resilience
solutions and a systems-level approach that takes
into account the interconnectivity of urban planning,
services and infrastructure (Gongadze, 2023). Urban
sanitation systems are interlinked with other critical
urban infrastructure and service systems, such as
water supply, transportation, energy, stormwater
and solid waste management. The vulnerability of
urban infrastructure and service systems to extreme
weather events, exacerbated by climate change,
creates complex interdependencies. Failure of
one system, or node within a system, can trigger
a cascade of adverse events which potentially
compromise sanitation systems or other critical urban
infrastructure or services. This interconnectedness
means that sanitation system failures can impact
non-sanitation systems and, conversely, disruptions
in other urban systems can affect sanitation services
(Way and Evans, 2020). These cascading effects
pose significant risks to public and environmental
health in cities, highlighting the need for integrated
resilience planning (Mukim and Mark, 2023). Climate
resilient sanitation systems therefore underpin
overall city resilience. There is also a key link between
sanitation and agriculture and food systems through
the safe reuses of wastewater and sludge. Reaching
beyond reuse, circular economy approaches play an
increasingly important role in fostering sustainable
and climate adaptive transformations in the urban
sanitation sector [1] .


The relationship between urban sanitation and
climate change is complex and unfolds across
two key dimensions: mitigation and adaptation.
From a mitigation perspective, sanitation
systems contribute to greenhouse gas emissions,
exacerbating global warming. Conversely, the
adaptation dimension focuses on the vulnerability
of sanitation infrastructure to climate-induced
disruptions and the need to enhance its resilience
to avoid amplifying health and environmental risks
(IPCC, 2022; Johnson et al., 2022; Willetts et al.,
2022a; WHO, 2022).

In accordance with World Bank (2025) we define
climate resilient sanitation as follows:


Climate-resilient sanitation (CRS) services
anticipate, respond to, cope with, recover
from, adapt to, or transform from climaterelated events, trends, and disturbances while
striving to achieve and maintain universal
access to safely managed services, even in the
face of an unstable climate, paying special
attention to the most exposed vulnerable
groups and, where possible and appropriate,
minimizing emissions. This is achieved by
adapting and transforming infrastructure,
service approaches, organizations, and
behaviors. CRS includes both sewered and
non-sewered systems designed to minimize
environmental pollution, reduce GHG
emissions, and improve public health by
ensuring the safe containment of waste along
the sanitation service chain. CRS also supports
transformative adaptation by integrating
sanitation into the broader urban water cycle,
fostering inclusive and equitable citywide
resilience, and enhancing the capacities
needed to manage it.


The ‘ _Citywide Inclusive Sanitation_ ’ (CWIS)
approach emerges as a pivotal paradigm for
providing integrated sanitation services and for
reducing vulnerabilities to climate threats across
the population. CWIS embraces the inclusion of
complementary urban infrastructure and services
into sanitation planning and adopts an outcomeoriented perspective focused on the realization
of adequate sanitation for all urban residents.
This entails ensuring the safe management of
human waste throughout the sanitation service
chain, a necessity that becomes even more critical
under the changing conditions and heightened
pressures wrought by climate change.


This background paper refers to both mitigation
and adaptation within the urban sanitation
sector, with a primary emphasis on adaptation,
and provides a rationale for adopting a _systems-_
_based approach_ to enhance resilience in the face
of climate variability and extremes. This approach

#### 2. Climate change impacts on urban sanitation systems


In recent decades, the world has rapidly urbanized,
with more than half of the global population now
living in urban areas. This shift puts increasing
pressure on urban infrastructure and services,
including water and sanitation systems. As
cities grow, so does water demand, leading to
higher volumes of wastewater being generated.
Inadequate wastewater and faecal sludge
treatment infrastructure and services, especially in
primary and secondary towns, is further strained by
the impacts of climate change. Urban populations,
due to their size and density, are particularly
vulnerable to these climate-related challenges
(UN-Habitat, 2023).


Climate change manifests itself locally as more
intense and/or prolonged precipitation, as more
frequent and/or more intense storms and cyclones,
as more variable and/or declining rainfall and
run-off, as increasing sea-level rise, and/or as
more variable and increasing temperatures,
including temperature extremes. (WHO, 2019;
WHO, 2018b). These effects can lead to hazards like
flooding, drought, erosion and altered water levels,
and thereby directly affect the performance of
sanitation systems with knock-on effects for public
health and environmental impact (WHO, 2019).

The potential impacts of climate change on
urban sanitation systems are categorized as
follows:


- Negative direct impacts: (i) damaged sanitation
infrastructure and loss of access [3] ; (ii) disrupted
services [4] ; and (iii) inhibited system efficacy [5] .


- Positive direct impacts: (i) prolonged life
or reduced maintenance requirements of
infrastructure; (ii) improved service delivery
(e.g., less disrupted emptying services due to
drier conditions); and (iii) improved system
performance.


Interdependencies with other urban
infrastructure and service sectors. Climate change
does not happen in sector silos. The sanitation
sector is increasingly moving towards a systemsbased approach, recognizing the complex
interdependencies with other urban sectors which
enable or hinder sanitation service delivery.
In other words, full sanitation service chains
of both sewered and non-sewered sanitation
depend on the effective operation of other urban
infrastructure and service systems such as water
supply, stormwater management, solid waste,
energy and transport (Way and Evans, 2020). In
the context of climate change adaptation and
resilience, this becomes crucial as, for example,
construction of ‘flood resilient’ raised toilets is
counter-productive without considering good
management practices (regular formal emptying),
urban stormwater management to reduce the
flooding, and transport infrastructure to make
sure emptying services can reach these toilets
(Okaali et al., 2022).

#### 3. Urban sanitation failures and climate change

Integrating climate impacts and sanitation
failure modes Urban sanitation systems use a
wide range of infrastructure, technologies and
service arrangements. In many cities there is a
mix of sewered and non-sewered systems and
services. Poor design, mismanagement and/or
environmental factors lead to urban sanitation
failures resulting in public and environmental
health risks.


A review of urban sanitation in 39 cities articulates
typical urban sanitation systems and corresponding
failure modes based on the analysis of excreta flow
diagrams (Peal et al., 2020), and categorizes the
urban sanitation failures into five urban sanitation
failure modes (FMs):


- FM1: Faecal sludge (FS) not contained / not
emptied


- FM2: FS and/or supernatant (SN) not delivered
to treatment


- FM3: FS and/or SN not treated


- FM4: Wastewater (WW) not delivered to
treatment


- FM5: WW not treated


FM1-3 thus apply to non-sewered systems and
FM4-5 to sewered systems.


Hyde-Smith et al. (2022) linked these failure
modes to the results of their systematic
assessment of the evidence base on climate
impacts on urban sanitation systems and explored
how the different identified impact categories
are likely to increase or reduce the probability of
each failure mode occurring. This paper presents

Public health and environmental health shocks:
Well-functioning sanitation systems are designed
to protect public and environmental health. Poor
sanitation is linked to transmission of diseases
such as cholera, diarrhea, dysentery, hepatitis A,
typhoid, trachoma, intestinal worm infections and
polio. In 2019, 38% of the diarrheal disease deaths
in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) were
attributable to unsafe sanitation, accounting for
564,000 deaths (Wolf et al., 2023), and in 2022, at
least 1.7 billion people used faecally contaminated
drinking water (WHO, 2023). The additional disease


(Bernardini et al., 2024). While this paper’s matrix
provides indicative guidance on where severe
acute outcomes might occur, a more detailed riskbased assessment (e.g., by using some of the tools
provided in the Sanitation Safety Planning (SSP)
Manual (WHO, 2022)) would be necessary for a
context specific thorough evaluation.


Greenhouse gas emissions: There is still a lot of
uncertainty around the magnitude of greenhouse
gas (GHG) emissions from sanitation systems
particularly for non-sewered systems (Lambiasi
et al., 2024; Strande, 2024). Growing research
points to greenhouse gas emissions from sanitation
systems being vastly underestimated (Johnson
et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Lambiasi et al., 2024;
Cheng et al., 2022). Emissions from sanitation
include methane (CH4), carbon dioxide (CO2) and
nitrous oxide (NO2), through: (i) _direct emissions_
along the sanitation service chain, from the
decomposition of organic matter found in excreta
at the household level through to wastewater
and fecal sludge treatment and disposal
processes; (ii) indirect _operational emissions_ from
the energy used to operate systems, including
pumping, treatment and transportation; and
(iii) _embedded carbon_ of sanitation infrastructure
(Lambiasi et al. 2024). [6]


Climate change impacts on sanitation systems
can be linked to the promotion of anaerobic
conditions in different sanitation elements that
enhance CH4 production – and thereby increase
_direct emissions_ - as a result of: (i) inundation
of storage tanks, pits, drying beds, planted
wetlands treatment plants and/or sludge landfills;
(ii) extended storage periods caused by disrupted
emptying services (e.g., due to road damage or
inaccessibility); (iii) reduced wastewater aeration
due to the failure of treatment plant aeration
systems following extreme events (e.g., power
failures after storms or flooding), or due to reduced
wastewater flow velocities in sewers during times
of water scarcity or due to sewer blockages after
extreme weather events (Smeaton-Russell et al.,
forthcoming; Johnson et al., 2022). Climate change
impacts on sanitation systems can also contribute

Climate-resilient sanitation services, implemented
at the citywide level with a mix of service types
and health risk-informed design, are consequently
essential to safeguard health (GCF, 2024; WHO,

is intensifying a recent cholera upsurge by
compounding enduring triggers such as poverty

Climate hazard induced damage or disruption
of sanitation system also has profound negative
impacts on environmental health. Environmental
pollution from wastewater degrades ecosystems
and threatens biodiversity (Tuholske et al., 2021).
Untreated wastewater and faecal sludge damages
freshwater and marine ecosystems, leading to
collapse of ecosystems and converting carbon sinks
to carbon emitters (Fourqurean et al., 2012). For
instance, seagrasses absorb carbon dioxide at 35
times the rate of rainforests, yet 88 percent of them
are exposed to wastewater pollution (Tuholske
et al., 2021) and it its estimated that current losses
of seagrass carbon stores potentially account for
10% of all emissions attributed to land use change
(Fourqurean et al., 2012).


All climate change-related impacts that impair the
effective functioning of sanitation systems thus may
result in negative public or environmental health
outcomes. However, these outcomes will vary in
temporal scale and geographic distribution. The
risk for negative public health, and environmental,
outcomes is highly context-specific and depends on
common building blocks of risk assessment, such as
hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. For example,
geographic location, elevation, population density,
and the urban built environment – including
housing types, road infrastructure, and stormwater
management - are key elements defining people’s
exposure and vulnerabilities to urban flooding

_Operational emissions_ are also correlated to
climate; for example, higher wastewater flows
caused by wetter climates may require more
pumping while low/slow flow conditions caused
by drier climates may require increased aeration

Increased intensive rainfall events may lead to
more frequent emptying of onsite systems, resulting
in increased operational emissions of the emptying
vehicles, although emissions from trucking sludge
were found to be relatively small when considering
total emissions from sanitation at a whole systemlevel (Johnson et al., 2022).


There is a gap in the literature that links climate
change impacts to increased _embedded carbon_
from sanitation infrastructure, however these can
be generally summarized as: (i) embedded carbon
related to infrastructure repair or replacement
caused by damages due to climate changerelated shocks (e.g., the destruction of latrines or
other infrastructure during flooding events); and
(ii) embedded carbon related to the adaptive
infrastructure necessary to deal with long-term
stresses related to climate change (e.g., the
provision of additional storage/transmission
infrastructure to deal with increased sewer flows
due to changing rainfall patterns, or the raising of
wastewater treatment infrastructure to adapt to
higher sea-levels) (NSW Government, 2024; Keil
et al., 2013).


It should be noted that the reduction of GHG
emissions is an important consideration in the
selection of sanitation technologies and services,
and should be weighed alongside other critical
factors that influence the long-term sustainability
of sanitation systems. These factors include cultural
acceptability, capital and operational costs, and
technical feasibility within local environmental
conditions, such as water availability, soil type,
topography and population density. Following a
citywide inclusive sanitation approach, the issue
of equity should guide decisions to ensure all



communities, and especially the most vulnerable,
benefit from resilient solutions. While reducing
GHG emissions and exploring circular economy
opportunities are vital, adaptation that enhances
system resilience to climate-related shocks must
take precedence to protect public health and
environmental sustainability.

#### 4. Citywide Inclusive Sanitation (CWIS) and climate change

The Citywide Inclusive Sanitation initiative
emerged in 2015 and was initiated by a group
of practitioners, researchers and development
partners in the wake of the sector committing to
the SDG targets on safely managed sanitation
after having failed to reach the far less ambitious
sanitation Millenium Development Goal
(MDG) targets. CWIS was designed to address
the persisting urban sanitation crisis, the low
prioritization given to non-sewered project
components trying to reach the urban poor, and
a general focus on technologies instead of on
outcomes, including safely managed services
that protect public and environmental health.
This narrow focus often overlooked the need for
investments in the enabling environment (policies,
institutions, regulation and financing [8] ), as well as
in operation and maintenance. As a public service
approach, CWIS seeks to plan and implement
sanitation systems that align with the SDG 6
objectives, emphasizing the provision of universal,
sustainable sanitation services (Schrecongost et al.,
2020; Gambrill et al., 2016).


While not explicitly centered on climate resilience,
CWIS inherently supports principles conducive to
climate-resilient planning. A notable overlap exists
between the characteristics of a CWIS project and
the key elements of climate-resilient sanitation
(see Table 2). Consequently, there is a strong
opportunity to increasingly anchor climate-resilient
sanitation planning into the CWIS approach.


Inclusive climate resilient sanitation for all:
Climate resilient sanitation systems are, first and
foremost, systems that provide safe sanitation
service outcomes in a given context under
current and future climatic conditions. CWIS
emphasizes a pragmatic approach to service
delivery, focusing on efficient service provision
rather than on promoting a specific technology
or infrastructure type. Given the diverse needs of
urban populations, flexible sanitation solutions—
ranging from sewered to non-sewered systems—

are key for delivering effective and resilient
services (Gambrill et al., 2020; Schrecongost et al.,
2020). Climate-resilient sanitation services must
include everyone: addressing sanitation gaps
and reducing inequalities will be increasingly
important as climate change adds further pressure
to dysfunctional or semi-functional systems
and exacerbates vulnerabilities, especially for
disadvantaged groups who are likely to bear
increased risks of sanitation failures (WHO, 2022).


Safe management of human waste along
the whole sanitation service chain: Effective
sanitation systems ensure the safe management
of human waste along the entire service chain,
from collection to treatment and disposal or reuse.
CWIS highlights the importance of safeguarding
environmental and human health by promoting
practices that prevent the release of untreated
waste into the urban environment and ecosystems.
Climate change induced hazards place increased
strain on sanitation systems. To address these
pressures, adaptive infrastructure and services
should be resilient enough to either withstand these
pressures and, recognizing that no adaptation
measure can fully eliminate the risk of failure or
account for all future scenarios, should rely on
robust management systems that can quickly
restore services. Such measures include public
communication strategies to minimize health risks
and aid rapid rehabilitation efforts after climaterelated disruptions. By incorporating measures such
as safe wastewater and fecal sludge recycling and
reuse, cities can align with strategies that enhance
sanitation system resilience to climate change
(Willetts et al., 2022b) and can increase overall
urban water resilience and hence city resilience.


Embracing diverse technical and service solutions:
CWIS supports a diverse range of technical and
service solutions, combining on-site and sewered
options, centralized or decentralized systems,
and resource recovery and reuse. This flexibility is
essential for building resilient sanitation systems
that can adapt to varied urban contexts and
future climate scenarios. By embracing a range
of technical approaches, cities can enhance their
climate resilience, reducing reliance on any one
system and increasing the adaptability of services
to future uncertainties (ISF-UTS and SNV, 2019).
Furthermore, climate mitigation potential should be
considered in sanitation planning, emphasizing the

this can mean that these users face higher costs, for
example, for undertaking the emergency emptying

climate-resilient sanitation should be overseen by
a lead ministry or agency at the national and local
levels. This recommendation aligns with CWIS’s
principle of sanitation as a public service approach.
While not all services or investments must originate
from the public domain, the government is
responsible for structuring and regulating the
market, ensuring equitable service outcomes for all
(Gambrill et al., 2020; Schrecongost et al., 2020)
even under changing climate conditions.


Investing beyond infrastructure for climate
resilient sanitation: Resilient sanitation systems
require investments beyond ‘climate proofing’
physical infrastructure. Increasing evidence points
towards the significance of ‘active management’
of sanitation systems for enhancing their climate
resilience and for reducing their greenhouse gas
emissions (Johnson et al., 2022). As climate impacts
increase the pressures on sanitation services,
preventive maintenance, flexible operations and
adequate emergency responses will become
increasingly important (Willetts et al., 2022b).
CWIS underscores the importance of providing
due attention to the non-infrastructure aspects
of service delivery and emphasizes the operation
and maintenance requirements of sewered and
non-sewered systems (Gambrill et al., 2020).
Furthermore, CWIS thinking aligns with the need
for long-term strategic planning in the face of

Political will and long-term funding for (resilient)
sanitation: Strong political will, technical leadership
and creative long-term funding strategies are key
for establishing climate-resilient sanitation systems.
CWIS underscores that achieving universal access
requires robust governance frameworks, and that
national and local governments must demonstrate
a commitment to sustained investments. To date,
the cost of ensuring the climate resilience of the
SDG urban sanitation targets have not been
estimated (Willetts et al., 2022a). Global sanitation
funding levels are currently insufficient to reach
the SDG urban sanitation target (WHO and
UNICEF, 2023) and annual sanitation investment
cost estimates (Hutton and Varughese, 2016) will
need to be further increased when considering
the need for climate resilience sanitation systems.
Public sanitation investments are traditionally
biased towards subsidizing capital investment of
centralized sewerage and treatment systems –
and, sometimes, their operational costs – which
often primarily serve more affluent parts of
the population and, therefore, reinforce urban
inequalities (Gambrill et al., 2020; Hutchings et al.,
2018; Andres et al., 2019; Mara and Evans, 2018).
A significant challenge for long-term financial
sustainability of sanitation services is their ongoing
recovery and adaptation as climate impacts
become more frequent and severe (Alam et al.,
2015; Purwar et al., 2020; Shimi et al., 2010). This
requires a shift from focusing mainly on upfront
infrastructure costs to securing continuous funding
for system upgrades and maintenance as climate
change evolves in unpredictable ways. Dickin
et al. (2020) point out that, to date, no substantial
funding for sanitation is provided through the
multilateral climate funds. In addition, there is a
concern that the climate fund eligibility criteria are
restrictive regarding the types of measures that
can be financed (Dickin et al., 2020; SWA, 2019),
and favor highly engineered, large, centralized
systems. CWIS advocates for a reevaluation of
funding mechanisms to ensure that investments in
sanitation systems are equitable and reflective of
the broad public benefits that improved sanitation
systems offer for present and future generations.

how climate change will affect future rainfall

of deterministic planning that can lock cities into
suboptimal pathways if climate change impacts

approach allows for the development of robust
sanitation strategies that can respond to various
climate scenarios and changing urban dynamics

Considering interconnected systems and
integrating sanitation in broader urban
development plans: CWIS highlights the
importance of incorporating complementary urban
services into sanitation planning (World Bank,
2024) and, inversely, sanitation into broader urban
development plans. This approach recognizes
the intricate interconnections between sanitation
and other essential urban systems, including
solid waste management, stormwater drainage
and water supply. For instance, inadequate
solid waste management can lead to clogged
drainage systems, exacerbating flooding risks and
compromising sanitation infrastructure. Similarly,
water supply shortages can impact sanitation
practices and the functionality of sewerage systems.
By acknowledging these interdependencies, CWIS
promotes a holistic urban planning strategy that
enhances overall system resilience and will be of
growing importance in the face of climate change
challenges. This intersectoral approach not only
improves sanitation outcomes but also contributes
to broader urban sustainability and resilience goals.
Recommendations for integrating wastewater and
faecal sludge recycling and reuse strategies into
sanitation resilience planning (Willetts et al., 2022b)
also align with CWIS’s approach to urban sanitation
provision.


Targeting vulnerable and underserved
groups: The impacts of climate change will put



additional pressure on already dysfunctional
sanitation systems. It is commonly accepted that
disadvantaged groups are likely to bear increased
risks of sanitation failures (WHO, 2022). CWIS
defines sanitation as a public service designed to
ensure universal access. This vision requires robust
governance and service frameworks capable of
adapting to the pressures of climate change. The
threat of climate change exacerbating urban
sanitation vulnerabilities underscores the need
for planned and coordinated adaptation actions
and for protecting public and environmental
health and the economy. The use of risk-informed
approaches, such as the WHO’s Sanitation Safety
Planning (SSP) (WHO, 2022), can support the
targeting of vulnerable groups including women,
ethnic minorities, the urban poor and people with
disabilities.


In conclusion, while the CWIS framework did
not originally emphasize climate resilience,
its principles of sustainable service outcomes,
effective governance and equitable funding
provide a solid foundation for the integration into
it of climate-resilient sanitation planning. There
are already examples of CWIS cities, such as
Lusaka, incorporating climate-resilient sanitation
technologies and practices into their planning
approach. Collecting more examples and evidence
of how utilities and municipalities implementing
CWIS are incorporating climate resilience
into service provision may reveal additional
opportunities to strengthen the resilience of urban
sanitation systems against the backdrop of global
climate change.

Citywide Inclusive Sanitation (CWIS)
and climate change


Citywide Inclusive Sanitation in Times of

Climate Change is where:


- Everybody benefits from adequate resilient
sanitation service delivery outcomes that
meet user aspirations and protect the
health of users


- Human waste is safely managed along the
whole sanitation service chain, ensuring the
protection of the environment and human
health even under climate change-induced
shocks and stresses


- A diversity of appropriate technical solutions
is embraced, combining both on-site and
sewered solutions, in either centralized or
decentralized systems, with consideration of
resilience, safe resource recovery and reuse
as well as mitigation potential


- Cities demonstrate political will, technical
and managerial leadership and identify
new and creative long-term funding
options for sanitation, including climate
finance and carbon offsetting


- Institutional arrangements and regulations,
with well-aligned incentives, are in place
for the operation and maintenance of
the full sanitation service chain, including
under conditions of climate change and
increased uncertainty


- Funding is allocated for non-infrastructure
aspects of service delivery, such as capacity
building, household engagement and
outreach, sanitation marketing, climate
vulnerability mapping and co-design of
resilience measures


- Complementary urban services,
including water supply, drainage,
greywater management and solid waste
management, are incorporated into resilient
sanitation planning


- Activities are included to target specific
unserved and underserved groups, using
a risk informed approach (e.g., WHO’s
Sanitation Safety Planning), such as women,
ethnic minorities, the urban poor, people
with disabilities, and groups that are
particularly vulnerable to the impacts of
climate change on their sanitation systems
(either by location or by system type).

#### 5. Conclusion

There is a pressing need for a systemslevel, resilience-oriented approach to urban
sanitation planning and management that
transcends traditional sector boundaries. The
interconnectedness of sanitation systems with other
vital urban infrastructures—such as water supply,
transportation, energy, stormwater and solid waste
management—underscores the risk of cascading
failures that could heighten public health and
environmental hazards.


The potential for climate change to exacerbate
sanitation failures calls for a broadened perspective
on urban sanitation planning. This approach should
encompass the entire sanitation service chain, from
containment through to treatment and disposal or
safe reuse, while valuing the role of both sewered
and non-sewered systems. Building resilient
sanitation systems requires more than just physical
infrastructure; it also involves adopting adaptable
operational and management practices.


This paper’s sanitation climate-impact matrix
serves as a structured overview of the multifaceted
impacts of climate change on sanitation systems
and hence can be used for identifying targeted
adaptation strategies. Strategies that enhance
the resilience of sanitation systems to climaterelated stresses frequently not only help prevent
system failures but also have the potential to
reduce greenhouse gas emissions from sanitation
systems. However, more research is needed to fully
understand the dynamic interplay between climate
impacts on sanitation systems and GHG emissions.


The Citywide Inclusive Sanitation approach, offers
a robust framework for the integration of climate
change considerations into urban sanitation
planning. _CWIS in times of climate change_ focuses
on reducing urban vulnerabilities and moving
towards sustainable, resilient and inclusive
sanitation services for all urban residents.


We have proposed an initial list of indicators (see
Annex 1) that could be used for monitoring resilient
CWIS programs. However, as the discourse on
defining and standardizing indicators for climateresilient sanitation progresses among sector
experts, further standardization and refinement of
these indicators will be possible.
