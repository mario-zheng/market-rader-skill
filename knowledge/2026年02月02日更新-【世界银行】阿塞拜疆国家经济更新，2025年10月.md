## **FOREWORD**



It is with great pleasure that we present the
first Country Economic Update for Azerbaijan, marking a significant milestone in
our partnership and shared commitment to
supporting the country’s development aspirations. The Azerbaijan Country Economic Update (ACEU) is a bi-annual publication
that provides an overview of recent economic
developments and prospects in Azerbaijan.
Each report is structured in two parts: The
first part presents the analysis of the recent
economic developments and discusses the
medium-term economic outlook and risks; the
second part provides analysis of a specific
policy issue.


Azerbaijan has demonstrated remarkable
resilience in the face of global and regional
shocks over the past decade. The country’s
strategic location in the South Caucasus,
coupled with its abundant energy resources, has underpinned strong growth and substantial reductions in poverty. The government’s focus on macroeconomic stability has
helped Azerbaijan weather periods of volatility, including the 2015 oil price shock and the
COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, the country
has maintained fiscal and external surpluses,
kept public debt low, and accumulated sizable
macroeconomic buffers.



Yet, Azerbaijan stands at a critical juncture.
Declining oil production, and commodity price
volatility present challenges to the country’s
traditional growth model. The need to diversify the economy, foster private sector development, and invest in human capital has never
been more urgent. The government’s strategic vision, articulated in the “Azerbaijan 2030:
National Priorities for Socio-Economic Development” and the “Strategy of Socio-Economic Development for 2022-2026,” sets out a
bold agenda for a competitive, innovative, and
inclusive economy, with a strong emphasis on
green growth and social justice.


This inaugural Country Economic Update
aims to inform policy discussions, support evidence-based decision-making, and promote
dialogue among stakeholders. We hope it will
serve as a valuable resource for policymakers,
development partners, and the broader public
as Azerbaijan charts its path toward a prosperous and sustainable future.


**Stefanie Stallmeister**
World Bank Country Manager for Azerbaijan

## **OVERVIEW**

**Economic growth decelerated to 1.5**
**percent in the first half of 2025, driven**
**by contraction in the hydrocarbon sec-**
**tor and marked slowdown in non-hydro-**
**carbon sector output.** Hydrocarbon sector
output fell 3.2 percent (yoy) as daily crude oil
production declined 5.3 percent (yoy) while
natural gas production levelled. Non-hydrocarbon sector growth slowed to 3.9 percent
in H1 2025 from 6.9 percent in H1 2024, as
activity normalized in construction, transport,
ICT, and retail trade following strong gains
last year. Despite this slowdown, agriculture
and hospitality have maintained momentum.


**Inflation edged down by end-June as**
**domestic and external pressures sub-**
**sided.** Annual Inflation remained elevated in
the first quarter at 5.9 percent due to high
food and services prices fueled by direct and
indirect effects of the hike in administrative
prices in mid-2024 and in early 2025. In the
second quarter, inflationary pressures eased,
and annual inflation eased to 6 percent within
CBAR’s inflation target range (4+/-2 percent).
CBAR reduced the policy rate to 7 percent in
July 2025.


**External and fiscal accounts retained**
**significant surpluses, albeit moderated**
**due to lower hydrocarbon sector inflows.**
The Current Account Balance surplus eased
to 6.3 percent of GDP in H1 from 7.7 percent
of GDP in H1 2024, reflecting subdued hydrocarbon exports and a moderate pick up in
imports. The fiscal surplus eased to 7.7 percent of GDP in H1 2025 from 10.3 percent in
H1 2024 as revenues levelled while expenditures expanded, though at a moderate pace.
In H1 2025, non-hydrocarbon primary balance widened to 14.7 percent of non-hydrocarbon GDP from 13.9 percent in H1 2024



as non-hydrocarbon sector revenue collection
slowed. Macroeconomic buffers continued to
be strong, with SOFAZ reserves increasing by
14.8 percent (yoy) by end June and amounting to USD 66.5 billion (88 percent of GDP).
CBAR reserves inched up to USD 11.2 billion
equivalent to 5.6 months of imports.


**Looking ahead, economic growth is pro-**
**jected to remain low in 2025 and in the**
**medium-term, as domestic demand eas-**
**es on the back of slowing fiscal spending.**
Growth is projected to average 1.7 percent in
2025-2027 as the fiscal policy is expected to
become tighter in the face of declining crude
oil prices and projected decline in fiscal expenditures in real terms. Inflation is expected
to stay within the Central Bank’s target range.
Lower projected hydrocarbon prices will put
downward pressure on fiscal and external balances. Downside risks include protracted low
growth of the economy and steeper fall in energy prices. While macroeconomic buffers remain sufficient to counter the risks looming,
the resilience of the economy in the medium
to long term will be largely determined by the
degree of success in moving towards a more
diversified growth model, which requires significant structural reforms.

## **ECONOMIC** **OUTLOOK AND** **RISKS**



**Economic growth is estimated to slow in**
**the medium-term as factors that sup-**
**ported cyclical upturn are expected to**
**unwind.** Real GDP is projected to grow by
1.9 percent in 2025. A modest recovery in
non-hydrocarbon sector growth is expected
in H2 2025, as execution in public expenditure
improves toward year-end, but this will not be
sufficient to counter the decline in hydrocarbon output. Natural gas production is expected to peak in 2025 and level afterwards. Real
GDP growth is expected to remain subdued
in the medium-term, averaging 1.7 percent, as
crude oil production shrinks further and less
favorable hydrocarbon prices limit fiscal space
to support the non-hydrocarbon sector. This
outlook underscores the urgency of advancing structural reforms to foster private sector–led growth in the non-hydrocarbon sector.


**Inflation is expected ease in the rest**
**of 2025 and remain within the Central**
**Bank’s target** (4+/–2 percent). Inflation has
already peaked and, in the absence of major
external shocks, is expected to edge downward for the remainder of 2025 and stay
within the CBAR’s target in the medium-term.
Assuming there is no major hike in administrative prices, inflation is expected to moderate
in 2026 and 2027, averaging around 2.9 percent amid slowing domestic demand.



**The projected decline in hydrocarbon**
**prices is expected to reduce the fiscal**
**surplus, warranting bolder steps to-**
**wards fiscal consolidation.** With energy
prices projected to moderate, budget revenues are expected to contract, resulting in
a narrowing of the fiscal surplus to an estimated 3.6 percent of GDP in 2025 and an
average of 1.8 percent of GDP in 2026–2027.
As fiscal space tightens, public expenditures
(including investment) will need to decrease
to ensure adherence to fiscal rule targets.
The Medium-term Budget Document (20262029) (MTBD) published by the Ministry of
Finance points to a medium-term fiscal consolidation, in line with adherence to the fiscal
rule, and assumes a gradual decline in public
investment. The benchmark assumed in the
MTBD is USD 65 per barrel, above the World
Bank projection of USD 64 per barrel and
IMF’s projection of USD 62.8 per barrel; thus,
a re-assessment of the fiscal position may be
needed given recent developments.

**ed to narrow further in the medium term**
**due to declining energy prices.** Lower hydrocarbon exports due to worsened outlook
will push downwards the foreign trade surplus. Lower demand for imports because of
low growth will partially offset the fall in exports, preventing further deterioration. Profit
repatriation from the main natural gas field
development project is expected to remain

## **INTRODUCTION**



**Azerbaijan’s post-independence growth**
**performance has been linked to oil pro-**
**duction and prices.** Azerbaijan’s growth
started rising in the late 1990s, driven by
growing oil production and exports, and
reached unprecedented double-digit levels in
2005-2008, at the height of the global commodity price super-cycle. Growth slowed following the global financial crisis, averaging 3.2
percent in 2010-2014, before coming to an
abrupt halt following the sharp decline in oil
prices in 2014.


**Physical capital accumulation played a**
**major role in driving growth.** During 20002023 capital accumulation accounted for
about a third of the recorded GDP growth
(Figure 13).


- Prior to 2005, more than half of GDP
growth could be attributed to physical
capital accumulation due to high state investment in exploration of hydrocarbon resources driven by FDI, and investments in
transportation capacity.


- The oil boom of 2005-2009 was characterized by rapid GDP growth. Since the
contribution of capital and labor remained
fairly constant, the standard GDP decomposition tool attributes the higher residual GDP growth (“the Solow residual”)
to possible improvements in total factor
productivity (TFP) gains. In fact, however,
these gains are likely simply a reflection of
oil windfall rents in the energy sector. In
this period, physical capital accumulation
was largely driven by public investment in
infrastructure, accounting for 18 percent
of the real GDP growth.




- As oil production decreased following its
peak in 2010, the contribution to growth
attributed to TFP turned negative; however, as explained, this simply reflects declining oil rents. State-led physical capital
accumulation (driven by public investment
in infrastructure) was the sole factor supporting growth during this period.


- The collapse in global oil prices in 2014-15
led to a banking sector crisis and fiscal adjustment in Azerbaijan. This, coupled with
declining oil production, resulted in slower
capital accumulation and stunted growth
in following years (2015-23).


**The strategic goal of economic diversifi-**
**cation hinges on creating the conditions**
**for private investment, particularly in**
**the non-hydrocarbon sector.** Azerbaijan’s Socio-Economic Development Strategy
(2022-2026) stresses the importance of diversifying the economy and transitioning towards a new growth model centered around
the private sector. In this regard, mobilizing
private investment is critical to revive growth
and lift the long-term growth potential of the
non-hydrocarbon sector. To make that possible, Azerbaijan will need to enhance the conditions for private capital attraction through
key enabling reforms. This special topic section focuses on analyzing recent investment
trends and identifying bottlenecks preventing
higher levels of private investment.

**involving private sector in operations**

payment structures. Operation and maintenance concessions (service concessions) go

and expertise, thereby supplementing limited public resources and accelerating project
delivery.

#### Part 2.e
## **CONCLUSIONS**



**The 2022-2026 Social and Economic**
**Development Strategy of Azerbaijan**
**aims at diversifying the economy away**
**from the hydrocarbon sector, with the**
**private sector playing a more prominent**
**role.** The strategy prioritizes transitioning to
a new private sector driven growth model. The
success of these goals hinges on increasing
investment levels, particularly private investment in the non-hydrocarbon sector.


**Achieving** **diversification** **goals** **and**
**shifting to a higher growth path requires**
**boosting non-hydrocarbon private in-**
**vestment, which has remained stagnant**
**over the past two decades.** Total investment dropped from around 30 percent of
GDP in 2013 and 2014 to less than 20 percent of GDP during the past three years. The
reduction in public investment post-2015,
due to decreased hydrocarbon revenues, has
been the primary driver of the overall investment decline. Foreign Investment into the
non-hydrocarbon economy declined as well
from around 5 percent of non-hydrocarbon
GDP in 2015 to just 0.8 percent as of 2023.
Meanwhile, domestic private investment in
the non-hydrocarbon sectors remained weak
and concentrated on inward-looking sectors
like construction, which is not conducive to
economic diversification.



**Raising private sector investment in**
**non-hydrocarbon sector requires imple-**
**mentation of structural reforms aimed**
**at eliminating constraints to the busi-**
