According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), by late 2024, there were
123.2 million forcibly displaced people worldwide. [1] A growing trend in the past 5 years, there are now 16.3 million
refugees, asylum seekers, returnees, internally displaced, and stateless persons in the Asia and Pacific region
alone, many of whom struggle to achieve durable solutions. [2] Forced displacement has multifaceted implications,
including disrupting lives and livelihoods; putting pressure on services and infrastructure; and often exacerbating
vulnerabilities, poverty, and inequality. This poses significant challenges to the achievement of sustainable and
inclusive development.


Over the past decade, a series of interconnected global United Nations (UN) and intergovernmental
initiatives have worked to better equip the international community in advancing durable solutions to forced
displacement. From the drafting of the 2018 Global Compact on Refugees (GCR) to the 2025 UN Guidance
on Solutions to Internal Displacement, these processes stressed that solutions must be based on reliable,
timely, and disaggregated data and analysis, ideally produced through national systems with support from
international partners when needed. This is imperative for understanding the scale, causal factors, and long-term
implications of displacement. Reliable data, produced through sustainable approaches, is needed to formulate
solutions‑oriented policies and responses. These global initiatives have influenced and accompanied evolutions
in the data landscape in the Asia and Pacific region.


Aligned with the increasing shift toward integrating development approaches with humanitarian efforts—
and the heightened attention given to forced displacement by multilateral development banks (MDBs)—
the Asian Development Bank (ADB) recognizes that improved data and analytical work related to forced
displacement informs policy dialogue, programming, and operational design. ADB’s Strategy 2030 provides an
institutional framework that prioritizes inclusiveness, resilience, and sustainable development. [3] ADB’s _Fragile_
_and Conflict‑Affected Situations and Small Island Developing States Approach_ specifically identifies displaced
persons as a key target group in achieving the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). [4] The bank’s _Disaster Risk_
_Management Action Plan 2024–2030_ also recognizes the growing risk of disaster displacement. [5]

Recent ADB studies highlight that data is crucial for understanding the impact of forced displacement
and developing effective responses, including when displacement is driven by disasters. [6] Context-specific
understanding and solutions emerging from analytical work supports institutional change and the needs of the
displaced—including women and children—as well as host communities. Yet, it appears that data, especially
in disaggregated form, is often unavailable or complex to collect in locations facing fragility and conflict.


In November 2024, ADB engaged the Asia Displacement Solutions Platform to review the data landscape on
solutions to forced displacement in Asia and the Pacific. [7] Against the backdrop of global initiatives, the objectives
were to identify relevant available datasets and actors in the region, outline methodologies employed, and
describe key gaps and challenges. This study focuses on data at national and regional levels, covering a spectrum
of displacement contexts, including situations of internal and cross-border displacement related to conflict
and natural hazards. It also aims to identify potential opportunities for ADB to support its developing member
countries (DMCs), develop relevant partnerships, and inform ADB’s operations and projects.


Finally, this study was impacted by the disruptions experienced by the humanitarian and development sectors
since the start of 2025, particularly a drastic reduction in available funding for many data producers as well as
ongoing restructuring and contraction of their capacities. Those parameters have been factored into the analysis
and recommendations, given the evolving situation which ultimately underscores the need and opportunity to
support government systems for sustained responses to data needs on displaced populations.

##### Early Developments


Conceptualizing and promoting durable solutions for refugees has a long history (Figure 1). In the current era,
solutions for refugees derive primarily from the protection and responsibility-sharing principles of the
1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol, [9] and the 1950 Statute of the Office of
the UNHCR. [10] Within this framework, a durable solution is achieved when a refugee (re-) establishes a _permanent_
_legal status_, through one of three pathways: voluntary repatriation to the country of origin, local integration in the
country of refuge, or resettlement in a third country. [11]


Recognizing internal displacement as a phenomenon and devising ways to address it have taken much longer.
The (non-legally binding) 1998 Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement was the first key milestone toward
comprehensive UN guidance on the subject. [12] It outline the responsibilities of governments and other actors, and
emphasizes that durable solutions must be voluntary, safe, and dignified, and that internally displaced persons
(IDPs) must be able to fully enjoy their human rights. To outline how to make progress toward solutions, in 2010
the UN Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) developed its Framework on Durable Solutions for IDPs.
This framework put forward a broadly accepted definition of solutions to internal displacement: “A durable
solution is achieved when IDPs no longer have specific assistance and protection needs that are linked to their

Global Initiatives on Solutions to Forced Displacement Since the Mid-2010s


Over the past decade, the global focus on durable solutions to forced displacement has significantly intensified,
resulting in structural changes and innovations in coordination and interventions. The growing number of IDPs
and refugees, the increasingly protracted nature of conflicts and displacement situations, the cumulative impact
of climate change and natural hazards on displacement dynamics, and the reduced funding for emergencies
have all contributed to a rethinking of humanitarian and development assistance toward more sustainable and
integrated approaches. Central to this shift was the recognition that humanitarian interventions alone cannot
create conditions for sustainable solutions, and that states should be encouraged and equipped to fulfill their
responsibilities toward displaced populations.


One of the main milestones came with the adoption of the 2018 GCR. [14] Its four main objectives (para. 7) center
on solutions, reflecting the compact’s aim “to facilitate access to durable solutions” (para. 85). As such, the
GCR established tools and arrangements designed primarily to better protect and assist refugees, support host
countries and communities, and ultimately promote durable solutions. Supported by a Global Refugee Forum

At the time, many stakeholders also felt that IDPs would be left off this global compact agenda. [16] Despite the
scale of internal displacement, limited global attention was attributed to a lack of political will, fear of interference
in states’ internal affairs, and weak international frameworks, turning IDPs into an invisible majority. [17] Therefore,
civil society and leading states called on the UN Secretary-General (UNSG) to dedicate specific attention to
internal displacement and ensure IDPs would not “fall through the cracks” of the two global compacts. [18] As a
result, in 2019 a High-Level Panel on Internal Displacement was established, followed by the UNSG Action
Agenda on Internal Displacement and a time-bound UN Special Adviser on Solutions to Internal Displacement. [19]
This led to UN commitments to find durable solutions for IDPs, to prevent displacement and provide protection
and assistance. Between 2019 and 2024, a “Group of Friends” in Geneva and New York brought regular feedback
from governments and from 2022, 15 countries piloted solutions efforts. [20] While UN-centric, those efforts
placed a strong emphasis on working with and through government systems and called for a change in mindset
to support national ownership, responsibility, and accountability for the IDP solutions agenda. [21] This was also
predicated on a strengthened humanitarian–development–peace nexus and development investments. [22] As the
Office of the Special Adviser closed at the end of 2024, two major evolutions took place:


   - In 2024, 28 UN entities developed a UN System-Wide Approach to Internal Displacement, recognizing
the need for different ways of working; they also set up new institutional mechanisms, including a new
Hub for Coordination of Solutions to Internal Displacement and a Global Solutions Data Platform.

   - In February 2025, the work of the Office of the Special Adviser resulted in the publication of a new
_Guidance on Solutions to Internal Displacement_, endorsed by both the UN Sustainable Development
Group (UNSDG) and the IASC. [23] Synthesizing insights gained over the years, this document offers
guidance to UN staff and other relevant actors on supporting governments in their solutions efforts.

UN Development Programme (UNDP). This trend highlights a growing recognition of the link with the SDGs
and its central principle of “leave no one behind,” including displaced populations. Major evolutions include

##### Data Implications of Global Solutions Initiatives


In the mid-2010s, considerable progress was made to advance data on refugees, notably through the affirmation
of the GCR in 2018. States recognized the importance of supporting harmonized or interoperable standards
for the collection, analysis, and sharing of disaggregated data, and the inclusion of refugees in national
statistical systems. [27] The GCR included a section emphasizing that “improving data and evidence will also
support efforts to achieve solutions.” Relevant pledges were made at Global Refugee Forums, notably under
a multistakeholder pledge. [28] Moreover, within the GCR follow-up and review process, UNHCR developed an
indicator framework feeding into the regular GCR Indicator Report which measures progress toward achieving
the GCR objectives, including solutions. [29] Concurrently, the UN Statistical Commission developed the 2018
_International Recommendations on Refugee Statistics (IRRS)_, an effort by UN member states to address the need

Box: What Is Data for Solutions?


“Data for solutions to forced displacement” refers to the collection, analysis, and use of statistical evidence to inform
policies, programs, and interventions aimed at achieving durable solutions for forcibly displaced persons. This process
extends beyond basic stock and flow figures to measure the extent to which displacement-related vulnerabilities are
being addressed. This involves assessing whether displaced individuals can fully enjoy their rights, regain agency, rebuild
the capacity to pursue personal development goals and aspirations, contribute meaningfully to national and local
development, and ultimately move beyond aid dependency.


This entails collecting socioeconomic data to assess the degree of displaced persons’ (re)integration into society.
Measuring progress toward durable solutions begins with evaluating the extent to which displaced individuals have
regained or been granted permanent legal status, documentation, and civic rights, along with access to effective
remedies and justice. Other important indicators include the restoration of personal security; adequate living conditions;
access to livelihoods, health care, and education; as well as the recovery of housing, land, and property.

which are commonly less actively involved than others, and more focused on statelessness than refugee or IDP
populations (footnote 43). Nevertheless, two regional institutions are members of EGRISS, the UN Economic and
Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific and its regional training arm, the UN Statistical Institute for Asia and
the Pacific. The Global Annual Inclusion Survey, which monitors global progress on statistical inclusion of displaced
populations, also reveals that Asia ranked last on the number of relevant projects currently implemented, although
there has been a growing trend over the past couple of years. [68] There were also noteworthy activities on specific
thematic areas such as the March 2024 Asia-Pacific Regional Workshop on Implementing Guidance on Inclusive
Civil Registration and Vital Statistics Systems, which gathered 11 governments in the region. [69] This workshop
emphasized, among other things, the importance of producing inclusive vital statistics and development indicators
and improving integration of displaced and stateless individuals in statistical systems. It set the stage for continued
regional dialogue and actions. [70]


Specifically on internal displacement, while Asia did not feature prominently in the High-Level Panel final report, some
contributions were made by states and civil society organizations (CSOs) in the region. Consultations with displaced
communities took place in Afghanistan, [71] Bangladesh, Indonesia, and the Philippines. [72] A November 2020 regional
virtual consultation on disaster displacement emphasized that Asia and the Pacific is the world’s most disaster-prone
region, with over 80% of documented disaster displacement between 2008 and 2019. [73] Since 2022, the UNSG Action
Agenda and the Special Adviser on Solutions to Internal Displacement included Afghanistan and Vanuatu as examples,
although those did not feature prominently in communications. [74] Nevertheless, the 2023 _Proposal for Improving Data_
_for Solutions to Internal Displacement (DSID)_ was developed based on country case studies and surveys, including some
in Asia. [75] Afghanistan is also one of the pilot countries in which the related diagnostic tool is being tested.


The above, therefore, seems to confirm that the Asia and Pacific region has been relatively less visible in global
processes addressing solutions to internal and cross-border displacement. However, there is an interesting basis
for the region to further contribute to and benefit from global initiatives. To better understand this issue, it is also
important to evaluate the data capacities and challenges specific to the region.

States’ Data Engagement and Capacities in the Region


States’ capacity to produce data on displacement, including durable solutions, varies widely depending
on several factors such as governance, infrastructure, security, stability, technical capacity, expertise, and
political will. The capacity of the region’s countries to produce data on forced displacement varies accordingly.
Commonwealth Nations such as India and Sri Lanka tend to have inherited similar reporting structures and
capacities, which simplify data tracing, validation, and interpretation (footnote 77). Most countries in the region
have established national statistical institutions. However, in fragile settings, governments tend to have weak
statistical systems and limited institutional capacity to collect granular socioeconomic data.


When limitations stem from capacity deficits, international organizations and NGOs can play a bridging role in
supporting and strengthening states’ data expertise and capabilities. This is done through programs that produce
operational data, geared to inform specific projects often done collaboratively with government ministries and
agencies. For instance, UNHCR works closely with the Government of Bangladesh on producing data related
to the refugee population hosted in Cox’s Bazar district. Since 2018, UNHCR and Bangladesh’s Refugee Relief
and Repatriation Commissioner have jointly conducted biometric registration to collect individual data and
deliver identification cards to refugees useful in accessing services. This process ensures that both UNHCR and
the government have harmonized and verified population data. Results are regularly published through joint
population dashboards, maps, and factsheets available on the UNHCR Bangladesh operational data portal. [90]
Aside from cooperating with the government, UNHCR contributes to other data efforts such as Inter-Sector
Needs Assessments coordinated by the Inter-Sector Coordination Group and aiming to ensure humanitarian
actors and the authorities can respond to up-to-date needs. [91] The Inter-Sector Coordination Group recently

With data protection safeguards in place, international data actors usually collaborate with governments, building
their capacities to adhere to statistical international norms and standards, collect quality disaggregated data
on forcibly displaced populations, and validate the data collected. There is also some collaboration between
producers of national statistics and external operational data producers. [94] Operational data can be crucial
to decision-making on responses, programming, and resource allocations because it is focused, detailed, and
usually much more timely than official statistics such as censuses. [95] It is also sometimes the only displacement
data available. Therefore, when integrating socioeconomic aspects, operational data can contribute importantly
to solutions programming. [96]


There are, however, inherent weaknesses associated with the operational nature of such data efforts. First,
short-term funding cycles often lead to a lack of coherence among the various datasets produced by different
actors, for different purposes and at different times. This results in challenges related to data heterogeneity,
fragmentation, and interoperability. Investigating specific queries by using data from multiple countries or sources
can be complex as it is often impossible to get the same level of granularity across the board. As mentioned by
one key informant, “The harmonization problem is not trivial: Sometimes one can be surprised by the amount
of [household level] data collected but it remains very complex or even impossible to harmonize it to make valid
comparisons” (footnote 65). This suggests clear limitations on the insights that operational data can provide
for solutions.


Even when produced jointly with governments, operational data tends to lack connection to national systems
and is therefore largely unable to provide longitudinal details and analyses needed to plan for solutions and
development approaches. For example, operational data actors increasingly emphasize area-based, localized
surveys which have shown limitations in their ability to connect with national development plans. One key
informant stressed, “The only way to get sustainability is working with national statistical offices, but this requires
governments’ recognition of [the link between development and displacement as a] long-term challenge”
(footnote 43). While key informants have highlighted progress made to improve the interoperability of different
datasets, this is a gradual evolution particularly connected to adherence to common standards. Operational data
has an important complementary role to play next to official statistics, but this requires further development in
national systems.

citizens, through censuses and other surveys and tools. However, marginalized groups, especially displaced
persons, are often excluded (footnote 43). [97] Governments can collect data on any population in their territory,
but this does not necessarily translate into collecting and using socioeconomic data or including displaced

The Impact of Funding Shortfalls on Data Actors and Systems


Over the past decade, regional capacities to develop, disseminate, and analyze data on displacement has
increased. Progress has been more prominent for operational data than with official statistics. It has not
necessarily focused specifically on the production of data to inform solutions to displacement, but datasets
covering socioeconomic perspectives are likely to inform relevant planning and development approaches.
This study has also confirmed that developing and maintaining data infrastructures is resource demanding
and a long-term investment.


However, since January 2025, the world has entered a new era of declining humanitarian and development
funding with liquidity challenges, primarily due to substantial reductions in donor contributions and the
suspension of key programs. [118] As a result, the aid sector is going through drastic downsizing, restructuring, and
re-prioritizing exercises, with potentially severe implications. While processes are ongoing at the time of writing,
the UN and its agencies have announced reductions in staffing worldwide and in the region. In March 2025, the
UNSG launched “UN80,” a comprehensive reform agenda aimed at ensuring the global institution remains fit for
purpose as it enters its 80th year. [119] NGOs are also adjusting to this new environment, reducing their presence
in the region and reorganizing their governance structures and activities. While there is little information and
evidence available yet on the direct consequences for countries in the region, the declining aid capacity is likely
to have a major impact on fragile and conflict-affected situations. The evolving trade and economic landscape is
also likely to affect development dynamics, states’ resources, capacities, priorities, and political dynamics.

“Data for solutions to forced displacement” refers to the collection, analysis, and use of statistical evidence to
inform policies, programs, and interventions aimed at achieving durable solutions for forcibly displaced persons. [1]
It also supports the monitoring of progress made by individuals and communities toward these solutions and
helps identify the barriers that hinder their realization.


While this process includes counting those who have achieved—or are on a pathway to achieving—a durable
solution, it extends beyond basic stock-and-flow figures to also measure the extent to which displacementrelated vulnerabilities are being addressed. This involves assessing whether displaced individuals can fully enjoy
their rights, regain agency, rebuild the capacity to pursue personal development goals and aspirations, contribute
meaningfully to national and local development, and ultimately move beyond aid dependency.


This entails collecting socioeconomic data to assess the degree of (re)integration into society. Experts have
proposed a range of indicators for this purpose, which are commonly grouped into three core categories: legal
safety, physical safety, and material safety. [2] Whether displaced internally or across borders, measuring progress
toward durable solutions begins with evaluating the extent to which displaced individuals have regained or
been granted permanent legal status, documentation, and civic rights, along with access to effective remedies
and justice. Indicators on physical and material safety include the restoration of personal security, adequate
living conditions, access to livelihoods, health care, and education, as well as the recovery of housing, land,
and property. The variety of indicators allows for approaches tailored to the specificity of populations groups
and contexts.

To enable comprehensive analysis and meaningful comparisons, such data should be disaggregated—particularly
by age and gender—and ideally collected through complementary approaches under three categories: [3]


          - **Official statistics.** These are produced using scientific and professional methods and published by
national statistics offices or by statistical units within government ministries that form part of national
statistical systems. These systems comprise agencies regulated by national statistics laws and other
relevant statistical regulations and standards. They rely on both traditional and nontraditional sources,
including sometimes operational data or administrative data.

          - **National statistics.** Broader than official statistics, these are produced within a country and used for
national purposes but may not fully meet the quality criteria or production processes of official statistics.

          - **Operational data.** Collected by a range of organizations, including government ministries and
humanitarian and development actors, operational data primarily informs decisions concerning
programming, responses, and resource allocation associated with humanitarian action. Some, but not all,
use professional and scientific methods. Operational data tends to be timelier than official statistics, but
may not be subject to the same rigor and validation. At times, this may be the only type of data available,
especially where national statistical systems are not functioning or lack significant capacity.


To measure progress toward durable solutions, indicators allow for different comparative methods:


          - comparing the situation of displaced with nondisplaced populations to assess variations (the approach
of the 2010 Inter-Agency Standing Committee framework);

          - comparing the situation of displaced populations with (minimum) standards on access to legal, physical,
and material safety; and

          - comparing to situations predating displacement, which allows to monitor progress as well as evolving
needs of the identified population.


An additional approach consists in addressing “soft barriers” to solutions, i.e., information on feelings of
belonging, acceptance, and social cohesion in host areas as well as areas of return. This more qualitative aspect
is rarely covered by data yet important to ensure durable solutions.

The objective of this background study is to provide a succinct overview that (i) investigates the situation of data
availability on forced displacement in the Asia and Pacific region; (ii) examines the extent of this data’s quality;
(iii) identifies potential gaps and challenges in data collection, analysis, and use; and (iv) recommends how the
Asian Development Bank may position itself on the regional and global data landscape to support its developing
member countries and its operations.


**Scope and parameters:** The study focuses on data relevant to the three internationally recognized durable
solutions to forced displacement: voluntary repatriation/return, local integration, and resettlement/relocation.
The study includes data at national and regional level, covering the full spectrum of displacement contexts
(i.e., situations of internal and cross-border displacement related to persecution, conflict, and disasters). [1]


**Methodology:** The research employed a mixed-methods approach that included the following:


1. **Desk review:** A review of existing databases, online repositories, reports, methodological manuals, and
other relevant publications from key stakeholders within the United Nations (UN) system, civil society, and
government actors. This included a review of both global data initiatives, and selected national and regional
efforts, as well as recent intergovernmental and UN initiatives on solutions.

2. **Stakeholder mapping and key informant interviews:** Engagement with data producers and users
to understand capacities, methodologies, challenges and evolutions. Thirteen semi-structured interviews
were conducted with experts in UN agencies and other international and nongovernment organizations
to gather information to be shared without attribution between December 2024 and June 2025.

3. **Data categorization and analysis:** Identified data sources were catalogued to capture key attributes
(e.g., geographic coverage, thematic focus, collection methodology, frequency, granularity, disaggregation,
accessibility, and other strengths and weaknesses). A gap analysis identified areas with limited or no data
and assessed challenges and opportunities.

The section below provides definitions of key terms used in or directly related to this study. The definitions are
mostly derived from the United Nations High Commission for Refugees (UNHCR) master glossary of terms,
International Organization for Migration (IOM) Glossary on Migration, and the World Bank Data Help Desk. [1]


**Asylum seeker:** A general term for any person who is seeking international protection. In some countries, it is
used as a legal term referring to a person who has applied for refugee status or a complementary international
protection status and has not yet received a final decision on their claim. It can also refer to a person who has not
yet submitted an application but may intend to do so, or may be in need of international protection.


**Complementary pathways for admission to third countries:** Existing legal admission pathways that are
adjusted for persons in need of international protection, as well as newly created programs that provide for lawful
stay in a third country where the international protection needs of the beneficiaries are met. These programs are
additional to and separate from resettlement programs. They are solutions-oriented and progressive pathways
to permanent residency or citizenship.


**Cross-border displacement:** The movements of persons who have been forced or obliged to leave their homes
or places of habitual residence and move across international borders.


**Data disaggregation:** The breakdown of summary data into smaller units or categories, such as by sex, age,
ethnicity, disability status, geographic location, or other characteristics, to better understand inequalities and
support evidence-based policymaking.


**Data protection:** The systematic application of a set of institutional, technical, and physical safeguards that
preserve the right to privacy with respect to the collection, storage, use, and disclosure of personal data.


**Disaster displacement:** Situations where people are forced to leave their homes or places of habitual residence
as a result of a disaster or in order to avoid the impact of an immediate and foreseeable natural hazard.
Such displacement results from the fact that affected persons are exposed to a natural hazard in a situation
where they lack the resilience to withstand its impacts.

**Durable solution (refugees):** In the refugee context, a durable solution requires (re-)establishment of
a permanent legal status and generally involves voluntary repatriation, local integration, or resettlement.


**Durable solution (internally displaced persons):** For internally displaced persons, a durable solution is
achieved when they no longer have any specific assistance and protection needs that are linked to their
displacement and can enjoy their human rights without discrimination on account of their displacement.


**Flows:** Illustrate movements, capturing the arrival or departure of displaced populations in or from a place over
a defined period of time. Flows are critical to track movement dynamics—how many people are displaced in or
out within a timeframe.


**Forced displacement:** The movement of persons who have been forced or obliged to flee or to leave their
homes or places of habitual residence (whether within their own country or across an international border),
in particular as a result of or in order to avoid the effects of armed conflict, situations of generalized violence,
violations of human rights, or natural hazards.


**Forced migration:** A migratory movement which, although the drivers can be diverse, involves force, compulsion,
or coercion.


**Forced return:** The return of foreign nationals to their country of origin or country of transit against their will.


**Host community:** A community that hosts large populations of refugees or internally displaced persons, whether
in camps, integrated into households, or independently.


**Host country:** The country in which a non-national stays or resides, whether legally or irregularly.


**Humanitarian–development–peace nexus:** This is intended to ensure strong cooperation, collaboration, and
coordination between humanitarian, development, and peace-building efforts at the national level 3 to ensure
collective outcomes on the basis of joined-up, coherent, complementary, and risk-informed analysis, planning,
and action.


**Internal displacement:** The involuntary or forced movement, evacuation, or relocation of persons or groups of
persons within internationally recognized state borders.


**Internally displaced person:** A person who has been forced or obliged to flee from their home or place of
habitual residence, in particular as a result of or in order to avoid the effects of armed conflicts, situations of
generalized violence, violations of human rights, or natural hazards, and who has not crossed an internationally
recognized state border.


**Local integration:** A durable solution for refugees that involves their permanent settlement in a host country.
Local integration is a complex and gradual process, comprising three distinct but interrelated dimensions: legal,
economic, and sociocultural. The process is often concluded with the naturalization of the refugee.
Local integration can also apply to internally displaced persons.


**Microdata:** Unit-level data obtained from sample surveys, censuses, and administrative systems. They provide
information about characteristics of individual people or entities such as households, business enterprises,
facilities, farms, or even geographic areas such as villages or towns. They allow in-depth understanding of
socioeconomic issues by studying relationships and interactions among phenomena. Microdata are thus key to
designing projects and formulating policies, targeting interventions, and monitoring and measuring the impact
and results of projects, interventions, and policies.

**Person displaced in the context of disasters and climate change:** A person who is forced or obliged to leave
their home or place of habitual residence as a result of disaster or in order to avoid the impact of an immediate,
foreseeable natural hazard including the adverse impacts of climate change. Most often, such persons are
displaced within their own country, but they may also be displaced across an international border.


**Planned relocation:** In the context of disasters or environmental degradation, including when due to climate
change, a planned process in which persons or groups of persons move or are assisted to move away from their
homes or place of temporary residence, are settled in a new location, and provided with the conditions for
rebuilding their lives.


**Refugee:** Any person who meets the eligibility criteria under an applicable refugee definition, as provided for
in international or regional refugee instruments, under the mandate of the UNHCR, or in national legislation.
Under international law and UNHCR’s mandate, refugees are persons outside their countries of origin who
need international protection because of feared persecution, or a serious threat to their life, physical integrity, or
freedom in their country of origin as a result of persecution, armed conflict, violence, or serious public disorder.
Under international law, a person is considered a refugee as soon as they meet the relevant criteria, whether
or not they have been formally recognized as a refugee. A person does not become a refugee because of
recognition, but rather is recognized because they are a refugee. Sometimes—notably in statistical contexts—
the word “refugee” is used to designate individuals or groups who have been formally recognized by states or
UNHCR as entitled to refugee status following an asylum or other status-determination procedure. When this
limited nonlegal meaning is intended, it should be clearly indicated. The clearest designation in such contexts is
recognized refugee.


**Refugee status determination procedure:** The legal or administrative process by which governments
or UNHCR determine whether a person seeking international protection is considered a refugee under
international, regional, or national law.


**Reintegration:** A process which enables returnees to regain the physical, social, legal, and material security
needed to maintain life, livelihood, and dignity, and which eventually leads to the disappearance of any distinction
or discrimination in relation to their compatriots.


**Relocation (internally displaced persons):** The transfer and integration of internally displaced persons into
another geographic area within the same country as one of the three forms of durable solutions.


**Resettlement (refugees):** The selection and transfer of refugees from a state in which they have sought treaty
protection to a third state that has agreed to admit them—as refugees—with permanent residence status.
The status provided ensures protection against refoulement and provides a resettled refugee and their dependents
with access to rights similar to those enjoyed by nationals. Resettlement also carries with it the opportunity
to eventually become a naturalized citizen of the resettlement country. As such, resettlement is a mechanism
for refugee protection, a durable solution, and an example of international burden and responsibility sharing.


**Registration:** The recording, verifying, and updating of information on forcibly displaced individuals with the aim
of protecting, assisting, and documenting them and of implementing durable solutions.


**Return:** In a general sense, the act or process of going back or being taken back to the point of departure. This could
be within the territorial boundaries of a country, as in the case of returning internally displaced persons.


**Returnee:** A former refugee or internally displaced person who has returned from a host country or a place of
internal displacement to their place of origin or former habitual residence, spontaneously or in an organized
fashion, with the intention of remaining there permanently and who is yet to be fully integrated.


38 Appendixes


**Socioeconomic data:** Information that relates to both social and economic factors of a population. This data are
used to analyze the well-being, living conditions, and financial situation of individuals or communities. This kind
of data can help make informed decisions about resource allocation, policy development, and service provision,
among other areas.


**Stocks:** Provide information about the total number of displaced persons in a place at a given time, usually the
end of the year. Stocks capture the snapshot of how many displaced people are currently present, reflecting
cumulative movement plus births, deaths, returns, or resettlement/relocations.


**Third-country solutions:** Safe and regulated avenues that provide recognized refugees and other persons in
need of international protection with lawful stay in a country other than the country of origin or first host country.


**Voluntary repatriation:** The free and informed return of refugees to their country of origin in safety and dignity.
Voluntary repatriation may be organized (i.e., when it takes place under the auspices of the concerned states
and/or UNHCR) or spontaneous (i.e., when refugees repatriate by their own means with little or no direct
involvement from government authorities or UNHCR).

Toward Durable Solutions
_Mapping and Analysis of Displacement Data in Asia and the Pacific_

This publication examines how more robust national data systems can support durable, inclusive solutions
to forced displacement across Asia and the Pacific. A collaboration between the Asian Development
Bank and the Asia Displacement Solutions Platform, it analyzes existing data systems and identifies how
stronger evidence can inform better policies, programs, and interventions for displaced persons and host
communities. By highlighting significant gaps and opportunities, the publication urges governments to
step up the collection and integration of displacement data. It also underscores the importance of regional
cooperation in shaping resilient, long-term solutions for displaced people.
