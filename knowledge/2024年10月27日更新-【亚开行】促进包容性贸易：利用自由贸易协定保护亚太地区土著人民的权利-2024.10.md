This brief explores free trade agreement (FTA) provisions related to Indigenous Peoples,
focusing on traditional knowledge and genetic resources within economies in Asia and
the Pacific. Given the substantial presence of Indigenous Peoples in Asia and the Pacific,
and the critical importance traditional knowledge and genetic resources (TK/GR) have
for climate action, food security, advances in medicine, as well as language diversity and
cultural heritage (Relevance of Traditional Knowledge and Genetic Resources to Critical
Issues in Asia and the Pacific), this brief explores how and to what extent traditional
knowledge provisions may promote regional cooperation, safeguard Indigenous Peoples’
rights, and navigate the complex relationship between these rights and TK/GR.

##### INDIGENOUS PEOPLES IN ASIA AND THE PACIFIC


Of the estimated 370 million Indigenous Peoples in the world, 70% are from Asia and
the Pacific. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations
(FAO), Indigenous Peoples in the region are extremely vulnerable and marginalized,
although they have developed a rich body of traditional knowledge, including biodiversity
and agriculture, because of the strong connection to their land and other territories. [1]
Despite significant disparities between indigenous and non-indigenous populations in
key indicators such as life expectancy, income, educational attainment, and employment,
indigenous knowledge and resources are recognized as invaluable to addressing
challenges faced by people worldwide, and furthering national and regional economies. [2]

##### REGIONAL COOPERATION AND INDIGENOUS PEOPLES


In Asia and the Pacific, economic cooperation at present is
characterized by subregional groups such as the South Asian
Association for Regional Cooperation or SAARC, the Economic
Cooperation Organization (ECO), the Association of Southeast
Asian Nations (ASEAN), the Pacific Islands Forum, the Central
Asia Regional Economic Cooperation or CAREC Program, as well as
“natural economic areas” such as the Greater Mekong Subregion. [3]
Such groups focus mainly on trade, but also cooperate on issues
such as information and communication technology, finance,
and socioeconomic matters. [4] Despite addressing socioeconomic
concerns, and regional cooperation and integration focusing on
reducing poverty and inequality, [5] no research to date has explored
the benefits of regional cooperation on Indigenous Peoples.



To allow the integration of indigenous communities with regional
development, a 2019 OECD study gave several recommendations,
including the (i) need for better indigenous statistics and data
governance; (ii) need to develop a supportive environment for small
business development and indigenous entrepreneurship at both
local and regional levels, including through ensuring stronger cultural
intellectual protection for indigenous services and products;
(iii) strengthening indigenous land tenure systems to enable
prospects for economic development; and (iv) putting in place a
“place-based approach” to economic development that enhances
policy consistency and empowers indigenous communities
(footnote 2).


However, no similar study has been done for the Asia and Pacific
region. It should be noted that one study published by the Asia
Indigenous Peoples Pact [6] observed that ASEAN member states
have not been able to protect Indigenous Peoples’ rights that may
be affected by resource extraction and development initiatives
implemented in indigenous territories by multinational companies.

##### TRADE AND INDIGENOUS PEOPLES


Indigenous civilizations have historically traded among each other
or with European nations, subject to trade laws that were a core
aspect of treaties with other nations. [7] Similar to modern-day
trade, language differences were historically a characteristic of
trade carried out by Indigenous Peoples, and trade protocols were
developed to address these differences.


It was also observed that indigenous treaties should be considered
preexisting commitments that bind nation-states under the Vienna
Convention on the Law of Treaties (footnote 7). Furthermore, the
United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples
(UNDRIP) Article 36 affirms the basic trade liberties for Indigenous
Peoples across country borders. This highlights the importance
for nation-states to detect possible contradictions between both
present and future investment agreements and existing indigenous
treaties, so that nation-states may then avoid resulting inconsistent
obligations or dissenting provisions.


However, indigenous treaty rights have not been recognized or
integrated in international law, nor in any of the 3,600 treaties that
control investment and trade as of 2020, despite the fact that
international trade exerts pressure on the resources, lands, and
territories of Indigenous Peoples. [8]

In a United Nations (UN) brief on the impact of international
investment and free trade on the human rights of Indigenous
Peoples, presented during the 70th session of the UN General
Assembly on 7 August 2015, [9] Victoria Tauli-Corpuz, then
Special Rapporteur of the Human Rights Council on the rights
of Indigenous Peoples, observed that (i) Indigenous Peoples are
not party to negotiations and development of FTAs, though FTA
provisions impact their self-governance, and the management and
utilization of their territories and resources; (ii) the provisions of
international investment agreements have significant implications
for indigenous rights and states’ “protective capacities,” in view of
nondiscriminatory and expropriation clauses frequently contained
in these agreements; and (iii) the conflicts between land rights and
investments protected by FTAs are likely to become more frequent
due to multiple extractive industries, tourism, and agribusiness
projects often implemented in indigenous territories.


Tauli-Corpuz stressed that Indigenous Peoples are more vulnerable
to the adverse impacts of such investments, considering potential
losses to the cultural and noneconomic benefits derived from
these territories. The UN brief also recommended that future
studies need to (i) review the implications of international
investment and FTAs on Indigenous Peoples’ rights and (ii) explore
how investment agreements can be equally beneficial for
investors and Indigenous Peoples. Studies can also focus on how
investment agreements have provided new trade and investment
opportunities, if any, for indigenous communities.


Though not an FTA, the Indigenous Peoples Economic and
Trade Cooperation Arrangement (IPETCA), established in 2021,
encourages cooperation among participating economies [10] to boost
the ability of Indigenous Peoples and indigenous businesses to
benefit from international trade and investment opportunities.
According to the IPETCA overview, [11] the agreement acknowledges
the significance of valuing and preserving the practices and
knowledge of Indigenous Peoples that contribute to environmental
conservation, given how critical the environment is to their
cultural, social, and economic well-being. Notable activities under
IPETCA that could be explored and applied to future agreements
between or among economies with Indigenous Peoples include
(i) engaging with Indigenous Peoples to determine impediments to
their effective participation in trade, and strategies to address such
impediments; (ii) enabling trade relationships with, between, and
among Indigenous Peoples; and (iii) supporting businesses owned
by indigenous women in local, regional, and global value chains.


##### Fostering Inclusive Trade

At present, though, FTA provisions relevant to Indigenous Peoples
focus on intellectual property protections of TK/GR, not on
enhancing trade opportunities. Recent FTAs on the protection of
intellectual property likewise mention the protection of traditional
knowledge. The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO)
considers traditional knowledge as a “living body of knowledge” which
is an integral aspect of the spiritual and/or cultural identity of an
indigenous community, and that is maintained, grown, and passed on
between generations within the community. [12] Traditional knowledge
is viewed by Indigenous Peoples in a holistic manner, intertwined with
their “culture, identity, histories, spirituality, livelihoods, location, the
environment, land” and other natural conditions that surround them.
Traditional knowledge, accumulated from experience and improved
upon by succeeding generations, is also often shared orally from one
generation to another, underscoring the importance of language in
preserving such knowledge.


It should be noted that English language phrases denoting
traditional knowledge, e.g., “traditional ecological knowledge,”
may not fully reflect the way Indigenous Peoples refer to, or think
of, their knowledge and lifeways, [13] which are rooted in specific
places and depend on the indigenous community’s “ongoing,
active, and sovereign relationships with land, more-than-human
kin, and with each other.” [14]


As for genetic resources, this is considered by the Convention on
Biological Diversity as “biological materials with genetic information
of actual or potential value and that may be reproduced,” including
but not limited to medicinal plants, genetic sequences, etc. [15]

##### FREE TRADE AGREEMENTS AND PROVISIONS ON PROTECTION


Of the 208 FTAs signed from 1991 to 2024, 34 contain at least
one provision on the protection of TK/GR. These provisions first
appeared in the Taipei,China–Panama FTA signed in 2003, a
South–South initiative. However, the Global North has been the
key driver of this development, with 65% of FTAs having such
provisions involving advanced economies like Australia, Japan,
New Zealand, and European countries (Table 1 and Table 2).

Taipei,China’s bilateral FTAs with three Latin American
economies—Guatemala, Nicaragua, and Panama—each have
two binding commitments on equitable sharing of benefits. [16]


While advanced economies in Asia and the Pacific, such as
Australia, Japan, and New Zealand, constitute more than
one-third of the 34 FTAs with TK/GR, most of their obligations are
nonbinding. Australia has nonbinding commitments to disclose
the source of genetic resources for patent applications in its FTAs
with Peru and the United Kingdom (UK), and another nonbinding
commitment to disclose the source of traditional knowledge for
patent applications in its FTA with Peru. New Zealand has two
nonbinding commitments on genetic resources linked to equitable
benefit sharing and disclosure of sources.


Among these three economies, only Japan has a binding
commitment to the equitable sharing of benefits coming from use
of genetic resources in its FTA with the UK. Japan’s FTA with the
EU has one nonbinding commitment on prior consent for the use of
genetic resources. Thus, this shows that, ironically, the North–North
FTAs involving Australia, New Zealand, and Japan, and their EU
and UK counterparts, cover mostly nonbinding commitments
on the protection of TK/GR. This illustrates that while advanced
economies in Asia and the Pacific and Europe drive the rise in FTA
provisions on TK/GR, North–South and South–South provide
binding commitments.


Apart from intellectual property rights, protections for Indigenous
Peoples’ rights are also covered by some FTAs. The Comprehensive
and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP)
recognizes the rights of Indigenous Peoples in its preamble:
#### The Parties to this Agreement reaffirm the importance of promoting corporate social responsibility, cultural identity and diversity, environmental protection and conservation, gender equality, Indigenous rights, labor rights, inclusive trade, sustainable development, and traditional knowledge, as well as the importance of preserving their right to regulate in the public interest. [17]


##### Fostering Inclusive Trade

CPTPP member nations incorporated exceptions and
safeguards for their indigenous populations. For instance, an
exception clause for the Treaty of Waitangi was backed by
New Zealand. This clause allows for the Māori, New Zealand’s
Indigenous Peoples, to receive preferential treatment, and
ensures that the significant rights the Māori presently hold as per
the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi are preserved within the framework
of the CPTPP. [18]


Despite these efforts, the recent multilateral agreement on
TK/GR adopted by WIPO member states establishes a more
comprehensive framework for safeguarding these rights. The new
WIPO Treaty on Intellectual Property, Genetic Resources, and
Associated Traditional Knowledge, approved in May 2024, sets
a standard for bilateral or multilateral agreements by requiring
patent applicants to disclose the origin of genetic resources
and associated traditional knowledge used in their inventions.
Notably, in its text, the WIPO treaty _acknowledges_ the UN
Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples and affirms
the parties’ commitment to achieve the objectives of the said
declaration, and _affirms_ that parties should exert their “best
efforts” to involve Indigenous Peoples in treaty implementation. [19]
This treaty not only enhances the protection and recognition
of Indigenous Peoples’ and local communities’ contributions,
but also serves as a model for future FTA provisions. The WIPO
treaty demonstrates potential for multilateral agreements to offer
more encompassing and inclusive protections for TK/GR, going
beyond the commitments seen in North–South and South–South
FTAs, and the largely nonbinding commitments in North–North
FTAs. The next crucial step is for governments to ratify the treaty
and reflect these in national laws and trade agreements.

##### STRENGTHENING PROTECTION OF INDIGENOUS PEOPLES’ RIGHTS


Developing countries have incorporated provisions on traditional
knowledge in FTAs to address biopiracy, defined as the misuse
and/or misappropriation of TK/GR of Indigenous Peoples
(footnote 12). Key features of these provisions include the need
to disclose the use and sources of traditional knowledge used
for patents for inventions, as well as the need for cooperation
between parties and compliance with international obligations on
benefit sharing and prior informed consent.

As for the significance or potential role of FTAs in protecting
TK/GR, there are critiques that refer to how trade and investment
practices continue to marginalize Indigenous Peoples as economic
and political participants from local to international levels. [20]


The above critiques stem from how traditional knowledge
concerns are largely addressed in FTAs through intellectual
property provisions, which are described by some sources as a way
of privatizing traditional knowledge to “serve corporate economic
and development strategies,” as opposed to understanding this
knowledge as “a collective heritage of peoples and communities.” [21]
These same sources question how much FTAs recognize the
rights of Indigenous Peoples, given what they describe as the
focus of FTAs on “monopoly rights to produce, use, buy and
sell… innovations.”


FTA provisions for protecting traditional knowledge are seen
as not enough by themselves, and instead should be seen
as complementary to domestic frameworks and other
international commitments of involved parties (footnote 12).
The misappropriation and commodification of traditional
knowledge cannot be fully addressed through trade agreements,
and consultation with Indigenous Peoples is essential so that trade
agreement stipulations do not nullify their rights.


One practice that could be explored for replication is the work done
by WIPO’s Traditional Knowledge Division to put in place measures to
accredit representatives of Indigenous Peoples and local communities
to participate in negotiations on the WIPO Intergovernmental
Committee on Intellectual Property and Genetic Resources,
Traditional Knowledge and Folklore, to finalize “an agreement on an
international legal instrument(s) relating to intellectual property (IP)
which will ensure the balanced and effective protection of genetic
resources (GRs), TK and Traditional Cultural Expressions.” [22] In such
forums, representatives of Indigenous Peoples can advocate for
inclusion of provisions related to indigenous treaty rights.


There remains the question of whether FTAs, as instruments
to protect traditional knowledge, recognize the complexity of
indigenous ways of knowing, which consider human rights, the
environment, and the economy, as “indivisible, interrelated, and
interdependent” issues.


Other issues involve potential additional pressure to extract resources
from Indigenous lands and territories resulting from implementation
of trade agreements (footnote 8). There are also concerns that
FTAs may be utilized as tools by corporations or nation-states to
deny indigenous domestic rights in the case of perceived loss of
investment. An example cited is the North American Free Trade



Agreement or NAFTA Chapter 11 (footnote 20), which permits states
to be sued by international investors for investment losses.


There are, however, good practices, such as IPETCA (as discussed
earlier), although this is a nonbinding economic agreement. There is
also the CPTPP, which is the first trade agreement with any reference
to Indigenous Peoples’ rights (footnote 8). This agreement links
environmental preservation, protection, and Indigenous Peoples’
rights as critical issues and has the potential to connect these to
how parties express their public interests. A provision in the United
States–Mexico–Canada agreement also specifies that states
can implement any measure that is “not arbitrary or unjustifiably
discriminatory” to protect Indigenous Peoples’ rights (footnote 7).


Also notable is the FTA between the Philippines and the EFTA
(which counts as members Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway,
and Switzerland), which took effect 1 January 2020 for Iceland,
but much earlier (1 June 2018) for Liechtenstein, Norway,
and Switzerland. Article XI of this agreement focuses on measures
related to biological diversity and traditional knowledge. [23] Paragraph
2 from the said article makes a clear call to support the broader
application of traditional indigenous knowledge and innovation,
if the consent and participation of the knowledge holders are
ensured, and emphasizes equitable sharing of benefits. The article
also states that parties may require disclosure statements to secure
prior informed consent on the use of genetic resources.


International investment and trade must be evaluated based on
indigenous principles and methods as well as indigenous law,
and not solely on criteria developed by nation-states, financial
institutions, investors, and corporations (footnote 20). Indigenous
Peoples also have the right to implement their own modes of trade
and investment that reflect their priorities.


Often-cited as good practice in acknowledging the necessity
of compensating Indigenous Peoples for genetic resources
commercialization is the 2019 benefit-sharing agreement
that sees the San and Khoi peoples of South Africa receiving
a yearly percentage of the total value of unprocessed rooibos
production in the country, based on recognition of the San and
Khoi as the traditional knowledge holders of the rooibos plant. [24]

##### CONCLUSIONS


- Despite the potential shortfalls of existing FTAs to safeguard
Indigenous Peoples’ traditional knowledge, the protections
for TK/GR embedded in some of these agreements are an
important starting point.

- Potential areas for future research would explore (i) links between
regional cooperation and integration and indigenous economic
development in Asia and the Pacific, as well as (ii) links between
the implementation of FTA provisions on Indigenous Peoples and
the indigenous rights on biodiversity in indigenous territories.

- ADB can play a role in providing empirical information on the
impacts of FTAs with TK/GR provisions on Indigenous Peoples
and their communities through case studies that tackle positive
outcomes, or even the negative effects of misappropriations
and biopiracy. Studies on the implementation of FTAs that
require prior informed consent should explore whether the
omission of the word “free” had implications on the process
followed. Such studies could guide ADB members on future
initiatives, including legislation, to expand protections for
indigenous TK/GR.

- Potential intersections between the implementation of
ADB’s Environmental and Social Framework and its attached
Environmental and Social Standards, and strengthening regional
and global FTAs can be explored further.
