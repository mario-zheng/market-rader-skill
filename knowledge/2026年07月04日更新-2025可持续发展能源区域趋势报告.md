##### **FOREWORD**

The Asia-Pacific region stands at a significant point in its pursuit of a
sustainable and just energy future. Over the past decade, remarkable
strides have been made in advancing energy access, expanding
renewable energy and promoting energy efficiency. These achievements
reflect the strong commitment of governments and partners.


However, much more needs to be done to meet the targets of Sustainable Development
Goal 7 (SDG 7) by 2030. Electrification has reached near-universal levels in many parts of
the region, yet progress on clean cooking lags significantly behind. Despite its importance
for health, gender equity and environmental sustainability, the clean cooking sector remains
underprioritized and underfunded. Nearly one billion people in Asia and the Pacific still rely on
traditional fuels, exposing households — especially women and children — to dangerous levels
of indoor air pollution. Addressing this disparity must be a regional priority.


The region has emerged as a global leader in renewable electricity, more than tripling its
installed capacity over the last decade. But fossil fuels continue to dominate the region’s energy
mix, especially outside the power sector. Integrating renewables into heating and transport,
alongside scaling up energy efficiency, is essential to accelerate decarbonization.


Energy efficiency presents one of the most cost-effective pathways to decarbonization and
resilience. Yet progress remains uneven, hindered by regulatory, financial and information
barriers. Unlocking the full potential of energy efficiency will require systemic policy reforms
and strategic investments.


The energy transition must also be just and inclusive. Countries in the region are increasingly
embedding just transition principles in energy policy, addressing the needs of workers,
communities and vulnerable populations. Regional cooperation, supported by knowledgesharing, blended finance and modernized infrastructure, will be vital in accelerating progress.


The _2025 Regional Trends Report_ offers a comprehensive assessment of where the region
stands in its just energy transition and what must be done to ensure no one is left behind. It
provides recommendations to boost investment, strengthen institutions, empower vulnerable
groups and foster innovation.


Going forward, the region must prioritize coordinated action, regional cooperation and
systemic reform. ESCAP remains committed to supporting member States through technical
assistance, knowledge-sharing and policy dialogue to help deliver on the promise of SDG 7 and
a sustainable, just energy future for all.

##### **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

The _2025 Regional Trends Report on Energy for Sustainable Development: Transforming_
_Energy Systems for a Low Carbon Future in Asia and the Pacific_ offers an extensive analysis
of the region’s progress and challenges in moving towards a low carbon energy future. It
examines, in detail, the region’s progress in achieving Sustainable Development Goal 7
(SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy) and in advancing the just energy transition. Despite
significant progress in electricity access across nearly all countries in the region and with
many national examples of accelerated renewable energy deployment, challenges exist,
particularly in achieving universal access to clean cooking and improving energy efficiency.
A just, equitable and inclusive transition necessitates accelerated action by governments,
substantial investments and enhanced regional collaboration. To support the realization
of a low-carbon future, the recommendations of this report are aligned with the outcomes
of COP28, where countries committed to tripling renewable energy capacity and doubling
energy efficiency improvement rates by 2030. The Asia-Pacific region plays a critical role in
achieving these global targets, as it is home to over 4.3 billion people and consumes more
than half of the world’s energy.


**Electricity access: Reaching the last mile with quality and reliability**


The Asia-Pacific region has made significant progress in enhancing electricity access.
In 2023, the region’s electrification rate reached 98.6 per cent, leaving approximately 50
million people without access to electricity. In urban areas, access to electricity is almost
universal, while rural areas have attained 97.4 per cent access (figure I). However, behind
these statistics there exists a more nuanced situation; in many countries, households
connected to the grid experience load shedding, poor service quality or insufficient supply.
They may also face affordability challenges. This underscores a significant deficiency in
the SDG 7 indicator for energy access, which measures physical connection but not the
availability, reliability and affordability of electricity. These factors significantly influence
how energy access impacts socioeconomic development. Improving the multiple
dimensions of energy access must continue to be a priority for national governments,
even after universal access through physical electricity connection is achieved.


**Clean cooking: Addressing the neglected dimension of energy access**


Progress on clean cooking has lagged significantly behind electricity access. This
sector remains the least funded and most overlooked component of SDG 7, despite the
disproportionate impacts of exposure to polluting cooking fuels on health, gender equality
and household emissions. Although regional access has increased from 38 per cent
in 2000 to 78.9 per cent in 2023, approximately 1 billion people continue to depend on
traditional, polluting cooking fuels and technologies that lead to poor indoor air quality
and are associated with negative health impacts, particularly for women and children.
The disparity between urban and rural regions persists, with only 63.6 per cent of rural
residents relying on clean cooking fuels and technologies, in contrast to over 92 per
cent in urban settings, according to 2023 estimates (figure II). Exposure to household air

**Renewable energy: Scaling up amid structural headwinds**


The Asia-Pacific region is the global epicentre of renewable energy growth. Installed
renewable electricity capacity increased from 568 GW to 1,785 GW over the period 2013–
2023. The per capita growth is also significant, with renewable energy capacity increasing
from 151 watts to 451 watts per capita in the decade leading up to 2023 (figure III). The
deployment costs of solar photovoltaics (PV) and wind energy have significantly decreased,
prompting a swift expansion of renewables in many economies of the region.


Nonetheless, this expansion is accompanied by a structural paradox; despite these
substantial increments, the proportion of renewables in the total energy supply on a
regional level is increasing slowly, having reached just 16.3 per cent by 2022 (figure IV).
This is either stalling or dropping in many countries due to two factors. First, despite rapid
growth in overall energy consumption, notably in the industrial and transportation sectors,
means that renewable energy is struggling to increase its share of the growing total energy
consumption. Second, the decrease in use of traditional biomass in many developing
countries reduces the contribution of renewables to the overall energy mix. Adding to this
challenge is the fact that progress is uneven with renewable energy deployment proceeding
rapidly in some economies, while in others, renewable energy shares have stalled or are in
decline. Fossil fuels dominate regional energy composition, constituting about 85 per cent
of total supply, with coal continuing as a primary electricity source in several countries.
Outside the power sector, renewable energy uptake is limited, highlighting the need to
integrate renewables particularly in heating and transportation, through the electrification
of end uses and the direct use of renewable thermal energy. Despite capacity growth,
the share of modern renewables in total final energy consumption reached just 11.2 per
cent in 2022, highlighting the necessity to enhance adoption in heating, transportation

**Distributed renewables: Empowering households**


The growth of decentralized renewable energy is accelerating throughout the Asia-Pacific
region, especially through the increasing use of rooftop solar systems. However, many
countries face significant challenges in tracking and regulating these installations due to
gaps in data collection, logging and accessibility. A significant portion of installed capacity
is undocumented, complicating the fair evaluation of national renewable energy progress.
The cumulative unrecorded capacity across the region is likely to amount to several
gigawatts, posing challenges to effective planning and policy formulation. Concerns have
also been expressed over unregistered installations of solar water heaters and small-scale
photovoltaic systems, especially in instances where regulatory regulations are inadequate
or absent. While these developments reflect the increasing demand for distributed energy,
if left unregulated, they may jeopardize national energy planning, grid stability and energy
equity. Comprehensive and cohesive planning is necessary to guarantee the equitable
distribution of benefits from the renewable energy transition and to ensure that systems
are equipped for an influx of variable and decentralized sources. ESCAP’s support in
improving tracking methods and decentralized energy data governance is crucial for
ensuring system integration and equitable access.


**Energy efficiency: A slowdown in improvement**


Energy efficiency – widely recognized as a ‘first fuel’ of sustainable energy systems and
frequently characterized as a catalyst of the just energy transition – is one of the most
economically viable approaches for reducing emissions, strengthening energy security
and enhancing economic resilience. Energy efficiency is measured by calculating the
amount of energy used per unit of economic output (GDP), with lower values of energy
intensity indicating more efficient energy use. SDG 7 includes a target of doubling the
rate of energy intensity improvement. The Asia-Pacific region is one of the most energyintensive regions globally and utilizes roughly double the energy per unit of GDP produced
as compared to Europe. In 2022, the region’s average energy intensity was 4.4 megajoules
(MJ) per unit of GDP (US$ 2021 purchasing power parity (PPP)), in contrast to the global
average of 4.1 MJ per unit of GDP and Europe’s average of 2.3 MJ per unit of GDP. While
the region’s overall energy intensity has been consistently declining over the past decade,
this momentum is decelerating. From 2020 to 2022, the region demonstrated an average
annual improvement in energy intensity of merely 0.9 per cent, a slowdown from the
annual average improvement rate of 1.3 per cent recorded from 2015 to 2020 – and lower
still than the figure of 2.8 per cent that was achieved between 2010 to 2015. Setting the
region on track to meet the SDG 7 target will require improving energy efficiency across
economic sectors to achieve an average 4 per cent improvement of energy intensity per
annum between 2022 and 2030.


This inefficiency has extensive implications, leading to increased emissions, reduced
competitiveness and rising costs for energy. Nevertheless, there are encouraging
advancements. Many countries in the region are exhibiting leadership in industrial energy
management, digital retrofitting and sustainable manufacturing. However, progress
is inconsistent among countries and sectors. Buildings, industries and transportation
continue to present significant unexploited efficiency opportunities. Barriers, such as
limited financial access, high upfront technological costs, insufficient enforcement of

**Just and inclusive transition: Ensuring that no one is left behind**


The energy transition must be just and inclusive. Many countries in the region are pursuing
innovative approaches to incorporate just transition principles, for example, addressing
the needs of workers and communities affected by declining fossil fuel industries through
reskilling programmes and investing in new clean energy infrastructure to offset these
impacts. However, new challenges are emerging. Some countries have cautioned about
increasing disparities in solar adoption, as affluent households are more capable of
investing in off-grid alternatives, while low-income areas continue to rely on inadequate
or unreliable public supply. These examples highlight the importance of considering the
needs of vulnerable groups and communities when formulating energy transition strategies
to ensure they are just.


**Technology choices and financing strategies: Expanding the transition toolbox**


Regional consultations, undertaken by ESCAP, underscored technology neutrality and
economic diversity as essential themes emphasizing the incorporation of a wider array of
low-carbon technologies, including natural gas, nuclear energy, blue hydrogen, and carbon
capture and storage, into just energy transition considerations. Countries are progressively
promoting a feasible combination of technologies suited to their specific conditions,

Furthermore, the increasing focus on green finance needs to be accompanied by
government policy support for transitional finance mechanisms, such as transition bonds
and public-private partnerships, which can finance grid modernization, clean cooking and

**Investment and cooperation: Mobilizing capital and regional cooperation**


The transformation of the region’s energy system will depend on mobilizing significantly
greater public and private investments. In 2024, the Asia-Pacific region garnered over
US$ 528 billion in renewable energy investment, accounting for more than 60 per cent
of the global total. However, these investments are predominantly concentrated in a
limited number of large economies, while many developing countries face challenges
in attracting adequate funding due to elevated perceived project development risks and
constrained institutional capacity. Between 2020 and 2023, the ten least developed
countries in the Asia-Pacific region received just 1.4 per cent of total energy transition
investment (ESCAP, 2025c).


Blended finance mechanisms, concessional loans and technical assistance need to be
augmented to bridge the investment gaps.


Regional cooperation can play an essential role in accelerating the just energy transition.
More advanced economies can expand partnerships with emerging market economies
and lower-income countries to scale energy transitions financing. However, Governments
must work to boost investment confidence with clear and coherent targets and policies
for transitioning the energy sector while providing transparent guidelines for financing
and investment.


**Overcoming structural barriers**


Achieving the region’s sustainable energy goals necessitates addressing persistent structural
challenges. Fragmented regulations, antiquated and inefficient permitting processes and
inadequate energy data systems persist in impeding renewable energy adoption and hindering
effective planning. Enhancing institutional capacity, streamlining processes, elevating data
quality and promoting digital solutions are crucial for facilitating investment, enhancing
policy responsiveness, and ensuring an inclusive and resilient just energy transition.


**Accelerating progress through shared vision and action**


While the Asia-Pacific region has made considerable advancements in several aspects
of SDG 7, the pace and distribution of these achievements are uneven across the region
and insufficient for meeting all SDG 7 targets by 2030. Despite rapid progress in electricity
access, the region is off-track to meet SDG 7.1.2 on clean cooking and SDG 7.3 on energy
efficiency by 2030, with many countries encountering structural, financial and institutional
barriers to the just energy transition. A just and sustainable energy future requires more
ambitious and cohesive solutions that simultaneously address accessibility to energy,
affordability, reliability and environmental sustainability.

To advance SDG 7 and accelerate the just energy transition, this report offers the following
interlinked recommendations for countries in the Asia-Pacific region:


**1. Create an enabling environment for investments in renewables and energy efficiency**


Boost investments by setting clear and long-term national policy targets, develop
financial incentives, such as tax incentives, grants or concessional loans, to catalyse
market development; de-risk private investment; and reduce market and administrative
barriers. Mobilize climate finance and encourage public-private partnerships to build a
pipeline of clean energy projects. Countries at all stages of development can improve
the enabling environment, but these gaps are more prevalent in developing countries
and countries in special situations.


**2. Strengthen integrated energy planning for a low-carbon future**


Institutionalize integrated energy planning frameworks that align national energy
strategies with climate commitments. Frameworks should be data-driven, crosssectoral and grounded in long-term modelling, and must incorporate resilience
considerations. While many advanced economies have established comprehensive
energy planning frameworks, least developed countries, landlocked developing
countries and the Pacific small island developing States are most in need of enhanced
energy planning.


**3. Promote energy efficiency and end-use electrification in high-impact sectors**


Set ambitious standards and targets, offer financial incentives and mandate energy
performance in buildings, appliances and industries. Strengthen regulatory and
enforcement frameworks, support innovation and enable access to finance. Given the
observed slowdown in energy efficiency across all subregions, this recommendation
applies broadly across countries of the region.


**4. Modernize infrastructure for resilience and flexibility**


Invest in smart grids, digital energy systems and battery storage, upgrading
transmission and distribution systems to handle variable renewable energy. Provide
decentralized energy supplies in off-grid and vulnerable areas. Developing member
States, particularly those embarking on renewable energy expansion in South and
South-East Asia stand most to benefit from modernizing their infrastructure. Countries
in the Pacific subregion and other small island developing States in the region, such as
Maldives and Timor-Leste, can benefit particularly from modernizing infrastructure to
support off-grid provision of electricity.


**5. Foster an inclusive and gender-responsive energy sector**


Achieving a just and equitable energy transition requires deliberate efforts to foster
inclusion and address systemic inequalities in the energy sector. Women, youth and
marginalized groups are often disproportionately affected by energy poverty and
underrepresented in decision-making. Enhanced efforts need to be made to ensure
their perspectives inform policies and strategies, while targeted measures – such as
skills training, mentorship and leadership opportunities – are needed to help remove
systemic barriers and empower them to play pivotal implementation roles for the just

energy transition. As gender gaps are noted across the region, it is imperative that all
countries pay attention to reversing gender imbalances.


**6. Strengthen regional collaboration and policy coherence**


Promote knowledge exchange, harmonize regulatory frameworks, align NDCs with
national energy strategies, and foster cross-border electricity trade and technical
cooperation. It is essential that more advanced economies of the region form SouthSouth and triangular cooperation partnerships with developing countries to foster
exchanges of knowledge, build capacities and highlight best practices.


**7. Enhance energy sector planning and just transitions**


Effective, inclusive and forward-thinking energy sector planning is crucial to attaining
sustainable development targets and fulfilling net-zero emissions targets. Robust

###### **1.1 Purpose of this report**

The _2025 Regional Trends Report on Energy for Sustainable Development_ provides a
comprehensive assessment of the progress of the Asia-Pacific region toward Sustainable

and modern energy. As energy-related greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are the principal
driver of climate change, the report also examines the region’s progress in meeting global
climate commitments, including the Paris Agreement and COP28. It aims to identify
key barriers to accelerating the just energy transition and looks to offer evidence-based

###### **1.2 Global energy and climate context**


The global energy landscape is undergoing rapid transformation, driven by the urgent need
to mitigate and adapt to climate change, ensure energy security and foster sustainable
economic development. Increasing climate commitments, evolving policy frameworks,
technological advancements and falling renewable energy prices, are driving the transition
towards cleaner, more efficient energy systems. Key international agreements, such as
the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, the Paris Agreement and COP28 pledges
set ambitious targets for countries to cut emissions, achieve universal access to modern
energy, enhance energy efficiency and increase the share of renewable energy. Specifically,
SDG 7: Affordable and Clean Energy provides an agreed set of sustainable energy targets
that align the energy trajectories of countries with climate goals and promote inclusive
development (United Nations, n.d.a.).​


_**Renewable energy expansion**_


Globally, the expansion of renewable energy has progressed rapidly. The costs of
renewable energy technologies have also significantly declined, making clean energy
more competitive than fossil fuels. Since 2010, the cost of solar photovoltaics (PV) has
decreased by over 80 per cent, while wind energy costs have dropped by 55 per cent,
enabling mass deployment across a large number of energy markets (IRENA, 2024b).


_**Electrification and electric vehicles**_


The electrification of end-use sectors has also gained momentum, contributing to a more
integrated and low-carbon energy system. For instance, the global electric vehicle (EV)
market reached record-high sales in 2024, surpassing 17 million vehicles, representing
a 25 per cent increase from 2023 – driven by policy incentives, advancements in battery
technologies and growing consumer demand (IEA, 2025).

_**Advancing and emerging technologies**_


In addition to renewable energy and electrification, a renewed interest in nuclear energy and
the exploration of emerging technologies may support decarbonization in electricity, heat
and other end-use sectors. Nuclear energy, though long established, remains a modest
share of low-carbon power generation for the region. Innovative reactor designs, including
small modular reactors (SMRs), are garnering interest for their prospective contribution
to the decarbonization of electricity. Green hydrogen is being investigated as a potential
solution for hard-to-abate heavy industries, shipping and long-haul transportation, as
well as for long-term, large scale energy storage, with countries like Australia and Japan
investing in large-scale hydrogen production projects (IEA, 2021c). Carbon capture and
storage (CCS) is being tested to mitigate emissions from industrial facilities and fossil fuel
power stations, although issues persist regarding cost and scalability.


Additionally, there is a growing emphasis on digital innovation, smart grid infrastructure,
breakthroughs in battery storage and demand-side technologies that enhance energy
efficiency and system flexibility. Emerging innovations, like AI-driven energy management
systems, advanced lithium-ion, sodium-ion, redox flow and solid-state batteries, and
adaptable building and industrial load controllers, are poised to advance the evolution
of low carbon energy systems. These technologies facilitate the integration of variable
renewables and promote more responsive, decentralized and consumer-oriented energy
systems, which can support SDG 7 targets and climate objectives.


_**Global climate commitments**_


The Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario (NZE Scenario) developed by the International
Energy Agency (IEA) delineates a strategy for the global energy industry to attain net-zero GHG
emissions by the mid-21st century (IEA, 2024d). It also expects that wealthy countries will
reach this goal before developing countries. Aiming to restrict the global temperature increase
to below 2°C – and ideally 1.5°C – with at least a 50 per cent probability, the Paris Agreement
remains the cornerstone of world climate ambition (IEA, 2024h). The COP28 outcomes further
strengthened these targets, with world leaders pledging to triple global renewable energy
capacity by 2030, double energy efficiency gains and phase down fossil fuel dependency
while ensuring energy security. A significant aspect of COP28 was the enhancement of climate
finance, with wealthier countries committing to provide financial assistance to less developed
nations to expedite their just energy transitions (United Nations Climate Change, 2024).


Countries are periodically updating their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) to
align with higher climate ambitions. Over 80 per cent of global emissions are now covered
by net-zero commitments, reflecting the growing urgency for climate action. Additionally,
carbon pricing mechanisms are gaining momentum, with over 60 carbon pricing initiatives
being implemented worldwide. Notable examples include the Emissions Trading System in
China, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism in the European Union and the emerging
carbon market in India, together with other mechanisms such as renewable energy
certificates (IEA, 2024h). These mechanisms aim to create market-driven incentives for
industries, as well as sectors such as buildings, aviation, maritime and road transport,
to reduce their carbon footprints. The third round of NDCs, known as NDC 3.0, are due
in 2025, and are intended to move beyond pledges to articulate concrete strategies for
national emissions reductions and adaptation.

_**Expansion of renewable energy**_


The Asia-Pacific region has significant potential for expanding the share of renewables.
Technological improvements, including cost and performance enhancements in solar
PV, battery storage and digital energy management systems, are lowering costs and

having installed 37 GW of energy storage capacity in 2024, supporting both renewable
energy integration and grid stability. India is also positioning itself as a major player,
targeting 34 GW of battery storage by 2030 to bolster variable renewable energy uptake

storage is becoming a vital enabler of the region’s clean energy transition.

SDG 7 offers an overarching framework to track the region’s sustainable energy development.
It comprises three main targets: universal access to electricity and clean cooking solutions
(SDG 7.1), a substantial increase in the share of renewable energy in the global energy mix
(SDG 7.2), and a doubling of the global rate of improvement in energy efficiency (SDG 7.3).
These targets are closely aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement of limiting global
warming to well below 2°C, and ideally to 1.5°C, above pre-industrial levels. Building further
on the SDG 7 commitments, at COP28, countries increased sustainable energy ambitions
by committing to triple global renewable energy capacity and double energy efficiency
improvements by 2030 (United Nations Climate Change, 2024).


Progress across the SDG 7 indicators is mixed. At the regional level, some indicators
demonstrate notable gains, while others show little progress. At the same time, differing
national capacities, policy priorities and levels of investment in sustainable energy
transitions has led to notable disparities across countries.


Regionally, universal electricity access (SDG 7.1.1) has nearly been achieved, although
hurdles remain in terms of last mile access and ensuring the quality and reliability of its
supply. Reliance on clean cooking fuels and technologies (SDG 7.1.2) has shown steady
gains, but the rate of access still lags far behind the electrification rate. Electrification and
clean cooking gains have been encumbered by the continuing impacts of the global energy
crisis. Government budgets and households, especially in low-income and vulnerable
countries, have faced rising energy costs, triggered by geopolitical tensions, supply chain
disruptions and surging fossil fuel prices.


While the renewable share of electricity is steadily increasing, the share of renewables
in the region’s total energy supply (SDG 7.2.1) is rising only incrementally as the supply
of fossil fuel continues to expand. Uneven progress toward renewables is particularly
pronounced across economies, in terms of overall renewable energy shares, per capita
electricity capacity and penetration into various sectors.


Challenges widely persist in energy efficiency, measured in terms of energy intensity
(SDG 7.3.1). The global target aims to double the rate of energy intensity improvement,
thereby lowering the amount of energy used to produce a unit of GDP. Regionally, the rate
of improvement has slowed, demonstrating that regional economies are increasingly
struggling to decouple energy use from economic growth.

Beyond the SDG 7 indicator, it is important to also consider other factors that define the

access that can fully support socioeconomic development. For example, in Pakistan,
despite achieving a national electrification rate of more than 93 per cent, many households,
especially in rural regions, endure extended load shedding, frequently lasting 12 to 13 hours
each day. Other countries of the region also struggle with similar reliability challenges. This
underscores the disparity between grid connectivity and the actual provision of electricity
services, and points to the need to consider aspects that are considered under the multitiered framework put forward by ESMAP, including capacity, reliability and affordability
(ESMAP, 2025).

The journey towards reaching universal electricity access requires countries to overcome

energy poverty in underserved areas. ESCAP identifies that in rural regions, energy access

systems have demonstrated how decentralized solutions can not only expand access but
also reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels.

with the ADB and IRENA has helped Nepal deploy community-based solar and microhydro systems, benefitting thousands of rural households (IRENA, 2023b). In Afghanistan,

electricity via decentralized solar power systems to over 1 million people (IFC, 2019).

###### **2.2 Progress in achieving access to clean cooking** **fuels and technologies**


While electricity access has improved substantially, the transition to clean cooking
solutions has been much slower. Regionally, the proportion of the population with primary
reliance on lean fuels and technologies reached just 78.9 per cent in 2023 (figure 15). This
translates to more than one in five people who still lack access, with serious impacts on
health, the environment and socioeconomic development. Many countries are struggling
to make progress toward the universal access target. According to the latest estimates,
approximately 1 billion people still rely on unhealthy and polluting cooking options
such as biomass, charcoal and kerosene, leading to severe health and environmental
consequences.


Traditional cooking fuels, such as wood, coal and crop residues, pose severe health risks,
particularly for women and children who are disproportionately exposed to household air
pollution from the use of inefficient stoves and open fires. Exposure to household smoke
is a leading cause of premature deaths, especially among women and children. The need
to collect firewood limits time availability for education, socialization and employment,
particularly for girls and women, while also straining natural resources. Addressing
clean cooking access is therefore integral to promoting gender equality and empowering
women.​ Polluting fuels used for cooking, heating and lighting also pose serious safety
hazards. Kerosene ingestion is a leading cause of childhood poisoning and household
energy use is linked to severe burns and injuries in low- and middle-income countries.
Moreover, emissions from inefficient stoves, especially black carbon and methane, are
major short-lived climate pollutants.


_**Urban-rural disparity in clean cooking access**_


Access to clean cooking solutions remains deeply unequal between urban and rural areas,
particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Despite efforts to expand access, a stark
urban-rural divide persists, underscoring broader issues of energy poverty, equity and
development. While 92.9 per cent of urban dwellers were recorded as enjoying access
to clean cooking options in 2023, in rural areas it was only 63.6 per cent (figure 15).


Recent research highlights that, in the Asia-Pacific region, rural households face systemic
barriers such as lack of financing for clean cookstove adoption, limited market penetration
of modern fuels and sociocultural preferences for traditional cooking (IEA, 2023b). This
points to the need for integrated clean cooking strategies within rural development plans.
IRENA stresses the importance of leveraging renewable energy-based clean cooking
solutions, such as biogas and solar-electric cookstoves, for rural households, noting

_**Barriers and policy measurers**_


The adoption of clean cooking technologies has been slow due to high costs, lack of awareness
and the limited availability of clean cooking fuels, particularly in remote regions. However, as the
role of clean cooking in achieving SDG 7 is increasingly being recognized, efforts such as the
expansion of LPG networks and the promotion of electric cooking are growing.


Limited prioritization of clean cooking in policymaking spheres has been a major hinderance
to progress. Numerous countries with significant deficits have yet to establish clean cooking
targets, reflecting a lack of political commitment and institutional frameworks necessary for
scaling uptake. Integrating the SDG 7 clean cooking target into national energy policies is
essential to avoid escalating social costs and to contribute to multiple SDGs, including those
related to health, poverty, gender equality, air pollution and climate change.


Another primary impediment to universal access to clean cooking is the need for substantial
financial investment. Global investment levels currently fall short, amounting to less than
3 per cent of the estimated $4.5 billion needed annually to achieve universal access by
2030. The high upfront costs of clean cooking technologies and fuels pose significant
economic barriers, particularly for low-income households that represent the bulk of the
deficit. The energy crisis and inflationary pressures following the pandemic have further
reduced household incomes and increased the prices of clean cooking fuels, prompting
many to revert to traditional, harmful cooking methods.


At the same time, the deployment of clean cooking technologies is often constrained by
inadequate infrastructure, especially in rural and remote areas. The lack of supply chains,
distribution networks and maintenance services further hampers the adoption of clean
cooking solutions.​ Traditional cooking practices are deeply rooted in cultural norms and
social behaviours in many countries in the Asia-Pacific region. Furthermore, Governments
and utilities have yet to position electric cooking within national electrification initiatives and
power planning, missing a key opportunity to improve access. Transitioning to clean cooking
methods requires not only technological adoption but also behavioural change, which can
be challenging without targeted awareness campaigns and community engagement.​


Overcoming these barriers requires a multifaceted approach involving increased investment,
supportive policies, technological innovation, infrastructure development, cultural
sensitivity and targeted health interventions. Collaborative efforts among governments,
international organizations, private sector stakeholders and local communities are
essential to accelerate progress toward universal access to clean cooking by 2030.

**Renewable energy deployment across sectors**


_Power sector_


The power sector has been the key focus for renewable energy development in the AsiaPacific region, with renewables in electricity production accounting for roughly 30 per cent
of total electricity consumption. This strong growth is attributable to factors such as better
defined economic incentives, well-established regulatory frameworks and enhanced
technical sophistication of renewable power technologies relative to renewable energy for
heat and transport sectors (IEA, 2024h).


Within the power sector, solar PV and wind power have predominated recent capacity
expansions. The capacity of solar PV systems has surged, propelled by substantial cost
reductions of above 80 per cent since 2010, and bolstered by comprehensive governmental
incentives and regulatory structures. Wind energy has also benefited from decreasing
prices and technical progress, leading to significant scale up (IRENA, 2024a).

Renewable energy use in heat generation in the region remains undeveloped when
compared to electricity generation, with renewables accounting for just around 11 per

heating buildings, despite the region’s enormous potential for solar thermal, geothermal
and bioenergy-based heat.

power and heat applications. To capitalize on its tremendous potential – estimated
at approximately 29 GW – the Government of Indonesia has set a goal of 7.2 GW of

the Philippines, geothermal energy is critical in supplying both power and heat demands.

more than 10 per cent of total power generation and provided industrial heat for food
drying, aquaculture and district heating (IRENA, 2022a). The Department of Energy of the
Philippines has proposed incentives to encourage more investment in geothermal fields,
with a goal of increasing total capacity by 2030 (ESCAP, 2024a). China has emerged as
a global leader in heat pump manufacturing and is increasingly implementing policies to

###### **2.4 Progress in improving energy efficiency in Asia** **and the Pacific**

Energy efficiency serves as a cornerstone of the just energy transition, offering one of
the most cost-effective and immediate means to lower final energy consumption, reduce
GHG emissions, improve energy security and support sustainable economic growth. As
the region experiences rapid urbanization and rising energy demand, accelerating energy
efficiency across buildings, industry and transport is essential to decoupling economic
development from energy use and emissions. Strengthening energy efficiency measures
also reduces the need for new energy infrastructure, lowers energy costs for consumers
and enhances resilience to energy price volatility.


SDG Target 7.3 aims to double the annual improvement rate of energy efficiency. [4] This section
assesses the region’s progress, looks at trends and best practices, and highlights important
gaps using the latest data and analysis. Energy efficiency is an indispensable component
of the just energy transition, providing a cost-effective means to cut GHG emissions, relieve
energy poverty and improve energy security with existing technologies. Making more efficient
use of energy through better technologies, systematic change and consumer choices drives
not only decarbonization, but also saves costs and increases energy security. Furthermore,
by reducing overall demand, it boosts the share of renewable energy.


Many of the technologies for advanced energy efficiency in buildings, industry, appliances
and vehicles are readily available, however the full application and energy saving potential
of these are yet to be realized across most countries of the region. There are many
factors that explain this phenomenon, including high upfront costs, information gaps, split
incentives and cognitive factors. The region continues to have high energy intensity levels
compared to other global regions, such as Europe, Africa and Latin America. This points
to a need for more focus on energy efficiency in regulatory and policy settings. Annex II
provides further detail on the factors behind the region’s high energy intensity.


Annual changes in primary energy intensity across the Asia-Pacific region from 1990 to
2022 present a picture of slowing progress on energy intensity (figure 22). From 1990 to
2010, the improvement rate was a modest 1.4 per cent on average per year. Subsequently,
significant progress was observed between 2010–2015, when the region recorded an
impressive 2.8 per cent average annual improvement. This coincided with widespread
policy initiatives in China, India and other major economies, including large-scale industrial
modernization, energy performance standards and the introduction of demand-side
management programmes. For example, in China, the reduction of energy intensity also
reflects the structural change of the economy, which is moving from the industrial to the
service sector. The share of GDP produced by the industrial sector reduced from 46.7 per
cent in 2010 to 40.8 per cent by 2015. Over the same period the service sector grew from
43.2 per cent of GDP to 50.8 per cent. By 2024 the industrial sector lost further ground to
the service sector, reaching 38.3 per cent and 54.6 per cent of GDP respectively.


However, momentum has weakened in recent years. From 2015–2020, the average improvement
rate fell to 1.3 per cent, and then to 0.9 per cent during 2020–2022. To make up for this

**Box 1: Energy transition in Georgia: Best practices supporting SDG 7**


Georgia is committed to advancing SDG 7, which aims to ensure universal access to affordable,
reliable and modern energy services, increase the share of renewable energy and improve energy
efficiency by 2030. In 2020, Georgia adopted the _Energy Transition Pathways for the 2030 Agenda –_
_SDG 7 Road map_, aligning its national energy policies with SDG 7 targets. The road map prioritizes
the development of hydropower as the backbone of the country’s renewable energy sector while
promoting diversification through solar and wind energy projects. It also emphasizes energy
efficiency improvements across residential, commercial and industrial sectors, and supports
sustainable consumption and production patterns.

From the outset of implementing the road map, Georgia has increased renewable energy capacity
and achieved measurable improvements in energy efficiency, leading to reduced GHG emissions
and strengthened energy security. These efforts also contribute to its commitments under the
Paris Agreement, demonstrating progress toward national climate targets. The road map further
advocates for cleaner use of fossil fuels to ensure a balanced and sustainable energy transition.

Key lessons include the critical role of integrated policy frameworks, multi-stakeholder engagement
and targeted investments in overcoming infrastructure and financing challenges. This holistic
approach provides a practical and replicable model for sustainable energy transition in the region,
supporting both national development and global climate goals under the 2030 Agenda.


**Opportunities for advancing energy efficiency**


Many economies in the Asia-Pacific region have made notable progress in enhancing
energy efficiency through policies, regulations and incentive programmes. Ensuring the
efficiency of appliances is one key area. Many countries globally now have mandatory
energy performance standards that mandate minimum energy efficiency levels. These
apply to over one hundred products categories used in homes, businesses and industries,
such as air conditioners, refrigerators, lights and televisions. However, in countries that
recently launched these programmes, energy savings remain low as replacing old and
inefficient equipment takes time.


India and China have expanded their programmes for energy-efficient appliances, such
as air conditioners, refrigerators and lighting, under initiatives like the Super-Efficient
Equipment Program, Bureau of Energy Efficiency’s star labelling scheme, and the Energy
Label system in China. In the Republic of Korea, the e-Standby Program promotes low
standby power consumption, while the Top Runner Program in Japan sets forwardlooking benchmarks for appliances based on the best available technologies. Australia
and New Zealand jointly operate the Greenhouse and Energy Minimum Standards Act,
harmonizing appliance efficiency regulation and labelling across their markets. China,

_**3.1.1 Electrification and fuel-switching: A key approach to decarbonizing end**_

Electrification and fuel-switching is critical for decarbonizing end-uses such as transport,
heating and industrial processes. By shifting from carbon-intense, fossil fuel-powered
systems to electric and lower-carbon alternatives, emissions from various sectors can

decarbonized through renewable energy sources. Estimates suggest that the bulk share
of energy demand could be electrified. Already electricity has the fastest growing share
of energy use in the Asia-Pacific region, now approaching one-quarter of total final
consumption.


The electrification of transport is one of the most effective strategies for reducing
emissions across economies. EVs, alongside improvements in vehicle fuel efficiency and
greater adoption of public transport networks, can drastically reduce emissions in the
transport sector. The International Transport Forum (ITF) projects that electric vehicles
could account for a 60 per cent reduction in transport emissions by 2050, which would be
a crucial contribution toward meeting the net-zero emissions target (IEA, 2024c).

_**3.1.2 Technological advancements for the power sector**_


Technological innovation is playing a pivotal role in reshaping the power sector amid
growing demands for decarbonization, reliability and access. Advances in renewable
energy technologies, digital tools, smart grids and energy storage are enabling more flexible,
efficient and resilient power systems. These developments are particularly important as
countries seek to accelerate the energy transition, manage rising electricity demand and
integrate higher shares of variable renewable energy.


**Distributed renewable energy sources and smart grids**


The transition to variable renewable energy sources requires fundamental regulatory
reforms to electricity markets and grid operations. Traditionally, power grids were designed
for centralized generation and passive consumption. In a clean energy future, grids must
be dynamic, decentralized and digitally managed.


Grid modernization includes advanced metering infrastructure, demand response
mechanisms, energy storage integration and smart distribution networks allowing
distributed energy resources (DERs) such as rooftop solar and home batteries to interact
with the grid and even actively participate in energy trading. Virtual power plants (VPPs)
will become increasingly important with DER integration. VPPs are a network of distributed
energy resources, such as rooftop solar PV, batteries and smart appliances, that are
aggregated and centrally controlled. This collective system functions like a single large
power plant, providing services to the grid by either generating and supplying power or
reducing demand to help balance electricity supply and demand. Dynamic pricing, such
as time-of-use tariffs and real-time markets, can be introduced to reflect the true value of
electricity and incentivize load shifting to periods of high renewable generation.


Smart grids, demand response systems, digital energy platforms and AI-based forecasting
tools are enabling more flexible, resilient and consumer-centric energy systems. Already,
countries are beginning to integrate smart grid considerations into policy and planning.
For instance, “Singapore’s Future Grid Capabilities Road map” demonstrates how digital
technologies can enhance grid management, integrate distributed energy resources and
empower consumers to actively participate in energy markets (EMA Singapore, 2025).


**Battery storage: Ensuring energy access, grid stability and reliability**


Energy storage technologies, particularly battery storage systems, are crucial for integrating
higher shares of renewable energy into the grid and ensuring energy security. By storing
excess energy produced during peak renewable generation periods and discharging it
when demand is high, storage systems can help decrease renewable energy curtailment,
maintain grid stability, support a reliable energy supply and defer the requirements for
network investment. In remote and off-grid regions, solar home systems paired with
batteries offer reliable, clean energy solutions. In countries like Bangladesh and Myanmar,

_**3.1.4 Industrial modernization and energy efficiency**_


The industrial sector, while driving economic growth in the Asia-Pacific region, has also
been the largest contributor to rising GHG emissions, pollution and resource depletion.
Industrial sectors such as steel and cement consume significant quantities of energy
as well as produce GHG emissions from energy use and direct emissions. As the region
confronts the imperative of decarbonization, industries face increasing pressure to adopt
technologies and practices that reduce their carbon footprint while sustaining growth and
competitiveness. Industries that invest in energy-efficient technologies stand to benefit
from lower operational costs and reduced exposure to energy price fluctuations. According
to the IEA, Asia-Pacific industries could reduce their energy consumption by over 15 per
cent by 2030 by improving energy efficiency (IEA, 2021a).


For the just energy transition to reach its full potential, transformation is required on both
the energy supply and demand sides. This type of ‘systems’ approach should include
energy-intensive industries and hard-to-abate sectors. Key energy efficiency measures in
industrial processes across the Asia-Pacific region include the use of variable speed drives
in sectors such as textiles and cement; waste heat recovery in heavy industries such as
steel; and the electrification of thermal processes using induction heaters to replace fossil
fuels. Variable speed drives on motors and pumps adjust power use based on real-time
demand, thereby achieving efficiency gains ranging from 20 to 60 per cent depending on
the application (IEA, 2021a). Permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSM) offer
great energy saving potentials and can be widely used in pumps, fans, ball mills
and conveyors. Waste heat recovery systems, including technologies such as heat
exchangers, organic Rankine cycle systems and regenerative burners, are increasingly
being implemented in energy-intensive industries.


Countries such as Thailand and Malaysia are witnessing the growing use of energy
management systems (ISO 50001), while SMEs benefit from energy efficiency financing
schemes that support upgrades to high-efficiency boilers, compressors and motors
(ESCAP, 2021). In Japan and the Republic of Korea, the deployment of AI-powered
energy monitoring systems and sensor technologies enables real-time analytics and
predictive maintenance, enhancing process reliability. In compressed air systems,
optimization measures can deliver 20 to 30 per cent energy savings.

_**3.2.2 Interventions for market transformation**_


Comprehensive market reforms through instruments such as carbon pricing, subsidy
reforms and incentives for clean energy investments are crucial to drive the transition
towards sustainable energy systems and to achieve climate commitments in the AsiaPacific region and beyond. Well-designed policy interventions can reshape market
behaviour, correct market failures associated with environmental externalities, and
provide clear and predictable investment signals that foster low-carbon innovations and
sustainable energy transitions.


**Carbon markets to drive clean energy investments**


Carbon pricing is a fundamental policy instrument to internalize the environmental costs
of GHG emissions. It provides economic incentives for emitters to reduce their carbon
footprint and fosters investment and innovation in cleaner technologies. There are two

primary forms of carbon pricing: emissions trading systems (ETS) and carbon taxes. In Asia
and the Pacific, several countries have successfully introduced carbon pricing mechanisms.
Singapore implemented a carbon tax in 2019, making it the first South-East Asian country
to do so. The initial rate was set at SGD 5 per ton of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e),
with plans to progressively increase it to between SGD 50 and SGD 80 by 2030 (World
Bank, 2023). The Republic of Korea has developed one of the most comprehensive ETS,
launched in 2015, covering about 70 per cent of the nation’s emissions and creating robust
carbon markets. Since 2011, the carbon market in China has progressed from regional
pilot initiatives to the establishment of a national emissions trading system starting in
July 2021. While the China ETS initially targeted the power generation sector, it has since
expanded to include industries such as steel, cement and other high-emission sectors.


The benefits of carbon pricing extend beyond emission reductions. Revenue generated
through carbon pricing can be reinvested into climate mitigation and adaptation projects,
just energy transition financing or redistributed to households to alleviate transition costs.
Successful examples globally have demonstrated that well-designed carbon pricing
schemes do not necessarily harm economic growth but can stimulate green sectors and
employment (IEA, 2024d). However, to maximize impact, carbon prices must be aligned
with the goals of the Paris Agreement. The High-Level Commission on Carbon Pricing
estimates that carbon prices need to range between $50 and $100 per tCO₂e by 2030 to be
consistent with net-zero emission pathways (World Bank, 2023).


**Reallocation of fossil fuel subsidies**


Fossil fuel subsidies, estimated at $7 trillion annually on a global basis, according to
the International Monetary Fund, distort market prices, encourage inefficient energy
consumption, and crowd out investments in renewables and energy efficiency. In the AsiaPacific region, several countries, including Indonesia, India and Malaysia have undertaken
substantial fossil fuel subsidy reforms in the past decade (ESCAP, 2024a). Indonesia
removed gasoline subsidies in 2015, freeing up fiscal space for infrastructure investments
and social protection programmes. India significantly reduced diesel subsidies and
leveraged savings to support renewable energy projects and expand rural electrification.
Such reforms not only improve market efficiency but also provide critical financial
resources to invest in sustainable energy initiatives. Subsidy reform is politically sensitive
and requires careful planning, public communication and social protection measures to
mitigate potential adverse effects on low-income households. Implementing targeted
cash transfers, energy vouchers or investing in energy efficiency programmes can ensure
that vulnerable populations are shielded during the transition.


**Financial and market incentives**


Providing strategic financial incentives is another vital policy lever to accelerate clean
energy deployment. Instruments such as feed-in tariffs (FiTs), tax credits, preferential
loans, grants and risk guarantees can lower the cost of capital, de-risk investments, and
stimulate private sector engagement in renewable energy and energy efficiency.

_**3.2.3 Market reforms and financial mechanisms**_


Market reforms and innovative financial mechanisms are central to accelerating the

emissions targets, requires dynamic and inclusive market structures that stimulate
competition, efficiency and innovation, alongside robust financial ecosystems that unlock
capital flows into sustainable energy investments.


In recent years, transformative shifts have occurred in how electricity markets are

potential of market-based decarbonization pathways. Comprehensive reforms are needed

**Modernizing electricity markets for a clean energy future**


Historically, electricity markets in the Asia-Pacific region were built around vertically
integrated monopolies, with limited competition and heavy reliance on centralized fossil
fuel generation. Transitioning toward a modern energy system demands open, transparent
and flexible market designs capable of integrating high shares of variable renewable
energy, distributed energy resources, storage technologies and demand-side management
solutions.


Electricity market reforms aim to modernize power systems by enhancing competition,
transparency and flexibility. These include the unbundling of generation, transmission and
distribution functions to foster market efficiency and accountability. The introduction of
wholesale spot markets for electricity and ancillary services, such as frequency regulation
and balancing, allows for more accurate price discovery and system optimization. Dynamic
pricing mechanisms, including real-time pricing and time-of-use tariffs, help align consumer
demand with periods of high renewable energy availability. Additionally, capacity markets
and other flexibility mechanisms are being established to ensure resource adequacy during
peak demand or periods of low renewable output. Grid access reforms are also crucial,
enabling open and non-discriminatory connections for independent power producers and
distributed energy resources, thereby expanding market participation and innovation.
Examples include the shift towards competitive power trading platforms in India, like the
Indian Energy Exchange, the ‘two-sided markets’ in Australia and the full liberalization of
retail electricity markets in Japan.

economic impacts on all segments of society, particularly women, youth and marginalized
groups. Governments should **adopt inclusive approaches** within national energy policies,
ensuring that transition strategies reflect the diverse needs and perspectives of affected
communities and stakeholders, while enabling inclusive participation in shaping the future
energy system.


Active engagement of women, youth and marginalized groups in energy planning
and governance is also critical. Policies and programmes should **promote leadership**
**development, entrepreneurship and workforce participation** for the just energy transition.


There are few countries in the region that do not experience gender gaps in the energy
sector. Accordingly, it is imperative that all countries focus on working to build an inclusive
and gender-responsive energy sector.


_**6. Strengthen regional collaboration and policy coherence**_


Regional collaboration is essential to advance the just energy transition in Asia and the
Pacific, improve energy security, and foster inclusive and sustainable development. **Cross-**
**border initiatives,** such as the ASEAN Power Grid, South Asian regional electricity markets
and transboundary renewable energy corridors, can optimize the use of energy resources,
address imbalances in supply and demand, and reduce system costs through shared
infrastructure and economies of scale.


To fully unlock these benefits, countries must work together to **harmonize** **regulatory**
**frameworks** and technical standards. Alignment can facilitate cross-border trade and
enable the interoperability of national energy systems.


**Stronger regional supply chains** for clean energy technologies and critical energy transition
materials are equally important. Coordinated efforts can reduce costs, enhance market
access and improve resilience to external shocks. Regional cooperation can also support
local manufacturing, workforce development and innovation in clean energy technologies.

socioeconomic consequences for workers and communities reliant on fossil fuel-based
sectors. Comprehensive support measures, such as broad reskilling and vocational

mechanisms, should be included in national just energy transition plans. Opportunities for
social dialogue and participation are also critical elements of successful just transition
initiatives. These approaches will ensure that transitions avoid economic and social
hardship while also providing equal opportunity for impacted areas.


Building climate resilience into infrastructure projects through rigorous climate risk
assessments and the adoption of climate-proofing standards is crucial. Investments should
prioritize infrastructure that can survive severe weather events, decrease susceptibility to
climate-related disruptions and assure long-term energy security. Inclusive stakeholder
engagement – actively including local communities, industrial stakeholders, civil society
organizations and marginalized groups – is critical for achieving transparent decisionmaking and broad public support. By addressing possible disparities and increasing
participatory governance, the just energy transition may be both equitable and sustainable,
encouraging long-term socioeconomic stability and environmental sustainability.

#### **Annex II - Understanding the** **high energy intensity in the Asia-** **Pacific region**

The Asia-Pacific region has traditionally had one of the highest levels of energy intensity
worldwide, suggesting large energy use per unit of economic production. Despite major
improvements since 2000, the region’s average primary energy intensity remained about
4.4 megajoules per USD (2021 PPP) in 2022, higher than the global average of 3.9 MJ/USD
and much higher than levels in Europe and Latin America. This highly inefficient energy
consumption is the result of a combination of structural, institutional and policy variables
that differ greatly between nations and subregions. An analysis can be conducted to
determine the underlying factors influencing changes in energy consumption or intensity
over time or among different entities such as countries or sectors. These quantitative
factors include activity effect, structural effect, energy efficiency effect and electrification
effect.


_Industrial structure and economic composition:_ The industrial focus of many countries in the
Asia-Pacific region contributes significantly to their high energy intensity. Major economies,
such as China, India, Indonesia and Kazakhstan, depend substantially on energy-intensive
industries such as steel, cement, textiles, mining and petroleum. In China alone, heavy
industries accounted for more than 60 per cent of TFEC in the early 2010s. Although this
percentage has subsequently decreased, the legacy of coal-based manufacturing remains
imbedded in the energy economy (IEA, 2024h). Even as the region transitions towards
services and digital economies, especially in East Asia, developing countries continue to
build industrial capacity to sustain economic development. This expansion often includes
older, less efficient technology and inadequately insulated manufacturing buildings.
Furthermore, the heavy dependence on thermal generating, particularly coal, in energy
supply chains drives increasing primary energy consumption relative to GDP.


_Dependence on fossil fuels and low thermal efficiency:_ The prevalence of fossil fuels,
particularly coal, in many countries is a structural driver of high energy intensity. Coal
combustion is less efficient than renewable or gas-based combined-cycle systems. In
2021, thermal power production in various South and South-East Asian nations averaged
33-37 per cent efficiency, significantly below the worldwide standard of 42 per cent (IEA,
2024h). Countries such as Indonesia, Mongolia and Bangladesh continue to rely largely on
subcritical coal plants, which often lack modern grid infrastructure and emissions controls.
Furthermore, many governments continue to subsidize fossil fuels, discouraging energy
saving and lowering incentives for technological advancement. Indonesia, for example,
committed more than $33 billion in fossil fuel subsidies in 2022, considerably undermining
renewable energy and efficiency investments (ESCAP, 2024a).


_Rapid urbanization and building sector inefficiencies:_ Urbanization in the Asia-Pacific
region is accelerating at an unprecedented rate, with more than 60 per cent of the region’s

informal settlements or poorly managed housing stock. Building regulations and standards

or enforceable requirements for industry, automobiles and appliances. According to (IEA
and others, 2024), more than 40 per cent of countries in the Asia-Pacific region lack a
national energy efficiency strategy, and even fewer have specialized energy efficiency
agency with financial power. Even where rules exist – such as the PAT plan of India or
the Top-1000 firms Programme in China – implementation and monitoring are often
fragmented, especially among small and medium-sized firms. In South-East Asia and the
Pacific small island developing States, limited institutional capacity and data shortages
impede evidence-based decision-making and long-term investment planning.


_Data limitation and informality_ : Data availability and dependability are ongoing problems.
Large amounts of informal economic activity, especially in the home and microenterprise
sectors, are unaccounted for in national energy balance sheets. This results in an
underestimation of baseline consumption, poor intervention targeting and incorrect
monitoring of efficiency gains. Countries such as the Lao People’s Democratic Republic,
Myanmar and Sri Lanka struggle to obtain disaggregated energy statistics owing to a lack
of human and technological resources. Inadequate monitoring, reporting and verification
systems can make it difficult to quantify energy savings, obtain green financing or
participate in international energy efficiency initiatives.


_Technological lock-in and low innovation adoption_ : Many countries in the Asia-Pacific
region continue to use outdated technologies, frequently owing to a lack of capital, hurdles
to technology transfer and risk-averse utility practices. Industrial facilities in Pakistan,
Bangladesh and Cambodia, for example, continue to use boilers and motors that are
decades old and lack energy management systems. Smart grids, building management
systems and AI-enabled efficiency technologies have taken root in nations such as Japan,
Singapore and Australia, but have struggled to gain traction in low-income contexts. The
absence of specific financial and capacity-building mechanisms further impedes the
implementation of such solutions throughout the region (IRENA, 2024b).
