This note synthesizes the World Bank’s approach

to supporting the Philippines in addressing the

plastic waste crisis. As one of the largest con

[the Philippines is estimated to leak more than](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaz5803)

lenges in establishing a circular and sustainable

solid waste management system, due to gaps in

infrastructure, governance, policy, and technical

capacity. Although there has been strong politi
cal will to tackle plastic pollution, the Philippines

needs technical and strategic guidance, as well

as funding for well-targeted projects to catalyze

meaningful change.


This note revisits the multifaceted engage
ment of the World Bank, which helped lay the

foundation for tangible progress in combating



plastic pollution. Through strategic interventions

supported by the PROBLUE multi-donor trust

fund, the World Bank raised national awareness

of the crisis, pooled in additional resources,

and advised the government on global best

practices. At the same time, it fostered the

development of local knowledge and capacity

while providing targeted financial assistance to

drive reforms and investment.


The experience shown in this document illus
trates how a tailored approach can navigate

complex national contexts and support pollution

management efforts. It tells the story of a strate
gic partnership and investment that culminated

in a formal request from the Government of the

Philippines for a World Bank-funded project to

support a national investment program aimed

at improving waste management and circularity

in plastics and other waste streams.

# **The Context**


###### 1.1. The global plastic pollution crisis and the case of the Philippines



Plastic is an exceptionally convenient material. It

is inexpensive, resilient, lightweight, corrosion-re
sistant, and easy to process. [2] These attributes

have fueled global dependence on plastics,

resulting in a 200-fold increase in production

since the 1950s [3] and the critical challenge of

sustainable plastic waste management (PWM).


Plastic can leak into the natural environment at

any stage of its life cycle, including raw material

extraction, production, packaging, distribution,

consumption, maintenance, recycling, reuse,

and final disposal or recovery. This issue is par
ticularly severe in marine ecosystems, where

plastic pollution from both land-based and

marine-based sources continues to flow into

the ocean. Once at sea, marine plastics signifi
cantly impact marine life, economies, human

health and society. [4]


The East Asia and Pacific region is among the

largest producers of plastic waste globally, [5]

with five of the top ten marine plastic pollut
ers located in this area. Malaysia, Indonesia, and

the Philippines have the highest proportion of

generated plastic waste entering the ocean—

with the Philippines alone leaking more than

0.3 million tonnes of plastic waste into marine

ecosystems each year [1], equivalent to 8.9 % of

the national plastic waste.


The staggering volume of mismanaged plastic

waste in the Philippines stems from a conflu
ence of factors, including rapid urbanization,

increasing consumerism, and insufficient solid

waste management (SWM) infrastructure. [6] One

of the main issues is that the Philippines has

become a major consumer of single use plas

(about 17.5 billion pieces annually), and 45 mil

billion pieces per year. [7] Most of these SUPs end

up in landfills, open dumpsites, or leaks into

waterways, ultimately flowing into rivers and

the ocean. [8]

###### 1.2. The missing links for circularity in the Philippines



Plastic circularity aims to impede the generation

of waste and pollution, promoting the preven
tion, or otherwise reuse and recycling of plastic

waste. This involves both pre-consumption

measures, such as product standards, restrict
ing disposal and incentivizing use of alternative

materials, and post-consumption actions, like

separate waste collection, sorting and recycling.


In the Philippines, rising imports of virgin plas
tic and plastic products (around USD 2 billion

worth of imports annually [9] ) coupled with the

lack of improvements in PWM infrastructure

[and policies, have led to low recycling rates of](https://archive.wwf.org.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/WWF_A4_PolicyBrief_PHILIPPINES.pdf)

[aproximately9% (as of 2019)](https://archive.wwf.org.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/WWF_A4_PolicyBrief_PHILIPPINES.pdf) [10] and a substantial

loss of material value that could otherwise be

recovered through reuse and recycling. But what

is preventing the realization of plastics circular
ity in the Philippines?


Sound PWM requires dedicated infrastructure

and recycling-oriented regulatory standards.

However, the Philippines faces clear logistical

and economic barriers to collecting recycla
ble plastic waste. As a vast archipelago with

over 7,500 islands, the country faces high costs

for building and maintaining PWM infrastruc
ture. The lack of effective implementation of

policies and targeted guidelines for standard
ized recycling further hinder a circular plastic

economy. Guidelines and other regulations are

crucial for the successful implementation of

circular PWM and for the development of

dedicated infrastructure.


The plastic crisis in the country has already led

to high pollution levels and detrimental effects

on public health, tourism, and natural resources.

###### 2.1. Gaining momentum through foundational analytical studies



Back in 2019-2020, the priority was to gener
ate government interest and build momentum

to address the plastic crisis. However, signifi
cant knowledge gaps were a major obstacle to

developing effective plans for the transition to

circularity. Specifically, there was little founda
tional knowledge to address central questions

such as:


- _What are the main obstacles to sustainable_
_PWM in the country?_

- _What key actions should authorities take to_
_shift toward sustainability in PWM?_


The World Bank played a key role in help
ing fill these knowledge gaps using in-house

experience, providing highly needed techni
cal expertise, and coordinating the pooling of

funding from different sources. For these types

of large-scale environmental pollution crises,

action cannot be effectively planned without a

solid knowledge base. In the Philippines, there

was no adequate data on amounts of gener
ated plastic waste, types of plastics found in

the environment, or hotspot locations of plastic

leakage into waterways. These data were essen
tial to build awareness of causes and severity

among government agencies, local stakehold
ers, and NGOs.



As initial step, the World Bank collaborated with

local experts from the _University of Philippines_

to conduct a comprehensive study on plastic

waste pollution in the Pasig River—a top emitter

of marine plastic through Manila Bay. [13] The study

involved field and remote-sensing surveys of

plastic waste and revealed the Philippines’ heavy

reliance on single-use plastics (SUPs). The top 10

plastic items that were found—comprising 85.7%

of total plastics sampled in the Pasig River—

were SUPs: polystyrene, _sando_ bags (single-use

carrier bags), _labo_ (flimsy without handle) bags,

snack wrappers, drink wrappers, PET bottles,

diapers/sanitary napkins, candy wrappers,

straws, and noodles/seasoning packaging. [13]


In addition to the lack of data on plastic waste,

a strategic and technical analysis of the barriers

preventing plastic circularity was also missing.

The World Bank led the pivotal and nationally

relevant report “ _[Market Study for the Philippines:](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/61e2e030-9dc2-5013-a8ff-7565919e17ee)_

_[Plastics Circularity Opportunities and Barriers](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/61e2e030-9dc2-5013-a8ff-7565919e17ee)_ ”

14, which was supported by the Department of

Environment and Natural Resources of the Phil
ippines and funded through Korea’s Korea Green

Growth Trust Fund (KGGTF). The market study

carried out baseline data collection and anal
ysis and engaged both the private sector and

government stakeholders to reveal the amount

###### 3.1. The Philippines’ roadmap for management of plastic waste and reduction of SUPs: a vision for short and long-term interventions.



The Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of

2000 [11] requires that all barangays have mate
rial recovery facilities, which are essential to

recover recyclable plastic. However, the reality

in the Philippines was that, in Manila, only about

20% of barangays in Manila had material recov
ery facilities, and only 44% nationwide. This led

to informal waste collectors (small enterprises,

individuals or families) to manage ~50% of the

plastic waste in the Philippines. As a result,

two-thirds of the barangays could not comply

with the 2000 act [8] due to lack of resources and

infrastructure for proper waste collection and

recovery of recyclables.


The World Bank was well-aware of these con
ditions on the ground thanks to the extensive

stakeholder consultations during the planning

stages. As a result, the prepared roadmap pro
posed a systematic approach to improving PWM

that was realistic and tailored to the current sys
tem and its limitations. Three strategic outcomes

were provided in the roadmap:


- closing plastic leakage pathways (by 2028)

- enabling plastic recycling (by 2034)

- managing plastic demand and have products
designed for circularity (by 2040).


These strategic outcomes reflect not only tech
nical targets, but also a shared vision shaped

by those working closest to the problem, from

governmental organizations to waste collectors,



civil society, and communities. Therefore, the

approach became more than a document

informing policy development; it was recognized

as a practical, inclusive path toward a more sus
tainable and equitable PWM system.


By mapping actions across immediate, medium,

and long-term timeframes, and by involving local

experts, the roadmap became both a technical

guide and a capacity-building tool. It ensured

that knowledge and ownership stayed within the

country, empowering Philippine institutions to

lead the transition and sustain progress beyond

the initial investment stages.


The Philippines’ Roadmap on SUPs also cata
lyzed downstream partnerships that were key

to implementing its strategic outcomes. Under

the leadership of the DENR and the UNDP

country office, the Philippines deepened its

commitment to addressing plastic pollution by

joining the Global Plastic Action Partnership

(GPAP) in 2023, which led to the official launch

of a [National Plastic Action Partnership (NPAP)](https://www.globalplasticaction.org/philippines)

in 2025. The NPAP is a strategic multi-stake
holder platform that aims at accelerating the

Philippines’ transition to a circular economy for

plastics through evidence-based strategies.

###### 3.2. Supporting EPR in the Philippines



Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) is a

critical mechanism to achieve or improve cir
cularity in PWM. EPR is a policy approach that

legally obliges producers to take full responsibil
ity for their products throughout their life cycle,

including the post-consumer phase, when the

products and packaging become waste. [16]


On July 26 [th], 2021, the Congress of the Philip
pines made an important step forward in the

fight against plastic pollution by institutional
izing EPR for a broad range of plastic products

through the Republic Act No. 11898 “ _Extended_

_Producer Responsibility Act of 2022_ ”. [16] This act

built on the previous Ecological Solid Waste

Management Act of 2000 [11] and provided policies

for a systematic, comprehensive and ecological

PWM program.


At the early stages of EPR implementation, the

government demonstrated strong ambition;

however, the country lacked practical experience

and the technical know-how to operationalize

the law. The World Bank supported the Philip
pine Government in designing the EPR regulation

through the study “ _[Combating the Plastic Waste](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099201003082324515/p177183036b0c904c08b1a0526fcb062538)_

_[Crisis in the Philippines Implementing Extended](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099201003082324515/p177183036b0c904c08b1a0526fcb062538)_

_[Producer Responsibility with Lessons Learned](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099201003082324515/p177183036b0c904c08b1a0526fcb062538)_

_[from Korea](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099201003082324515/p177183036b0c904c08b1a0526fcb062538)_ ”. [17] The report examined Korea’s

experience, since Korea has a long history of

successful EPR implementation. It assessed the

policy context at the time Korea introduced EPR

legislation, how the policy was developed, and

how it now functions in practice. [17]


The study also provides key recommendations

for EPR success in the Philippines, including:




- clear legislation (a comprehensive legal
framework with unambiguous provisions),

- realistic targets

- an enforceable monitoring system (public
record of producers, regular and random
inspections, on-site audits)

- financial flows (for local PWM and infrastructure)

- operational arrangements (waste separation,
collection, sorting, and recycling, and eco-design of products).


This study was shared with the government of

the Philippines, including the DENR-Environ
mental Management Bureau (EMB), which was

leading the EPR National Framework.


Furthermore, there were significant concerns

among government representatives of potential

downstream distributional impacts of the EPR

law. The World Bank played a fundamental role

at these stage by conducting a technical study

on “ _Distributional Impacts of Plastics Policies_

_and Economic Instruments_,” using the Plastic

Policy Simulator (PPS): a technology-financial

model that estimates how firms and households

will react to plastic policy instruments, and what

the potential costs, revenues, and other impacts

of these policies will be before laws are passed

or public money is spent. This study comple
mented also quantitative and qualitative surveys

to determine consumers’ and communities’

awareness and perceived effectiveness of the

regulations on plastic pollution. [18] This technical

work allowed for the Philippine Government to

understand how policies on PWM could impact

people livelihoods and jobs.

###### 3.3. The intuition of drawing lessons from Korea



The World Bank sought to bring in a capable

and experienced international partner to facili
tate capacity development for implementation

of EPR. Given its regional proximity and proven

success in EPR implementation, South Korea

was identified as a potential partner.


Korea’s impactful EPR and waste circularity pol
icies managed to boost recycling rates, offering

valuable lessons for other countries. [21] To facilitate

knowledge transfer, The World Bank coordinated

knowledge exchange events between Korean

experts and policy makers from the Philippines,

including DENR and the Department of Finance.

The goal was for Korean experts to share their

experience in implementing the necessary policy

and regulation to achieve effective EPR.
