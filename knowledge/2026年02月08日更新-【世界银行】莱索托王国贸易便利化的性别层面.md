This study is one component of broader World Bank trade
facilitation assistance program provided to the government
of the Kingdom of Lesotho (hereafter referred as Lesotho).
This note presents key findings from the study, which focused
on identifying the challenges faced by **women owned, led**
**or managed and/or men owned, led or managed firms** _[14]_
(also referred to as “ **traders** ”) and **customs brokers** as
they complete cross-border processes and procedures
to import and/or export goods in Lesotho, as well as their
experiences with other important aspects of trading, such as
issues of border infrastructure and safety at border facilities.
Recommendations to address the challenges identified are
also included in this note, primarily within and beyond the
scope of the World Trade Organization’s Trade Facilitation
Agreement (WTO TFA). _[15]_


WTO TFA measures are assumed to be nondiscriminatory
and applicable to all traders in principle; however, there
is limited research on how trade facilitation interventions
impact women and men at the firm level. Without genderdisaggregated data, it is difficult to know whether all traders
utilize trade facilitation measures effectively and thus benefit
from them to the same degree. Closing this knowledge gap
will facilitate improved design of inclusive trade policy reforms
and program interventions. The World Bank (WB)—through
the Trade Facilitation Support Program (TFSP)—therefore
supported the undertaking of this study to identify the genderspecific obstacles that traders and customs brokers face when
engaging in cross-border trade of merchandise goods, with a
particular focus on the areas covered in the WTO TFA. _[16]_ This
study builds on similar surveys conducted in other countries _[17]_


This report presents findings from phone-based interviews of
620 traders and 38 customs brokers across Lesotho during
October 9–December 14, 2023. Due to the small sample size
of customs brokers (23 women and 15 men), the analysis of
data for customs brokers was not disaggregated by gender
because doing so would not provide statistically reliable insights.
The analysis identifies areas for improvement in cross-border
trade-related processes and procedures and highlights where

participating in international trade. Of the surveyed firms, only

**2** **Transparency**


2.1 31 percent women traders reported facing difficulties in finding
trade-related information. Among the firms encountering challenges

two most common sources of information for trade firms are personal

2.2 43 percent of women traders found regulations on trade-related

40 percent of women traders reported that use of customs brokers is
mandatory, when in fact it is not mandatory, an example of there being
a misunderstanding on the official rules.


**3** **Public Private Consultations**


3.1 An overwhelming majority of traders (94 percent of women traders)
and 76 percent brokers are not aware of the existence of the National
Trade Facilitation Committee (NTFC).


3.2 A total of 73 percent women traders have never been consulted on
changes to official trade regulations and processes. Among those not
being consulted, 31 percent women traders believe their businesses are
too small to be consulted while 40 percent brokers were not aware of
opportunities for consultations. Of those who are consulted, 36 percent
did not think their feedback was considered.


3.3 74 percent of women traders were not members of trade or industry
associations, with the lowest membership among micro firms
(19 percent). Of traders not belonging to an association, 67 percent of
women traders reported not being aware of trade associations.


**4** **Border Agency Cooperation**


Compounding the issue of delayed consultations and awareness
among traders of changes to regulations, is the fact that changes to
procedures in South Africa necessitates corresponding adjustments
in Lesotho, often leaving minimal time for Lesotho to solicit input from
traders and customs brokers before implementation.

Between October and December 2023, 620 traders and
38 customs brokers were surveyed in Lesotho. _[18]_ Twenty-six
percent of trade firms were owned and/or managed by women
(hereafter referred to as “women traders”), while 74 percent
were owned and/or managed by men (hereafter referred
to as “men traders”). Of the brokers interviewed, however,
a majority were women (23 women customs brokers out of
38 interviewed or 61 percent) (Figure 1).


Educational attainment among survey respondents was
relatively high. More than 70 percent of respondents attained
a diploma or university degree, with slightly more women
traders completing higher education compared to men traders
and customs brokers. This aligns with other research on
education and gender in Lesotho showing that gender gaps
in education are more favorable to women and girls than men
and boys. _[19]_


The majority of traders had less than 10 years of experience
(62 percent of women and 66 percent of men). On the other
hand, customs brokers were more experienced in cross-border
trade, with 58 percent of them having more than 10 years of
experience.


The majority of women and men traders led micro and smallsized firms (87 percent and 92 percent, respectively). _[20]_ Less
than five percent of surveyed firms were large firms (Figure 2).


Figure 1. Gender Breakdown of Respondents



Ninety-two percent of firms in Lesotho were involved in imports
only, with a minimal difference among men and women traders _._
This is in line with the national statistics wherein Lesotho’s
net trade in goods (balance of payments in current USD)
has historically been negative. _[21]_


The main source of finance for a majority of women and
men traders are personal or company savings (66 percent of
women and 65 percent of men), followed by official bank loans
(25 precent for both men and women). Fifty-five percent of
customs brokers also use company savings to finance their
customs clearing businesses (Figure 3).


Women traders traded less frequently than men (33 percent of
women reported trading more than once a month compared
to 52 percent of men traders) (Figure 4). Interestingly, more
women reported expanding their trading activities outside
of the African continent. While both women and men traders
imported mostly from the Republic of South Africa (86 and
93 percent, respectively), more women also imported from the
rest of the world (13 percent compared to 6 percent of men).
A similar pattern is observed with exports, where 21 percent of
women traders exported to outside of the continent compared
to only nine percent of men. A larger share of women traders
(44 percent) compared to men (26 percent) exported animal
and vegetable products and beverages; whereas a larger share
of men traders (29 percent) exported mineral, plastic, rubber
products compared to women (22 percent).

### **1. Increase Participation** **of Women in Trade**

In Lesotho, there are fewer women traders than men traders
(26 percent vs 74 percent), and they also trade less frequently
than men. _[22]_ Research suggests women comprise 39 percent
of formal businesses in Lesotho _[23]_ yet only 26 percent are
engaged in international trade may be indicative of genderspecific barriers to engaging in cross border trade.


While Lesotho has made progress in promoting gender equality
through legislative reforms and policy initiatives in recent
years, challenges persist in translating these efforts into



tangible improvements in women’s economic empowerment
and participation. In 2023, the female labor force participation
rate was 57.6 percent compared to 73 percent for male
employees. _[24]_ Research has highlighted persistent gender
disparities and barriers to women’s economic empowerment
in Lesotho. _[25]_ Issues such as gender-based violence and
disadvantageous social norms appear to hinder the full
realization of economic potential for women in Lesotho.


The survey also found that a larger number of women traders
(41 percent) compared to men (33 percent) have no further

## **Annex A** Survey Methodology

34


**17274-WB_Lesotho TF-2ndPgs.indd  34** **6/10/25  11:19 AM**


The study aimed to identify specific barriers that women-led
(referred to as women traders) and men-led firms (referred to
as men traders) and customs brokers face in conducting crossborder trade of goods in Lesotho. Two sets of questionnaires
adapted for traders and custom brokers were developed
based on standard WB methodology. The questionnaires were
adapted to the Lesotho context and provided in English or
Sesotho depending on the respondent’s preference.


Formally registered trading firms and custom brokers engaged
in cross-border trade were included in the study, provided
they met the following criteria:


**Trade Firms:**


- The firm conducted three or more trade transactions in the
previous 12 months.


- Respondent was either the owner or top manager of the
business.


- State-owned enterprises, binational and public companies,
union entities, nonprofit, and religious organizations were not
eligible.


**Customs Brokers:**


- Licensed brokers involved in providing services related to
export and/or import of goods.


**Women-led:** A firm was defined as “women-led” if
the CEO/top manager/head of the firm was a woman
or the majority (over 50 percent) of the shareholders
were women. The survey captured the gender of both
CEO/top manager/head of the firm and the gender of
the owner (holder of at least 50 percent of company
shares). The definition of the women-led/men-led was
based on the gender of the respondent.


**Firm Size** was defined by the number of full-time
employees, according to the following criteria:

  - Micro: <5

  - Small: 5–19

  - Medium: 20–99

  - Large: 100+

The study acknowledges the following limitations:


- The study exclusively interviewed firms involved in trading
goods and not services because the scope of the survey was
primarily on the WTO TFA, which covers trade of goods only.


- During the screening process, several firms were identified
as no longer trading. The study only assessed active trading
firms, with no exploration into reasons why trading firms
ceased operations or why firms do not trade in the first place.


- While the survey gathered perspectives from owners and
top managers of trade firms, it is important to note that these
viewpoints might not fully capture the gender dynamics and
challenges faced by all individuals involved in cross-border
trade, particularly those at different levels of decision-making
or with other roles.


- The study only focused on identifying challenges to
the cross-border trade of traders with officially registered
transactions and not informal traders. _[49]_
