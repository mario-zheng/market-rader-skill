### **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**


The digital revolution is changing every aspect of our lives. International trade, as
one of the main pillars of a globalized world, is at the center of this revolution, as
cross-border transactions of goods and services become an integral, often invisible, part of our daily digital routine.


Digital trade benefits from a comprehensive, modern and non-discriminatory regulatory framework. Regulation can provide the legal tools necessary for remote
contracts, clarify the rights and obligations of the multiple actors involved in digital transactions, and establish a framework that promotes trust in digital markets,
even when the consumer does not know the merchant or that the merchant is in a
different country. However, regulation can also further strengthen segmentation
of digital trade, de facto restricting digital transactions.


The report presents the Digital Trade Regulatory Readiness (DTRR) database
compiled by a team of World Bank experts. The database offers novel information on laws and regulations on digital trade for 121 economies around the world,
out of which 95 are developing countries. The database reflects countries’ regulatory framework on specific matters directly relevant to digital trade, structure
on nine “regulatory areas” (i.e., i) electronic documents; ii) electronic signature &
digital ID; iii) paperless trade; iv) online consumer protection; v) data protection;
vi) artificial Intelligence; vii) cybersecurity & cybercrime; viii) cross-border data;
and ix) intermediary liability) distributed along three policy pillars, namely electronic transactions, trust-building regulation, and platform regulation. Altogether,
the DTRR offers more than 13,000 datapoints, including over 5,200 regulatory

### **DTRR REGULATORY AREAS**



**A comprehensive and modern digital regu-**
**latory framework is essential for supporting**
**digital trade.** Regulation plays three critical roles
for digital transactions. It provides the legal tools
needed for concluding valid and enforceable electronic contracts, such as rules giving effect to the
electronic authentication of documents and e-signatures. Frameworks for personal data protection,



online consumer protection, and cybersecurity
offer safeguards that enhance trust in digital markets. Finally, regulations on the legal liability of
platforms for unlawful or harmful content and rules
on the international transfers of data set out basic
rules that support a transparent and predictable
framework for digital markets.


**Regulation can also introduce unwarranted**
**limitations to digital trade.** For instance, unnecessarily restrictive or overly cumbersome
requirements for cross-border data transfers limit
access to certain digitally delivered services. Limitations on the types of goods and services offered
online may be warranted by public policy goals
(e.g., restrictions on guns or controlled substances)
or may add costs and inefficiencies by segmenting
markets (e.g., requiring the use of domestic cloud
services). The central challenge for policymakers and regulators is therefore to adopt balanced
frameworks that minimize restrictions on digital
trade while offering a comprehensive framework
for digital transactions and sound safeguards for
users. By requiring that public policy measures
minimize unnecessary trade restrictions, trade
agreements help establish useful benchmarks for
setting regulatory boundaries.


**The DTRR database focuses on horizontal reg-**
**ulations that provide essential regulatory tools**
**for remote transactions, promote trust in digital**
**markets, and define rules for online platforms.**
The data gathered helps inform global progress
on digital governance by identifying regulatory
regimes that lack elements essential to digital
trade, thereby introducing costs in the form of legal
uncertainty for consumers and providers. In this
context, “readiness” is intended as the ability of
the regulation to comprehensively address the
challenges presented by digital technologies in line
with international best practices.


**The database offers novel information on laws**
**and regulations on digital trade around the world.**
The database reflects countries’ regulatory framework on specific matters directly relevant to digital
trade, specifically in the areas of electronic transactions, trust-building regulation, and platform
regulation. The DTRR offers a total of over novel

**iii. Different legal systems**

**It is important to note that the DTRR does not**
**account for differences in legal systems or the**
**various sources of law they may recognize** .
Instead, it focuses exclusively on written laws
and regulations adopted by legislative or executive bodies. Legal systems around the world differ
in the sources they recognize for establishing general rules. One key distinction is between civil law
and common law traditions. Civil law systems rely
primarily on written instruments—laws and regulations enacted by legislatures or, in some cases,
adopted by the executive branch under specific
conditions. In contrast, common law systems,
while also recognizing written legislation, give
legal weight to judicial decisions. These decisions,

enabling framework for digital trade by identifying
regulatory gaps, good practices, and restrictions.
However, the lack of policy coordination across
countries poses a risk of regulatory fragmentation.
If countries develop isolated digital trade frameworks, this could lead to marginalization of certain
economies and the formation of suboptimal regulatory blocs that hinder seamless cross-border
digital transactions.


To avoid this, fostering international regulatory
cooperation is essential. Alignment with global
best practices—through multilateral agreements
(e.g., WTO e-commerce negotiations), regional initiatives, or bilateral dialogues—can help ensure
a cohesive and inclusive digital trade ecosystem.
