Between the late 1990s and the mid-2010s, the rise of software-as-a-service (SaaS) fundamentally
dismantled the on-premises application market. Early SaaS pioneers such as Salesforce (founded in 1999)
demonstrated that multi-tenant, subscription-based delivery could outperform licensed, install-andmaintain software on cost, speed, and scalability. By the late 2000s, SaaS adoption accelerated rapidly as
cloud infrastructure matured, culminating in the 2010–2015 period when SaaS became the dominant
model for new enterprise software purchases. These SaaS companies built their new business on the back
of cloud software infrastructure. These foundational capabilities, spanning cloud infrastructure, cloud
platform services, cloud operations, and cloud financial and commercial management, were crucial
prerequisites for SaaS adoption at scale and had to mature before the SaaS market could fully take off.


Much like the industry’s transition from on-premises software to SaaS, agentic AI will also usher in a new
software delivery model, leading to very different underlying software infrastructure (see figure 1). A
software infrastructure model is beginning to take shape for agentic AI, providing the foundational
services required to enable the transition from application-centric SaaS platforms to agent-centric agentas-a service (AaaS) systems. In the following sections we describe how this agentic AI software
infrastructure landscape is shaping up and then offer some investment hypotheses to capitalize on this
shift.

## The evolving agentic AI software infrastructure landscape

The agentic AI software infrastructure market remains nascent and rapidly evolving. While definitive,
mature platforms akin to today’s cloud databases or container orchestration layers do not yet exist, clear
patterns of capabilities are crystalizing. These early patterns provide a valuable lens into how investors,
vendors, builders, and enterprise adopters should think about future infrastructure investments.


At its core, agentic AI infrastructure must support a set of emerging capabilities that extend well beyond
traditional AI platforms. First, solutions must be capable of running in secure, standalone environments
that meet enterprise compliance and data governance requirements. Second, they must support dynamic
discovery and integration with external services and data sources, enabling agents to act on up-to-date
business context. Closely related is the ability for agents to discover and collaborate with one another,
sharing work and knowledge rather than operating as isolated silos. Persistent memory, both short-term
and long-term, is essential, as is the ability to store evolving decision context that reflects how agents
learn over time rather than simply recording transaction results. From an operational perspective,
infrastructure must enable secure, cost-effective scaling of agents at enterprise scale, paired with tooling
to measure and understand the economics of agent execution—a prerequisite for cost-effective
operations, pricing, and commercial viability. Finally, the infrastructure must support the integration of
third-party agents and services, enabling ecosystems of agents to coalesce around value streams.

The emerging landscape of agentic AI infrastructure can be logically decomposed into a series of
functional layers (see figure 2):


 - **Agentic platforms**, including an _agentic AI runtime_ that provides the core execution layer where

This is the locus of autonomous decision-making. Complementing this is an _agentic AI orchestration_
layer, responsible for coordinating agents; managing scheduling, logging, and security; reconciling
context and memory; and enabling agents to discover one another and external services.

 - **MCP (Model Context Protocol) runtime and servers,** which allow agents to interact with external

services and standardized APIs, acting as gateways and registries for MCP servers that agents access. [1]

 - **Agentic context stores**, including context and long-term memory infrastructure that persists
knowledge and evolving agent state across interactions and operational cycles, in addition to _knowledge_

_stores_ that house domain content, ontologies, structured business context, and foundational data used
by agents for informed reasoning.

 - **Agentic market capabilities**, including an _agent registry_ to make agents readily discoverable, _agent_
_catalogs_ to allow users to browse and manage available agents, and _third-party agents_ that accelerate
the development of multi-agent systems.

### **Financial and commercial management**


Financial and commercial management represents one of the most critical, and least mature, layers of the
emerging agentic AI software infrastructure stack. At a foundational level, many of the required
capabilities are not new. Enterprises still need to maintain product catalogs, configure offerings, generate
prices and quotes, send invoices, process payments, and recognize revenue. These core commercial
functions remain essential regardless of whether the underlying offering is a traditional SaaS product or an
autonomous agent-based service.


However, the systems that deliver these capabilities are themselves undergoing a profound
transformation as AI—and increasingly agentic AI—becomes embedded directly into commercial
workflows. Established enterprise platforms are beginning to infuse autonomous intelligence into
traditionally manual or rules-based processes. For example, Salesforce has introduced autonomous AI
agents capable of proactively monitoring sales pipelines and drafting quotes from natural-language
prompts. ServiceNow offers AI-powered CPQ as part of its broader workflow platform, with particular
strength in tying intelligent quoting into end-to-end service delivery and revenue processes. Similarly,
HubSpot has embedded AI into its CPQ capabilities to automatically generate personalized quotes with
minimal human intervention.


In parallel, a new class of agentic-AI-native entrants is emerging, designed from the ground up for modern
AI-centric business models. Alguna exemplifies this shift with a no-code, AI-first CPQ platform built to
support complex usage-based, hybrid, and bundled pricing structures—capabilities that are increasingly
essential for AI and agent-based offerings. Peak AI, now part of UiPath, represents a different dimension of
innovation: an agentic pricing engine that learns continuously from historical bids and market signals and
can autonomously execute pricing decisions rather than merely recommending them. On the cost

Pricing models are also poised to evolve well beyond traditional seat-based approaches. As agentic

services depend on a dynamic mix of underlying technologies—models, GPUs, cloud services, data
sources, and third-party tools—many of which experience volatile pricing. Daily or even intra-day swings in
GPU or inference pricing can amplify margin volatility, making real-time financial transparency and
adaptive controls essential rather than optional.


In the near to medium term, most solutions in this space will resemble incremental “Band-Aids”—
extensions of existing CPQ, billing, and FinOps platforms that only partially address the needs of agentic

accelerates and vendors are forced to re-architect commercial systems to support autonomous services
at scale, with transparency, control, and economic resilience built in by design.

### **Agentic platforms**


In today’s market, agentic AI runtime and orchestration are often delivered together, frequently by the
same vendors and within the same platforms. This bundling reflects the immaturity of the market rather
than a fundamental architectural requirement. While closely related, runtime and orchestration serve
distinct roles and will increasingly diverge as agentic AI moves from experimentation to enterprise-scale
deployment.


**Agentic AI runtime**


The runtime is the execution layer for an individual agent. It is responsible for how an agent reasons, acts,
and maintains local state while performing work. Core runtime capabilities include:


 - Foundation models used as basis for reasoning

 - A continuous reasoning loop (plan # act # observe # update # learn)

 - Short-term memory and working context

 - Tool and API invocation, including external services

 - Policy enforcement and guardrails at execution time


From an architectural perspective, the runtime is analogous to an application runtime or container
execution environment: it must be efficient, portable, and increasingly standardized.


**Agentic AI orchestration**


The orchestration layer, by contrast, acts as the control plane for agentic systems. It is responsible for
coordinating many agents, managing shared state, and enforcing enterprise controls across
environments. Key orchestration capabilities include:


 - Agent discovery and registration

 - Multi-agent coordination and task delegation

 - Scheduling and prioritization across agent fleets

 - State propagation and shared context management

 - Observability, logging, and tracing

 - Security boundaries, identity, and access control


As agent deployments scale, orchestration becomes the primary locus for governance, reliability, and
economic control.


**Market direction: bundled today, unbundled tomorrow**


While runtime and orchestration are currently bundled, we expect these markets to unbundle over time.
Agent runtimes will become more open and standardized, enabling agents to be portable across clouds
and platforms. Orchestration layers, meanwhile, will emerge as the strategic differentiation point,
competing on security, visibility, governance, interoperability, and cost controls, rather than on raw
execution.


The runtime market already shows clear segmentation, with the big foundation model providers leading
adoption:


 - Foundation model providers: OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemeni

 - Hyperscalers: Google (Vertex AI), Microsoft (AutoGen), AWS (Bedrock AgentCore)

 - Open-source runtimes: frameworks such as LangGraph and AutoGen

 - Embedded runtimes within automation and workflow platforms such as UiPath and ServiceNow


These runtimes differ primarily in performance characteristics, safety controls, and ecosystem integration,
rather than in core agent logic. We anticipate standards bodies will shape what this layer looks like and
over time, expect this to “commoditize” as the market demands more agent portability. Standards around
tool and data access (for example, MCP), agent-to-agent communication (for instance, A2A), execution
semantics and state (for example, Open Agent Runtime Semantics), and identity/security/governance (for
instance, W3C). We also anticipate that the “Big 3” will continue to dominate the LLM market, however, we
expect that markets will begin to form around other foundation models, such as time series (for example,
Nixtla, IBM), tabular data (for instance, H20.ai, DataRobot), consumer behaviors (for example, Google,
Meta), and industry-/domain-specific models.

### **Agentic AI operations, governance, and risk management**


Agentic AI operations, governance, and risk management capabilities together form the control plane
required to safely deploy, scale, and operate autonomous agents in enterprise environments. We describe
each in this section.


**Agentic AI operations**


Operations capabilities focus on the day-to-day execution, visibility, and evolution of agentic systems in
production. A foundational element is observability and monitoring, which extends beyond traditional
service health metrics to capture detailed telemetry on agent behavior. This includes agent actions,
planning decisions, tool invocations, API interactions, and downstream outcomes. By adding semantic
context to logs, metrics, and traces, observability enables teams to answer not just what happened, but
why an agent behaved the way it did. These capabilities are critical for detecting unexpected behavior in
real time, debugging failures, optimizing agent decision quality, and understanding interactions across
LLMs, tools, and enterprise systems. Leading providers in this space include Fiddler AI and Zenity.


A second operational pillar is life cycle and change management. Agentic systems are dynamic by nature,
requiring disciplined management of agent versions, configuration changes, policy updates, and rollbacks
across development and production environments. Similar to DevOps and MLOps, but tailored for
reasoning systems, these capabilities ensure safe deployment of updates, track and govern drift across

**Agentic AI governance**


Governance capabilities define the boundaries within which agents are allowed to operate. This includes
enforcing enterprise and regulatory policies, managing model risk, automating policy enforcement, and

regulatory regimes such as the EU AI Act, HIPAA, and GDPR, while also enforcing internal policies related

**Agentic AI risk management**


Agentic AI risk management capabilities systematically identify, assess, and mitigate threats arising from
autonomous agents. There are standard threat frameworks emerging to address the new class of threats

3
that agentic AI introduces. Emerging threats include:


**Agency threats (identity and misuse)**


These threats focus on the “agentic” nature of the AI—its ability to act on behalf of a user and hold
credentials. Examples include the following:


 - **Agent impersonation and credential theft.** Attackers steal or forge the service tokens/API keys used
by an agent to gain persistent access to systems. Because these agents possess valid credentials, their
malicious actions may look legitimate.

 - **Agent hijacking and goal manipulation.** Attackers trick the agent into abandoning its original,
secure instructions to follow malicious, user-injected goals.

 - **Memory poisoning.** Agents often have persistent memory (long-term storage). Attackers can implant
false or malicious data into this memory, causing the agent to behave maliciously in future, unrelated
sessions.

 - **Multi-agent communication poisoning.** In systems using multiple, collaborating agents, attackers
can corrupt the communication channel between them, creating a cascade of failures.


**Autonomy threats (runaway and logic)**


These threats emerge from the AI’s ability to operate without human supervision, making decisions and
taking actions.


 - **Cascading failures (runaway agents).** An agent might misinterpret its goal, leading to unexpected,
autonomous actions that cause widespread system disruption, data leaks, or resource exhaustion (DoS).

 - **Unsafe tool/API orchestration.** Agents can be manipulated into using their authorized tools (code
interpreters, database connectors) in dangerous ways, such as executing remote code (RCE) or
accessing unauthorized data.

 - **Adaptive evasion.** Agents can be instructed to analyze security defenses and adapt their behavior in
real time to avoid detection, essentially creating autonomous malware.


**Access threats (context and data)**


These threats relate to the agent’s ability to interface with sensitive data sources (via retrieval-augmented
generation, or RAG) and external systems.


 - **Privilege escalation through chaining.** A single agent might have limited access, but by
manipulating it to call multiple tools in a chain (API chaining), an attacker can access sensitive data or
systems the agent was not directly authorized to access.

 - **Data exposure (RAG poisoning).** Agents accessing RAG systems can be forced to disclose
confidential information embedded in their context windows.

 - **Insecure tool integration.** Weak integration of tools (like SQL databases or file systems) allows
agents to be manipulated into performing actions that breach data confidentiality or integrity.


Today, risk governance platforms exist to help organizations track and manage these threats, with
companies such as NomaSecurity, Reco, Credo AI, and Fiddler AI as early entrants into this space.
However, these platforms will need to continue to evolve to support not just governance, but also realtime traceability, threat detection, and incident response to address these new threat vectors.


Audit and traceability capabilities are needed to ensure that every agent action, decision path, and data
access event is recorded in a tamper-evident and reviewable manner. These capabilities support
regulatory reporting, enable root-cause analysis, and provide transparency into autonomous behavior.
Platforms such as ModelOp and Credo AI play a central role in this domain.


Incident response and threat detection capabilities are needed to provide real-time detection of
anomalous or unsafe agent behavior, such as policy violations, prompt exploitation, or attempted data
exfiltration, and enable automated or human-in-the-loop responses. These tools help contain fast-moving
threats before they escalate, provide context-aware remediation actions, and integrate agent behavior
into broader SecOps workflows. Leading providers, including Obsidian Security and CrowdStrike, are
beginning to incorporate agentic risk management capabilities within their SecOps offerings, but we
believe these capabilities will need to be combined within the broader agentic AI risk management
platform to be fully effective.

### **Agentic context stores**


Vector databases have emerged as a foundational building block for modern AI systems, particularly
those designed to reason, retrieve information, and take action autonomously. Their primary role is to
enable semantic understanding, allowing AI systems to work with meaning rather than exact matches. In
practice, vector databases underpin RAG by grounding large language model responses in enterprise
data, significantly reducing hallucinations and increasing trust. They also serve as a form of long-term
memory for agents, persisting facts, conversations, decisions, and learned experiences over time. Beyond
retrieval, vector databases play a growing role in agent planning and execution by enabling semantic
matching between tasks and available tools or workflows; supporting personalization through latent
similarity across users, products, or content; and enabling multimodal reasoning across text, images,
audio, and structured signals within a unified search paradigm.


A number of vector database platforms have gained traction in enterprise AI stacks. Pinecone offers a fully
managed vector database service optimized for scale and operational simplicity. Milvus provides an opensource foundation with managed cloud options, appealing to organizations seeking greater control and
extensibility. Chroma is often used in early-stage and developer-centric environments due to its
lightweight, open-source nature. Major cloud providers also offer native vector database capabilities
embedded within their platforms; while these offerings typically provide a narrower feature set, they often
deliver higher performance and tighter integration with adjacent cloud services, making them attractive
for latency-sensitive or highly standardized deployments.


While vector databases are crucial, agentic AI systems depend on a broader set of knowledge stores to
function effectively in real-world enterprise environments. Graph databases play a particularly important
role by storing entities and the relationships between them, such as people and roles, systems and
dependencies, policies and constraints, or tasks and prerequisites. This relational structure enables agents
to reason about how elements connect, not just what they are. As a result, graph databases are essential
for multi-step planning, dependency resolution, root-cause analysis, and more sophisticated
organizational and system-level reasoning. Leading vendors in this space include Neo4j and TigerGraph,
both of which are commonly used in complex enterprise knowledge graphs.

where the majority of enterprise knowledge resides, including policies, procedures, contracts, technical
documentation, and institutional context that rarely exists in structured systems of record. For agents to

### **MCP runtimes and servers**


The Model Context Protocol (MCP) ecosystem is rapidly coalescing around several distinct but
complementary elements: MCP runtimes, MCP server providers, and emerging marketplaces and catalogs
that connect the two. Together, these form the execution and integration fabric for agentic AI systems
and external services.


On the runtime and gateway side, several classes of providers have emerged. Cloud-native MCP
platforms, including offerings from AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, extend existing cloud
control planes to support MCP-based access to services, data, and tools. These platforms emphasize tight
integration with native identity, security, and observability services, making them a natural choice for
organizations building agents directly on hyperscaler infrastructure.


Alongside these, a set of pure-play enterprise MCP platforms has emerged, designed from the outset to
support MCP as a first-class abstraction. Platforms such as Workato, TrueFoundry, and Docker’s MCP
Gateway focus on securely exposing enterprise systems, workflows, and integrations as MCP servers, with
built-in governance, life cycle management, and operational controls. These platforms are not tied to a
single cloud provider and are typically optimized for complex, cross-system enterprise use cases.


A third category consists of API gateway platforms that have expanded to support MCP, including Kong
Enterprise, Tyk, Gravitee.io, and Zuplo. These providers extend familiar API management capabilities—
authentication, authorization, rate limiting, routing, and observability—to MCP traffic. In this model, MCP is
treated as an additional protocol to be governed within an existing API control plane, rather than as the
core platform abstraction.

### **Agentic market**


As agent development becomes increasingly democratized across the enterprise, organizations will need
a centralized way to manage, govern, and scale these agents. Borrowing from data mesh principles,
leading organizations are beginning to expose agents through a centralized agent catalog, where agent
owners can publish their agents as managed products. Access controls and governance mechanisms can
then be applied based on the characteristics and risk profile of each agent, helping ensure compliance
with security, cost, and operational guidelines. For companies where agentic AI development has already
proliferated, these catalog capabilities are becoming essential—not optional. Today, many of these early
catalog-like capabilities are being delivered by platforms such as ServiceNow as well as platforms
historically focused on data products like Alation and Atlan. We also expect developer-centric platforms
such as Hugging Face and GitHub to evolve their offerings to support more distributed, agentic mesh
operating models.


A significant portion of the agents surfaced through these catalogs will come from third-party providers.
Major enterprise software platforms, including ServiceNow, Salesforce, SAP, and Oracle, are already
developing and deploying thousands of agents across their ecosystems. At the same time, thousands of
start-ups are building highly specialized agents tailored to narrow domains or workflows. This has resulted
in a highly fragmented market where discovering high-quality, fit-for-purpose agents is increasingly
difficult. We expect agent evaluation and certification services to emerge as a vital layer in this ecosystem,
helping enterprises navigate the market, compare alternatives, and select agents with confidence.

Beyond human-driven discovery through catalogs, agents will also need to be discoverable dynamically
by other agents in real time. This is where agent registries become essential. Registries define how agents
are described, registered, discovered, invoked, governed, and made portable across environments. They
enable agents to expose their capabilities and make themselves discoverable through predefined,
standard protocols. While there is not yet a single dominant standard for agent registries, several adjacent
standards are rapidly evolving to support these capabilities, including OpenAPI Initiative, emerging agentto-agent (A2A) protocols, MCP, OAuth 2.0, OCI, and governance efforts from bodies such as W3C and
ISO. Early examples of registry-focused solutions include offerings such as Credo AI and Collibra, while
many agent development platforms are embedding registry-like capabilities directly into their toolchains.


Looking ahead, we expect the agentic AI market and supporting technology ecosystem to evolve rapidly
over the next 12 to 24 months. Several core capabilities remain immature or fragmented, but as
enterprises confront the operational reality of managing hundreds—or eventually thousands—of agents,
the need to centralize agent discovery, governance, and life cycle management will become unavoidable.
Organizations that move early to establish agent catalogs and registries will be better positioned to scale
agentic AI safely, cost-effectively, and with appropriate enterprise controls.

### **Development and testing**


Agent development represents a fundamental shift from traditional application engineering, requiring a
hybrid of prompt engineering and conventional software development. As a result, a diverse and stillfragmenting ecosystem of development platforms and software development kits (SDKs) has emerged.
Some SDKs are purpose-built for agentic development, focusing on orchestration, tool use, and reasoning
loops. Examples include platforms such as Devin, Replit Agent, Cursor, Streamlit, CrewAI Studio, and
GitHub’s Agent Development Kit. In parallel, developers continue to rely on established machine-learning
frameworks such as PyTorch, JAX, and TensorFlow for model training and fine-tuning. This is further
complemented by agent-oriented SDKs offered directly by the major foundation-model providers,
including the OpenAI ADK, Anthropic ADK, Claude Code, Google ADK, and Antigravity, as well as cloudprovider–native offerings such as Microsoft’s Semantic Kernel and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore.


Over time, we expect agentic SDKs that integrate tightly with broader software development ecosystems
to emerge as the dominant platforms. Integration with existing code repositories, CI/CD pipelines, and
developer workflows will be a decisive advantage, particularly as software development itself undergoes
an architectural reinvention, with increasing portions of code generation, testing, and refactoring handled
by agents. While this paper does not explore that adjacent sub-market in depth, it is important to
recognize that agentic AI is reshaping not just applications, but the very process of software engineering.
Platforms like Devin, Google’s AntiGravity, and Claude Code are the leaders in realizing this vision.


Testing in an agentic AI world must also be rethought. Unlike traditional applications, agentic solutions
require validation across multiple, distinct layers. At the business-process level, testing must confirm that
the agentic system delivers the intended business outcomes and economic impact. At the application
level, teams must ensure that agentic behaviors integrate smoothly into user workflows and meet
stakeholder expectations for usability and reliability. At the agent level, testing focuses on decision quality,
stability, and the mitigation of anomalies such as hallucinations or unintended actions. At the model level,

assurance.


Looking ahead, the rise of physical AI—where agents operate in the real world through robots, vehicles,
and intelligent machines—will further expand the agentic development landscape. This shift will require
development environments that seamlessly combine virtual and physical agents, enabling teams to
design, train, and validate complex systems before deployment. At the core of this toolchain are
simulation and digital-twin platforms, which allow complete physical agents to be built and tested in
realistic virtual environments. Leading examples include NVIDIA Isaac Sim, Unity, and CoppeliaSim.

Taken together, these trends underscore a central reality for technology leaders: agentic AI development

physical agents. As a result, we anticipate orchestration and development platforms that span core, edge,
and physical devices will eventually emerge, but we don’t expect the ecosystems for both physical and
virtual agents to converge for a number of years. It is going to take time for each of the environments to

## Investment theses in agentic AI infrastructure software


The central question for investors is how to approach investments in the agentic AI software market given
the structural trends outlined above. While the market is still early and evolving rapidly, we expect
significant growth over the coming years, driven by the shift from application-centric SaaS to agentcentric operating models. In this environment, growth-oriented investments are most likely to outperform.
Winning companies will be those that can move quickly, adapt to emerging standards, and establish
durable control points as the ecosystem takes shape.


Below we outline eight investment theses that, taken together, frame how private equity investors could
think about capital allocation in agentic AI software infrastructure.

### **Thesis 1: orchestration is the control point—and the primary platform** **bet**


Agent runtimes are likely to commoditize over time, driven by emerging standards (for example, MCP and
A2A), hyperscaler dominance, and enterprise demand for portability. Similarly, foundation models will
largely be owned by firms with the data, capital, and digital infrastructure required to train and operate
them at scale. In contrast, orchestration emerges as the durable control plane for agentic systems.


The orchestration layer will sit at the center of all agentic AI operations. At a minimum, it will define
security boundaries, enforce real-time cost controls, coordinate multi-agent behavior, manage agent and
MCP discovery, provide observability, and integrate governance hooks. Over time, orchestration is also
likely to become the context of record—the system that captures how decisions are made, how agents
interact, and how outcomes are produced. In this sense, orchestration plays a role analogous to cloud
orchestration in the cloud computing era.


Attractive investment targets include independent orchestration platforms that are not locked to a single
cloud, workflow-plus-orchestration hybrids with existing enterprise penetration, and developer-led
platforms that are moving up-market into governance and control. Significant value can be unlocked by

layering additional capabilities, such as governance, agentic FinOps, registries, and MCP integration, on

### **Thesis 2: agentic financial and commercial management is the** **largest greenfield opportunity**


Financial and commercial management represents the least mature yet most critical layer of the agentic
AI stack, particularly for agentic AI providers and enterprises seeking to manage rapidly growing AI costs.
Traditional CPQ, billing, and FinOps systems were not designed for autonomous actions, outcome-based
pricing, or highly dynamic cost structures driven by GPU and inference volatility. Nor were they built to
integrate tightly with agentic orchestration platforms.


This creates a large greenfield opportunity for AI-native financial infrastructure. Attractive targets include
AI-native CPQ platforms, usage- and outcome-based billing engines, and FinOps platforms that treat AI
inference as a first-class cost object. From a value-creation perspective, this is well suited to a platform
build strategy (for example, bundling CPQ, billing, and AI cost control into a unified offering and
expanding it through targeted tuck-in acquisitions).

### **Thesis 3: operations, governance, and risk will converge—back the** **converger**


There is a clear opportunity to build an integrated platform—potentially through a roll-up strategy—that
unifies operations, governance, and risk management for agentic systems. As agents move into
production and mission-critical workflows, enterprises will demand platforms that provide semantic
observability (in other words, understanding why decisions happened), continuous policy enforcement,
non-human identity governance, and real-time incident detection and response.


This market is currently in a land-grab phase, before standards fully harden, and buyers are already
signaling a strong preference for integrated platforms over fragmented point solutions. Initial acquisition
targets could include agent-native observability vendors, AI governance platforms expanding into
operations, and identity-centric security providers adapting their offerings for autonomous agents. The
strategic goal is to own the agentic control plane for safe, compliant, and scalable deployment.

### **Thesis 4: agent markets will become mandatory infrastructure**


At scale, managing hundreds or thousands of agents without centralized catalogs and registries becomes
operationally infeasible. Agent catalogs and registries are therefore likely to become mandatory
infrastructure, with strong analogies to API gateways, data catalogs, and service registries—categories that
historically exhibit high stickiness once embedded.


Attractive targets include governance-first agent registries, platforms extending data catalogs into AI and
agent domains, and developer portals evolving into enterprise-grade agent hubs. A compelling valuecreation strategy is to establish an anchor registry combined with governance, then expand into agent
evaluation, certification, and marketplace economics. Over time, these platforms could also embed agent
selection and evaluation services, further increasing lock-in and strategic relevance.

### **Thesis 5: MCP and integration layers are the new API economy**

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is emerging as a critical abstraction layer for agent-to-system
interaction, without fully replacing existing integration and decoupling mechanisms. This evolution closely
mirrors the early days of API gateways and iPaaS platforms. As agentic systems proliferate, enterprises will
require MCP platforms that provide governance, security, observability, and discovery at scale.


Attractive targets include enterprise MCP gateways, integration platforms that expose enterprise systems
as MCP servers, and tooling designed to govern MCP traffic across large agent fleets. Value creation lies in
positioning MCP as the enterprise integration control plane, building ecosystems and marketplaces
around it, and monetizing governance and control rather than commoditized connectors.

### **Thesis 6: the emergence of agentic AI infrastructure as a service**


We expect a new class of infrastructure-as-a-service providers—led by players such as Together.ai,
alongside peers including Anyscale, Fireworks AI, Replicate, and Modal—to increasingly subsume larger
portions of the agentic AI infrastructure stack and deliver them as integrated, managed services. These
platforms abstract away much of the complexity associated with model hosting, orchestration, scaling,
and performance optimization, allowing enterprises to consume agentic capabilities without directly
assembling and operating the full underlying stack.


This dynamic closely mirrors the evolution of cloud infrastructure. Today, more than 30 percent of
enterprise technology spend is allocated to cloud services, and an estimated 50 to 60 percent of

enterprise workloads run on cloud platforms. We believe agentic AI infrastructure is on a similar

trajectory, with a growing share of spend shifting toward “agentic AI infrastructure-as-a-service” offerings
as enterprises prioritize speed, flexibility, and operational simplicity over bespoke, self-managed
deployments.


The critical question for investors is whether independent start-ups such as Together.ai can sustain
durable competitive positions as the market matures, or whether these capabilities will ultimately be
absorbed by hyperscale cloud providers including Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud Platform, and
Microsoft Azure. While hyperscalers bring unmatched scale, distribution, and capital intensity, start-ups
may retain an edge through faster innovation cycles, tighter integration with open-source ecosystems,
and neutrality across models and clouds, making this a central axis of competitive tension and investment
risk over the next several years.

### **Thesis 7: agent workforce management platform**


The concept of agent workforce management could reshape the agentic AI software infrastructure stack.
At scale, enterprises will need the ability to define roles for agents, segment responsibilities across agent
populations, and, in some cases, allow agents to compete dynamically to determine which are best suited
for specific tasks or functions. Over time, these systems will also need to articulate, develop, and
continuously refine the skills required for agents to perform their assigned roles effectively.


Equally important, enterprises will require mechanisms for supervision and accountability. Agent
workforce management platforms will need to monitor agent behavior in real time, ensure agents are

### **Thesis 8: cross-portfolio–company agent factory and marketplace**


Every private equity firm today oversees a portfolio of companies, virtually all of which stand to benefit
from the application of agentic AI. Yet building agentic capabilities from scratch at the portfolio-company
level is both costly and inefficient. Foundational investments in agentic AI infrastructure require highly
specialized technical talent that is scarce, expensive, and difficult to recruit, while the time required for
teams to experiment, learn, and operationalize these systems represents a meaningful opportunity cost.


This creates a compelling opportunity to centralize capabilities and capture real synergies across the
portfolio. By pooling agentic talent, reusable agents, shared tooling, and accumulated learning, firms can
dramatically reduce duplication of effort while accelerating time to value for individual portfolio
companies. Over time, agents themselves become reusable assets—improving with scale and exposure
across use cases—rather than bespoke implementations rebuilt company by company.


One natural expression of this model is the creation of a shared services platform that develops, operates,
and commercializes agents on behalf of portfolio companies. Initially, such a platform would focus on
serving internal demand across the portfolio, delivering both cost leverage and faster deployment. Over
time, however, this shared capability could evolve into a standalone business, selling agentic solutions,
infrastructure, or managed agent services into the broader market. In doing so, what begins as an internal
efficiency play has the potential to become a differentiated platform investment in its own right.

## Additional investment considerations


There are also clear areas investors should approach with caution. Pure developer-tool point solutions are
difficult to scale, as developers are unlikely to converge on a single toolset. Model-dependent bets carry
significant platform and commoditization risk, and single-cloud–locked solutions may struggle as
enterprises prioritize portability and optionality.


An attractive long-term strategy is to assemble an integrated agentic stack over time: start with an
orchestration anchor, add governance and registries, layer in financial controls, expand through MCP and
integration capabilities, and ultimately extend into agentic market capabilities. This approach aligns capital
with the control points most likely to matter as the agentic AI ecosystem matures.
