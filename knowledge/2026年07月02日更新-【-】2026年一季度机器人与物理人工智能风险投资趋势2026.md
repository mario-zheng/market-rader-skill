**Key takeaways**


**Q1 2026 marked a structural break in robotics financing:** Robotics & physical AI startups raised $16.3

billion across 492 deals, the strongest single quarter on record by both measures, up 154% QoQ and

256% YoY. Three rounds (Shield AI at $1.5 billion, Saronic at $1.8 billion, Neura Robotics at $1.8 billion)

account for roughly $5 billion of the value total. Strip those and the underlying quarter is approximately

$11.3 billion, still the highest ex-outlier figure in the dataset. However, the break is not the headline

figure. It is that three independent shifts moved in the same direction at once: The US military

institutionalized permanent marketplace procurement, the software layer repriced through market
clearing transactions, and the exit market opened for the first time.


**The diligence filter tightened around two questions:** scale and stickiness: For most of the 2021 to

2024 cycle, the question was whether the technology works. Q1 added a second test weighted about

as heavily: Can the company build at volume, and does it hold a paying customer? RaaS contracts

and repeat commercial orders are the signal. Viral demonstrations are not. Agility Robotics’ Digit

cleared more than 100,000 totes at GXO under a multiyear robotics-as-a-service contract, the clearest

commercial deployment signal in the humanoid category globally, and Waymo reached 3,067 vehicles

and approximately 500,000 paid rides per week across 10 cities. Against those benchmarks, more than

70% of Unitree’s 2025 revenue came from research and education clients rather than factory operators,

and NVIDIA acknowledges that its DreamZero model is not yet reliably deployable.


**The robotics software layer repriced in a single quarter:** Skild AI raised $1.4 billion at a $14 billion

post-money valuation, backed by SoftBank and NVIDIA. Physical Intelligence was reported in

active talks above $11 billion, roughly double its Q4 2025 post-money valuation in four months.



AI & autonomy platforms reached $5.3 billion TTM, up 431% YoY. The mechanism is integration

cost. Hardware capability is no longer the binding constraint in deployment. Integration is and

software that compresses it captures the margin. SoftBank paying approximately 2.3x revenue

for ABB’s robotics hardware division in the same period codified the inverse: Intelligence captures

margin, hardware commoditizes.


**US defense procurement moved from policy to infrastructure, but the budget headline did not follow:**

In the span of a single week in late March, the Army launched an “Amazon-style” UAS Marketplace on

March 24, opening with roughly 30 vetted systems and scaling to 70 or more. The Navy replaced its

cancelled MASC program with a production-ready MUSV Marketplace on March 26, advancing seven

vendors to prototyping against approximately $2.1 billion in One Big Beautiful Bill Act funding. These

mechanisms convert the traditional procurement valley of death into a recurring revenue pathway for

vendors with domestic supply chains and demonstrated production lead times. They are operational

today. The proposed $54.6 billion fiscal year 2027 autonomy budget is not. Roughly 98% depends on

a reconciliation bill that may not pass, and modeling returns on it without weighting enactment risk

misprices the category.


**Industrial robotics is on track to overtake defense as the top segment by capital deployed:** Defense

& security and industrial together captured roughly 62% of Q1 capital, and both now exceed $10 billion

TTM. Defense attracted $4.5 billion in Q1 across 101 deals, reaching $11.9 billion TTM, up 48% versus

the prior TTM period. Industrial reached $5.6 billion across 118 deals, $10.8 billion TTM, up 83%,

now within 10% of defense and growing faster on both value and count. If those growth differentials

hold, industrial will displace defense in two to three quarters, carried by assembly & manufacturing

robots at $7.8 billion TTM, up 385% YoY.

**China leads on volume, the West on deployment proof:** Asia represented 54% of Q1 deal count but only

about 25% of deal value, an average deal size near $15.3 million against $72 million in North America.

AgiBot shipped its 10,000th embodied robot in the quarter, and Unitree filed for a STAR Market

IPO targeting roughly $616 million with committed annual capacity of 75,000 humanoids. Western

companies show deeper enterprise pull: Agility at GXO; Apptronik piloting with Mercedes-Benz, GXO,

and Jabil; and Figure AI at a $39 billion post-money valuation, the highest in the dataset. Which model

captures long-term value is the central unresolved question for industrial robotics in 2026.


**The exit market opened, and the broadening buyer base matters more than the headline value:**

Exit value reached $6.2 billion in Q1 2026 alone, more than the cumulative total of the preceding

five years combined. Six public listings drove $5.3 billion, led by Shenzhen Edge Medical ($2 billion,

surgical robotics), Huayan Robotics ($979 million, assembly and manufacturing), Galaxis Technology

($833 million, defense UAS), and Merlin Labs ($800 million, defense UAS). The more durable

signal is who is buying. The acquirer pool has moved well beyond defense primes into automotive

(Mobileye), e-commerce (Amazon), warehouse automation (Symbotic), and infrastructure software

(Bentley Systems). That broadening field of potential acquirers is the most consequential exit

development of the quarter.


**Venture-growth capital matched later-stage VC for the first time, signaling a pre-IPO cohort:** Each

stage reached $5.9 billion in Q1 2026, and together they deployed 73% of total Q1 value, against $3

billion combined in Q4 2025. The cohort behind the shift (Shield AI, Saronic, Apptronik, Zipline, and

Neura Robotics) has crossed from R&D into production, holds government or commercial contracts,

and is positioning for public markets. Venture-growth median pre-money valuation reached $363

million in Q1, up from $239 million for full-year 2025.



**Logistics & warehousing reversed two years of decline, but one quarter is not a trend:** The segment

returned to 70% TTM-over-TTM growth on the strength of last-mile delivery, which reached $1.3

billion TTM, up 140% YoY, while autonomous mobile robots and automated guided vehicles remain

in value decline. Amazon acquired RIVR Technologies, and Symbotic acquired Fox Robotics in the

same quarter, an early sign of strategic consolidation. The Federal Aviation Administration’s targeted

Part 108 BVLOS final rule is the regulatory unlock that could extend the recovery into inspection and

commercial delivery through the second half of 2026 and into 2027.

deals—a record count, reversing the formation-stage compression that characterized 2023 and 2024.

Early-stage VC delivered $4 billion across 181 deals. Late-stage VC and venture growth each reached

$5.9 billion, the first time the two stages have matched in the dataset. Together they deployed $11.8

known tasks and software that may generalize across unknown ones, and the pricing gap between the

two categories is widening.


**Physical AI themes**


**The software layer repriced:** The clearest Q1 2026 valuation signal was not one round but two

SoftBank transactions. SoftBank paid roughly 2.3x revenue for ABB’s robotics hardware division—$5.4

billion for a business generating $2.3 billion in 2024 revenue—then co-led a round valuing Skild AI,

a software-only platform with estimated revenue of about $30 million, at $14 billion. Hardware was

priced like an industrial asset. Software was priced like an option on the future of robotics. The gap is

increasingly visible in market pricing.

That repricing extended beyond Skild. Physical Intelligence was in reported talks to raise roughly $1

billion at a valuation above $11 billion—nearly double its $5.6 billion post-money valuation from four

months earlier. AI & autonomy platforms reached $5.3 billion in TTM deal value, up 431% YoY. Robot OS

& middleware reached $523 million TTM, up 328%. The full robotics software stack is being revalued.


The technology is not solved, though. NVIDIA’s DreamZero World Action Model, published on arXiv in

February 2026, posted more than 2x improvement in generalization to new tasks and environments

compared to state-of-the-art vision-language-action models in real robot experiments. [1] NVIDIA’s

own GEAR Lab lead described it as not yet GPT-3 reliable. Skild AI’s president has framed the core

bottleneck as a data problem: There is no internet of robotics. Path planning & navigation rose just 7%

in TTM deal value despite 31% deal-count growth, pointing to commoditization in parts of the stack.

The software layer has repriced. It has not yet been won.


**Orchestration and edge-AI gained validation:** NODA AI’s $25 million Series A carried more signal than

its size suggests. Booz Allen Ventures and Huntington Ingalls Industries participated alongside lead

investor Bessemer Venture Partners, with both defense services primes described in the press release

as strategic partners in delivery on Department of War customer milestones. NODA coordinates more

than 30 OEM platforms across air, surface, subsurface, and ground domains and was selected to lead

orchestration for a DoD multidomain collaborative autonomy program. [2]


Legion Intelligence’s Centurion launch addressed the adjacent gap: autonomy that holds when

connectivity fails. Centurion is an offline-first agentic edge-AI system rated IL2 through IL6, packaged

as integrated hardware, software, and sustainment. NODA and Legion point to the same thesis. Scaled

autonomy requires a cross-vendor cognitive layer that no single platform vendor will build alone.



**Manufacturing capacity became a diligence priority:** Anduril began Fury production at Arsenal-1

in late March, roughly three months ahead of the original July 2026 target. [3] At full three-shift

capacity the 22-station line can produce up to roughly 150 aircraft per year, per company officials

quoted by Breaking Defense. Production had started—not merely been announced. In this sector,

that distinction matters.


The same capacity lens ran through the quarter’s largest rounds. Figure has described BotQ as moving

toward an hourly robot production cadence. Saronic is building 300,000 square feet of fabrication

space and planning Port Alpha at 10x current capacity. [4] Neura Robotics expanded manufacturing

across India and Hangzhou while securing Schaeffler as an actuator supplier.


Material handling & palletizing attracted $1.4 billion in TTM deal value across 12 deals, up 674% on

zero-count growth. End effectors & tooling rose 1,035% YoY. Investors appear to be paying increasingly

for throughput, component reliability, and production readiness—not just prototypes.


**China leads on volume; the West leads on deployment proof:** China’s humanoid ramp is unmatched

on volume. AgiBot shipped its 10,000th embodied robot in Q1, scaling from 5,000 to 10,000 units

in roughly three months. [5] Unitree has filed for a STAR Market IPO targeting approximately $616

million, although the company remains privately held pending completion of the listing process, with

committed annual capacity of 75,000 humanoids and 115,000 quadrupeds. China’s humanoid robot

output is expected to grow by up to 94% in 2026, according to TrendForce.


That said, the customer mix is worth keeping in perspective. More than 70% of Unitree’s humanoid

revenue in the most recent filing period came from research and education buyers, not factory

operators. Security concerns around Unitree platforms could further limit adoption in certain markets. [6]

**Macro and industry backdrop**


**Labor scarcity and robot density are structural, not cyclical:** Global robot density reached 162 robots

per 10,000 employees in 2023, doubling over the prior seven years, per the International Federation

of Robotics’ (IFR’s) World Robotics 2024 report. [8] China ranks third globally at 470 robots per 10,000

workers; the US ranks tenth at 295. [9] The demand-side pressures are unlikely to reverse quickly. Japan

and South Korea face among the most severe demographic aging in the world. Germany’s working-age

population is projected to decline by roughly 4 million through 2035. In the US, interest in engineering

programs from international students dropped 40% as of early 2025, compressing the talent pipeline at

the moment deployment demand is expanding.



Commercial adoption data confirms the cycle is already running. Global professional service robot

sales rose 30% in 2023 to more than 205,000 units, according to the IFR. [10] Logistics robots drove more

than half that total—roughly 113,000 units—followed by hospitality at roughly 54,000 and agriculture

at roughly 20,000. Medical robot sales rose 36% YoY. These are deployed commercial systems,

not venture-backed startups, and they confirm that automation adoption is broad and accelerating

independent of the investment cycle.


The key point for investors is that this demand does not reset with the cycle. Demographic pressure

does not ease in a downturn. MIT research by Acemoglu and Restrepo found that countries with rapidly

aging populations invest disproportionately in manufacturing automation to sustain output regardless

of macro conditions. This makes the category less dependent on the venture cycle alone.


**Sovereign programs are channeling noncyclical capital into hardware:** Governments are treating

robotics hardware components—sensors, actuators, power systems—as strategic assets. China’s

Made in China 2025 initiative channels capital into intelligent robotics with explicit domestic
production targets. Japan, South Korea, Germany, and the EU continue to support robotics through

national and regional industrial-policy programs spanning sensors, autonomous mobility, and secure

local component ecosystems.


Hardware and component startups in lidar, 3D vision, tactile sensors, and high-precision motion

systems may benefit from less cyclical sovereign demand. DJI restrictions and National Defense

Authorization Act (NDAA) supply chain rules are strengthening the defensibility of domestic and allied

drone and component suppliers. Reshoring is changing the unit economics of hardware startups with

domestic production. Late-stage capital is flowing to platforms and integrators with strong upstream

partnerships because sovereign investment may de-risk their scaling path.

**RaaS is expanding the addressable market beyond enterprise buyers:** Traditional robotic deployments

cost $100,000 to $500,000 upfront, locking out most small and mid-size businesses (SMBs). RaaS

converts that capital expenditure into an operating expenditure. Collaborative robots priced at roughly

$10,000 to $20,000 paired with subscription or leasing structures are pulling in SMB buyers across

floor-cleaning, service robotics, and warehouse automation. Agility’s GXO contract is the most visible

enterprise-scale RaaS deployment, but the model’s larger impact on the investment landscape is at the

SMB tier, where it opens deal flow in categories previously inaccessible to startups.


Integration cost amplifies the opportunity. Integration can represent a large share of total robotics

deployment cost, making software-led deployment tooling a key value lever. AI-driven integration

tools—trained via simulation and human-video pretraining rather than manual programming—are

projected to cut those costs materially. The binding constraint in robotics deployment is not hardware

capability. It is integration cost. That is the structural tailwind behind the software-layer repricing.


**Regulation is unlocking some markets and constraining others:** The FAA’s proposed Part 108 BVLOS

rule—targeted for approximately mid-March 2026 after a 43-day government shutdown delayed

publication—would convert per-flight waivers into a scalable performance-based framework with

Remote ID, detect-and-avoid requirements, and Part 146 Automated Data Service Provider certification.

For logistics, inspection, and commercial delivery operators, this would convert point deployments

into scalable networks. Implementation would lag publication by six to 12 months, so the investment

impact would land in H2 2026 and 2027 data. The regulatory unlock is in motion, but timing remains

dependent on final-rule publication and implementation.



ISO 10218, the core robotics safety standard, is expanding from 115 pages to roughly 400 to cover

autonomous mobile robots and humanoids. Legacy standards were designed for caged industrial

robots. Companies that participate in standards-setting can design to the standard from the start

rather than retrofit compliance—a growing strategic priority for startups and incumbents alike.


In Europe, the European Union Aviation Safety Agency is advancing U-space and SORA 2.5 with AI

risk modules. Civil-drone regulatory harmonization is progressing. The gap is on the defense side:

Eurocontrol and industry observers flag that Europe still lacks an integrated counter-UAS security

architecture as incursions over airports and military sites increase. In China, HEIS 2026 establishes the

first national humanoid and embodied intelligence standard system—the same state standards-and
policy scaffold Beijing used in EVs, solar, and 5G, now applied to robotics.


**Cybersecurity and governance risks are underpriced:** Mobile and humanoid robots are cyber
physical systems. Insecure sensors, wifi protocols, and cloud integrations create attack surfaces

that legacy IT security frameworks were not built to handle. In hospitals, exploitation could expose

patient data. In factories, compromised systems become vectors for operational disruption. Security

researchers have flagged data-collection and backdoor concerns in Unitree platforms—a risk that

could restrict allied government procurement and narrow the addressable market in ways unit-volume

projections do not capture.


Reveal Technology’s acquisition of Anomaly Six adds governance and privacy risk exposure, combining

tactical mapping capabilities with location-intelligence capabilities that have previously drawn scrutiny.

That profile could affect allied adoption and draw regulatory attention that current deal models

may not have fully priced in.

**Deployment and demand signals**


**Defense procurement infrastructure became permanent:** The most consequential structural change

of the quarter was not a contract award but the institutionalization of standing procurement channels.


The Army launched its UAS Marketplace on March 24, opening with roughly 30 vetted Group 1 through

Group 3 systems via Basic Ordering Agreements and a perpetual Commercial Solutions Opening,

scalable to 70-plus systems and hundreds of companies over two years. Every vendor screens for

NDAA and Blue UAS compliance and demonstrated production lead times. Program manager Col.

Danielle Medaglia called it “a fundamental pivot in how we execute acquisition for uncrewed aircraft

systems.” [11] The model is explicitly based on Ukraine’s DOT-Chain Defence platform, which had

delivered 485,000 UAVs and other equipment through the first months of 2026 through a marketplace

listing nearly 800 items from more than 200 Ukrainian manufacturers—with a Ministry of Defense

target of routing 70% of all drone purchases through the platform. In the first 3.5 months of 2026 the

system moved UAH 22.9 billion worth of equipment, compared with UAH 10.5 billion in the first six

months of 2025. That compression rate is what the Army is importing.


Two days after the UAS Marketplace launch, the Navy replaced the cancelled MASC program with a

production-ready MUSV Marketplace funded by approximately $2.1 billion under the 2025 reconciliation

law, commonly referred to as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. No prototyping phase—vendors must



deliver 2,500 nm-range vessels with at-sea tests by the end of fiscal year 2026 and deliveries in fiscal

year 2027. PEO RAS Rebecca Gassler confirmed the construct will be reused across the complete

family of all unmanned surface vessels.


The DAWG’s proposed $54.6 billion fiscal year 2027 autonomy R&D request—up from $225.9 million

in fiscal year 2026 and not yet appropriated, with roughly $53.6 billion dependent on a reconciliation

bill—is a demand signal, not a bankable number. The marketplace mechanisms are operational today.

The budget headline is not.


Darkhive shows what the funded pipeline looks like when it works: a $49.7 million APFIT award

for Real-Time C2 at the Tactical Edge, immediately followed by a $30 million Series B led by RTX

Ventures. NDAA-compliant, production-proven, prime-backed. That sequencing is what the Army

marketplace is designed to replicate at scale.

now encompassing more than 300 ground-robot companies, is the largest live-fire autonomous

systems development environment in the world. In Q1 the country committed to 25,000 UGVs

commitment is the DOT-Chain platform described above.

Automotive-adjacent autonomy companies are buying humanoid capability rather than building it.

Serve Robotics acquired Diligent Robotics, maker of the Moxi hospital delivery robot, for $29 million in

stock plus a potential earn-out, a cross-segment move worth watching as a sidewalk delivery company

extends into indoor mobile manipulation on a shared autonomy stack. The rest were undisclosed-price

tuck-ins: RIVR Technologies to Amazon, Fox Robotics to Symbotic, Roboteam to Ondas, Canvas to

JLG Industries, Pointivo to Bentley Systems, MAVRIK to HavocAI, SEQSENSE to Iris Ohyama, Scythe

Robotics to Autonomous Solutions, and Zelostech to Cainiao. ARLANE and Dessintey took Axinesis

private. Amazon and Symbotic each acquired warehouse or last-mile assets in the same quarter, which

reads as deliberate platform-building.


Acquisition volume has climbed sharply. The sector recorded six transactions in 2024 and 23 in 2025,

the highest annual count on record at $54.4 million in disclosed value. Q1 2026 added 14 transactions

at $946 million in disclosed value, almost all of it Mentee. Most other deals carried no disclosed

price, so count tracks the trend better than value, and the trend is up. The more durable shift is in

who is buying. The acquirer pool has moved well beyond defense primes to automotive (Mobileye),

e-commerce (Amazon), warehouse automation (Symbotic), consumer robotics (Autonomous



Solutions), and infrastructure software (Bentley Systems), each folding autonomy, perception, and

automation into an existing platform rather than building from scratch. That broadening buyer base is

the most consequential exit development of the quarter. It widens the field of potential acquirers well

beyond the handful of defense and industrial names that historically defined the exit landscape.


**Conclusions**


**Q1 2026 was a structural break, not a continuation:** Three shifts moved in the same direction in a

single quarter, and each would have mattered on its own. The US military institutionalized permanent

marketplace procurement for autonomous systems in the span of a week, turning a persistent valley
of-death problem into a credible recurring revenue path. The software layer repriced through market
clearing transactions: Skild AI at $14 billion, Physical Intelligence in reported talks above $11 billion,

and SoftBank’s roughly 2.3x revenue payment for ABB’s hardware division in the same window, which

fixed how value is splitting between software and hardware. And the exit market opened for the first

time, with $6.2 billion in Q1 against roughly $0.6 billion across the prior five years combined, including

six public listings that set the sector’s first real IPO benchmarks. None of this showed up in the prior

[quarterly data. The Q4 2025 report](https://pitchbook.brightspotcdn.com/b2/d5/5e5e56924c3b88be9d5dc2156e63/q4-2025-robotics-physical-ai-vc-trends.pdf) called the sector “moving decisively from experimentation toward

large-scale deployment.” Q1 2026 is that movement reaching the capital markets.


The diligence filter has tightened around two questions now, not one. For most of the 2021 to

2024 cycle the question was whether the technology works. Q1 points to a second filter weighted

about equally beside it: Can the company build at volume, and does it have a paying customer? The

largest Q1 rounds—Shield AI, Saronic, Apptronik, Neura Robotics, Hadrian, Zipline—answer both.

They have crossed from R&D into production and hold government or commercial contracts. The

companies that have not—most humanoid platforms outside Agility, most early-stage UAS names

outside the marketplace-vetted cohort—are raising at earlier stages and smaller checks, or not at all.

The barbell is widening.


Three things remain unresolved. Humanoid commercialization is the first. 2026 is the year that starts

separating companies with durable factory revenue from those running staged demonstrations on

research-client income. Agility’s GXO benchmark is set, and the open question is how many others

meet it by year-end. Those that cannot will face a harder fundraising environment in 2027. The DAWG

budget is the second. The $54.6 billion fiscal year 2027 request is an ambition, not an appropriation,

and roughly 98% depends on a reconciliation bill that may not pass. The marketplace mechanisms

are bankable today. The budget headline is not. Investors who model returns on $54.6 billion without

weighting enactment risk are mispricing defense robotics. The logistics reversal is the third. One

quarter of growth after two years of decline is not a trend. Last-mile delivery is carrying the recovery

while AMRs and AGVs keep declining in YoY value. The FAA BVLOS rule should add a tailwind in H2

2026, but the recovery needs another quarter to confirm.


**Segment outlook:** Three segments are accelerating. Defense & security has operational procurement

rails and structural demand across UAS ($9 billion TTM) and maritime ($2.1 billion TTM), with the

unappropriated DAWG budget the main risk. Industrial robotics is close behind, led by assembly

and manufacturing at $7.8 billion TTM, up 385% YoY, with deployment proof expanding, although

the humanoid demo-to-deployment gap and the China-volume-versus-West-revenue question hang

over it. Robotics software & AI completes the group: AI autonomy platforms at $5.3 billion TTM, up

431%, with orchestration validated at the prime level, held back mainly by generalization that is not

yet GPT-3 reliable.



Two segments are stable. Medical and healthcare holds on surgical robotics ($1 billion TTM, up 64%)

and diagnostic imaging ($322 million, up 362%), although hospital logistics stays thin and quarterly

value is flat YoY. Robotics hardware & components is steady on end effectors ($430 million TTM, up

1,035%) and sensors and vision ($1.3 billion TTM), but actuators are declining in value even as deal

count doubles, and power systems are commoditizing.


Two segments are on watch. Logistics & warehousing saw last-mile delivery ($1.3 billion TTM, up

140%) reverse the segment decline with the BVLOS unlock incoming, but AMRs and AGVs are still

contracting. Consumer robotics shows strong subsegment growth in companion & social ($1.2

billion TTM, up 226%) and entertainment ($344 million, up 1,148%), offset by a 56% TTM-over-TTM

decline that creates unfavorable optics and read-through risk from Unitree’s IPO pricing. Agriculture

& food is mixed: Food service ($219 million TTM, up 303%) and crop monitoring are recovering while

autonomous farming vehicles and food processing contract against a structurally constrained market.


**What to watch in Q2 2026:** On the investment data, the central question is whether deal value holds

above $8 billion or normalizes toward $6 billion. The $16.3 billion Q1 figure rode three rounds totaling

$5 billion; strip those and the quarter is $11.3 billion, which puts the structural run rate at $8 billion to

$11 billion. Below $6 billion signals a real pullback. Above $12 billion without identified mega-rounds

means the valuation baseline has reset higher. Three stage-level signals sit alongside it: whether

venture growth holds above $4 billion and stays level with late-stage VC after the first-ever tie at $5.9

billion each, whether seed count holds above 90 after the record 113 in Q1, and whether logistics posts

a second straight quarter of growth. It will be interesting to see whether industrial robotics, now within

10% of defense on a TTM basis ($10.8 billion versus $11.9 billion) and growing faster, takes the top

segment spot by Q3 or Q4.
