Executive Summary

### _Executive_ _Summary_




Executive Summary


Sector-specific data collected from seven water
institutions in the region show how childcare
responsibilities intersect with gender dynamics,

staff morale, and workplace outcomes, with direct
implications for employee performance and
institutional resilience. Yet, formal support is almost
absent, with few of the participating institutions
offering on-site childcare, lactation rooms, or
flexible work options. These gaps translate into
potential productivity losses and weakened staff
morale, highlighting the opportunity for targeted
employer action. While childcare has traditionally
been assigned primarily to women, this study helps
confirm that this issue does not exclusively affect
mothers. The burden of stress and anxiety related
to childcare is experienced by fathers as well, and
inclusive solutions are required for all.


IX


Executive Summary


Why Childcare Matters


Childcare support addresses multiple objectives at
once, generating benefits that extend far beyond
individual employees:


**Improved institutional performance and resilience**


Reliable childcare reduces absenteeism, turnover,
and recruitment costs. In the water sector, this
translates directly into operational reliability: when
key staff—such as technicians, plant operators,
or emergency response teams—are absent
or distracted by caregiving pressures, service
continuity and crisis response capacity suffer.
Conversely, stable childcare arrangements enable
faster recovery from disruptions and sustained
delivery of water and sanitation services.


**Strengthened inclusive participation, workforce**
**diversity, and gender equity**


In a sector where women represent only 24 percent
of the workforce, childcare support reduces a core
barrier to women’s entry to and retention in the
water workforce. For women workers in particular,
childcare support can be the difference between
career advancement and professional stagnation.


**Delivery of multigenerational benefits**


Beyond workplace and institution-level outcomes,
childcare supports children’s early development,
making it both a workforce investment and a
human capital investment.



Current Gaps and
Impacts on Employees


Despite clear benefits, employer-supported
childcare remains limited. Key insights emerging
from the survey of employee parents and interviews
with key informants in the seven targeted water
sector institutions include the following.


**Effect on workforce performance**


Six in ten employees who responded to the survey
reported that childcare demands disrupted their
work, affecting focus, schedules, productivity, or
career growth due to insufficient or inadequate
childcare arrangements. The impact was especially
pronounced for women, who were nearly three times
more likely than men to consider leaving their job
because of childcare demands, pointing to heightened attrition risks in a sector where women are
already underrepresented.


**Impacts on both mothers and fathers**


Childcare burdens affected both mothers and
fathers, signaling a generational shift towards more
involvement of fathers in child raising, with implications for workplace productivity as parents attempt
to balance work–life priorities.


**Reliance on informal arrangements**


Nearly 40 percent of employees relied on relatives
for childcare, an arrangement that is often unstable
and collapses when older caregivers become
unavailable.


**Greater impacts for some employees**



About one in five parents of children with disabilities
reported considering unpaid leave, and 20 percent of
parents who reported being single, divorced, separated or widowed (this group may or may not share
caregiving responsibilities with a partner), said they
had forgone advancement opportunities—highlighting the disproportionate challenges they face.


X


**Lack of institutional childcare modalities**


Interviews with managers and human resources
representatives revealed an absence of guidelines
and policies to support childcare solutions for
employees.


**Desire for actionable solutions**


Preferred interventions highlighted by employees
included on site childcare centers, flexible
schedules, financial subsidies, and after-hours
and back-up childcare services.





This study is the first of its kind in Latin America
and the Caribbean’s water sector to collect
primary data from water employees with childcare
responsibilities, as well as from managers, human
resource staff, and civil society representatives.
Funded by Invest in Childcare through the World
Bank’s Early Learning Partnership Multi-Donor
Trust Fund, the study gathered quantitative and
qualitative evidence from seven water institutions
across the Dominican Republic, Mexico, and Peru
over a 10-month period. These three countries
were selected based on their engagement with
the World Bank through water sector operations,
ongoing technical assistance, and strong interest
in advancing gender-inclusive policies at the
institutional level.

Implementing Childcare Solutions


The report outlines a menu of 11 actionable
childcare solutions that employers can adopt to
support their workforce. Each option draws on
international good practices and on findings from
detailed assessments across participating water
institutions. The menu of solutions is not meant
to prescribe a fixed sequence or priority. The
relevance and feasibility of each option will depend
on institutional capacity, workforce needs, and the
national context. Some institutions may already
have certain measures in place, while others may
face cultural or regulatory factors that shape what
is possible. It is important to note that no institution
should implement all options, and each involves
costs and trade-offs to consider. To reflect this
diversity, the menu of options are grouped into four
complementary areas (a longer description and a
corresponding matrix are included in Chapter 4):



**A** **Institutional and policy foundations**

Creating an enabling environment for family-friendly
workplaces and childcare support starts with robust
institutional and policy measures. These actions
formalize commitments, clarify responsibilities,
and establish mechanisms for ongoing evaluation
and improvement. Such measures also build trust
and signal that the work–life balance is a shared
priority, turning support for families into a core part
of organizational identity. Options include:


**1.**
**Needs assessment.** Conduct an institutional assessment of employees’ childcare needs, existing
support measures, and legal frameworks
to identify priorities and feasible actions.

**Institutional policy framework.** Develop an

**3.**

**Support for children with disabilities.** Offer financial support and tailored policies for families with
children with disabilities, including stipends, special

and equitable.

An inclusive workplace goes beyond policy—it
fosters belonging and care. These measures foster
a work environment where employees can balance

promote inclusivity, productivity, and well-being

normalizing coresponsibility in parenting and care.
Options include:



**6.**
**Flexible work arrangements.** Implement flexible

hours, makeup days, and compressed workweeks—
to help employees balance family responsibilities
and reduce stress.actions.


**7.**
**Lactation support measures.** Provide lactation support through private, equipped nursing spaces and
flexible leave policies for breastfeeding mothers.


**8.**
**Training and resources.** Provide parenting workshops and wellness and mentoring support, and
share information on public childcare programs
through accessible channels.


**D** **Access to direct childcare services**

Reliable childcare services are often the most
visible forms of support for working parents. These
measures provide employees with access to safe,
high-quality childcare through partnerships with
local providers or in house services. They are designed to address the practical challenges faced
by working parents during and beyond standard
school hours. Options include:


**9.**
**On-site childcare facilities.** Establish an onsite childcare center or partner with accredited
providers, ensuring compliance with quality
standards and allowing phased implementation
or outsourcing for flexibility.


**10.**
**Childcare services through partnerships.** Partner
with nearby accredited childcare providers to secure safe, high-quality care aligned with employees’
work schedules.


**11.**
**School break and backup care programs.**
Provide childcare programs during school breaks
and closures, or partner with existing services,
offering subsidies when external options are used.

Introduction

### _Introduction_




Introduction


This chapter presents the background, objectives,
and methodology of a study on childcare needs
in the water sector across three countries in Latin
America and the Caribbean, while making the case
that employer-supported childcare is a value-driven
investment that can strengthen workforce inclusion,
institutional performance, and gender equity
across the region.


Background and Motivation



Globally, on average, women make up less than 21
percent of the workforce in water utilities. For the
Latin America and the Caribbean region, the figure
is only marginally higher (24 percent) (World Bank
2026). While women are increasingly represented
in the water workforce, persistent barriers continue
to hinder their full participation and advancement.
Among these, the lack of childcare support—
including childcare facilities and lactation rooms—
is a key structural and often overlooked constraint
limiting women’s ability to enter, remain in, and
progress in the sector. Childcare-related logistics
can negatively affect employees—both men and
women—diminishing their optimal productivity
(IFC 2019b).


While World Bank-financed projects aim to
promote female participation in water institutions,
employer-supported childcare alternatives
have seldom been explored as transformative
investments. Investing in childcare solutions that
foster greater female participation represents
a high-impact opportunity for the water sector,
as evidence shows that gender-diverse water
institutions consistently outperform their less
inclusive counterparts (World Bank 2019).



While employers may decide to comply with
baseline legislation regarding parental benefits,
the unavailability or unaffordability of childcare
affects the choices that parents—especially
women—make about work, including whether to
stay at home or how to balance employment with
caregiving responsibilities.

Introduction



Benefits of Investing
in Childcare Solutions


Investing in childcare solutions in the workplace,
including in the water sector, has the potential to
generate wide-ranging benefits for the economy,
for workforce participation, for social values such
as equity and inclusiveness, for child development, for productivity and service delivery, for the
financial performance and reputation of enterprises, and for sectoral innovation. The following five
subsections explore these aspects in more detail.


**Wider economic and employment benefits**



There are multiple benefits associated with childcare in the workplace. The 2024–2030 World Bank
Group’s Gender Strategy emphasizes childcare as
a critical enabler of gender equality and economic
development (World Bank 2024). Childcare boosts
labor force participation (Devercelli and Beaton-Day
2020; Halim, Perova and Reynolds 2021). When
affordable and reliable care is available, more
mothers enter and stay in the workforce, parents
can work longer or more regular hours, and women
in particular are less likely to drop out after childbirth (IFC 2017). Investing in childcare helps reduce
the unpaid care burden that disproportionately falls
on women (ILO and ADB 2023). Childcare drives



**Social, developmental and equity benefits**


Childcare investment yields multigenerational
impacts by boosting human capital formation and
helping reduce inequality (Ahmed et al. 2023). Quality early childhood care supports cognitive, language,
emotional, and social development, laying the foundation for better long-term educational outcomes. It
is especially transformative for low-income families:
reliable childcare helps break intergenerational
cycles of poverty by enabling parents to take up
stable jobs while giving their children a fair start in
life through early learning and socialization opportunities. Workplace childcare facilities may attract
female job seekers and create role models, inspiring
girls to pursue science, technology, engineering, and
mathematics education or enroll in technical and
vocational programs, ultimately increasing women’s
participation in the workforce (World Bank 2019).
Inclusive childcare for children with disabilities is
particularly important in helping mothers of children
with disabilities overcome the significant barriers to
labor market participation that they face, including
childcare fees, transport costs, provider unwillingness to accept their children, poor service quality,
and staff often lacking the skills or resources to support children with disabilities (World Bank 2025).



Childcare support also strengthens social equity when applied through transparent governance
arrangements that prevent gender-biased career
penalties and ensure fair access for low-income staff
and those raising children with disabilities. Moreover,
childcare support helps attract more and better
talent, especially women, and improves employee
relations. Thus, not investing in childcare solutions
becomes a missed opportunity for businesses and
for society as a whole.

2


Although the developmental benefits of childcare are
generally positive—such as gains in school readiness,
language, and socioemotional development, especially
for children ages 3–5 years—findings for children under
2 years of age are more mixed and are highly sensitive to the quality of, time allocated to, and intensity of
childcare, and family context. For example, evaluations
of Quebec’s universal childcare expansion found small
adverse noncognitive effects (including behavioral) in
the short run, with some impacts persisting into later
childhood (Baker, Gruber, and Milligan 2019). These results underscore that quality and design drive outcomes
for children—and therefore the returns to investment.


**Operational gains and improved workplace culture**


Investing in childcare solutions is not only a social good
but is also a sound business investment. Evidence
demonstrates that companies providing childcare support realize tangible benefits: greater female participation in the labor force, lower attrition and absenteeism,
higher employee focus and productivity, and reduced
recruitment and training costs, leading to greater overall
efficiency. Given the water sector’s reliance on shiftbased operations, emergency response, and fieldwork,
access to reliable childcare strengthens workforce stability and ensures uninterrupted service delivery. A study
by the International Finance Corporation of 10 companies across industries worldwide demonstrates the
benefits of offering childcare-related support, including
improved quality of job candidates, reduced staff turnover, faster vacancy fill rates, lower workplace accident
rates, a more gender-diverse workforce, and enhanced
reputation as “employers of choice” (IFC 2017).



Introduction


By helping employees—particularly women—stay
and advance in their professions, childcare support improves job quality and aligns with the World
Bank’s agenda to support more and better jobs.
Childcare support also strengthens employee satisfaction and workplace culture. Workers feel valued
when their employers recognize and support their
caregiving responsibilities. This translates into higher
engagement, better collaboration, greater scope
for professional growth, firmer career commitment,
and a more positive workplace culture (Shellenback
2004; ILO and UNICEF 2021; Kos et al. 2024).


**Financial and reputational gains**


Beyond operational gains, investing in childcare
makes strong financial sense. Recent research from
five United States companies suggests that childcare investments yield returns between 90 and 425
percent, largely due to reduced turnover and fewer
missed workdays (Kos et al. 2024). Turnover of experienced workers is costly, especially in industries
with hard-to-fill roles. Previous studies estimate that
employee attrition can cost employers around 16–20
percent of an employee’s annual salary, and up to
twice that amount for senior level positions (Devercelli and Beaton-Day 2020). These preventable costs
underscore why employer-supported childcare is
not just a benefit, but a strategic investment. In fact,
evidence shows that even retaining just 1 percent of
eligible employees can offset the cost of providing
childcare benefits (Kos et al. 2024).


By investing in childcare solutions, companies also
position themselves as forward-thinking, socially responsible employers. Women and caregivers—who
often face barriers to full-time employment—are
more likely to thrive in environments that offer family-friendly policies. Offering childcare support sends
a clear message that parenting is a shared responsibility between mothers and fathers, helping to break
down traditional beliefs and stigmas, and enhancing
corporate reputation (IFC 2019a).

Introduction



**Sectoral gains through industry innovation**


While childcare is ultimately a cross-sectoral and
public policy issue, government-speared interventions often take time to implement and deliver
impact. In the meantime, sectoral institutions—such
as water utilities—can act proactively, driving innovation by incorporating childcare support into their
overall compensation strategies to enhance business
outcomes (IFC 2017). Beyond increasing individual productivity, childcare benefit thus becomes
a value-driven investment and sustainable business strategy with potential spillover effects on the
organization as a whole, other employers, and other
male-dominated industries. Well-aligned sectoral
initiatives can complement national policies and
help accelerate implementation. This integrated approach—whereby national frameworks provide the
foundation and sectoral actors contribute innovation—offers the most effective path toward sustainable childcare solutions.

Study Objectives
and Target Audience


Against this backdrop, the objectives of this report
are to examine how childcare responsibilities affect
employees’ experiences and preferences in the
water sector, and to identify practical solutions to
strengthen workforce inclusion, well-being and
institutional performance. The study draws on
evidence from seven water institutions in three
countries of Latin America and the Caribbean
to analyze how insufficient childcare support
undermines productivity and equity. The findings
aim to guide decision-makers and practitioners in
the water sector—including managers of utilities,
public water agencies, and related institutions—in
developing actionable, evidence-based responses
that benefit both employees and employers.
The report also serves as a reference for actors
engaged in workforce inclusion and institutional
development across infrastructure sectors. By
framing childcare as both a workforce and an
institutional issue, it seeks to inform action at the
organizational level while contributing to a broader
understanding of how sector-based approaches
can complement national childcare systems.


The report is organized to guide the reader from
evidence to action. Chapter 2 looks at how
childcare affects employees’ well-being and
performance, and why it matters for employers.
It then turns to the current situation in the
participating water institutions (chapter 3), showing
how parents are managing childcare today and
what barriers they face. Building on this, the report
highlights, in chapter 4, examples of promising
initiatives and lays out 11 practical solutions that
employers can consider. It closes (chapter 5) with
key takeaways and implications for the water sector
and other industries facing similar challenges.


4


Methodology and
Research Scope


This report examines how inadequate childcare
arrangements influence employees’ experiences
at work, with implications for productivity and
professional growth in Latin America and the
Caribbean’s water sector. Using a mixed method
approach, the study analyzes the childcare

Childcare Leads to Better Job Performance


This chapter considers how childcare affects
employees’ well-being and performance, and why
it matters for employers. It uses the results of the
study of water institutions in the Latin America and
the Caribbean region, supported by results from
other studies, to demonstrate how increased focus
on childcare support could reap benefits for both
employees and employers.



Childcare support benefits are broadly
recognized, but not to the full extent


Employers in the study recognize the benefits of
providing childcare solutions for employee retention,
satisfaction, and productivity, but are not fully aware
of the extent of investment returns. Although some
human resources representatives and managers
express an understanding of these benefits, interview
findings reveal that most are not fully aware of the
many ways in which employer-supported childcare
would increase the institution’s productivity and operational efficiency. There is limited recognition of the
negative impacts and unforeseen costs of inaction.


Interviews with civil society representatives further
underscore the productivity gains of investing in
childcare. An anthropologist and communicator from
Peru, specializing in gender and paternity, observed
that many Peruvian companies limit themselves to
low-cost measures such as lactation rooms, while
avoiding more substantive investments in childcare
due to perceived costs. Yet, examples from Europe
show that initiatives such as flexible leave, dedicated
childcare facilities, and parental workshops promoting coresponsibility can foster better work–life balance and advance gender equity. Similarly, the United
Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) Mexico described
its initiative Empresas que Cuidan (Companies



That Care), which provides technical assistance to
companies to adopt family-friendly policies, ranging
from extended parental leave and flexible schedules
to tools that measure the impact of such investments
on talent retention, employee well-being, and overall
organizational productivity.


Childcare access affects
workforce performance


Around the world, workers experience persistent
stress and anxiety linked to childcare responsibilities.
In a survey of large employers in the United Kingdom
conducted by the MORI Social Research Institute,
over 40 percent reported that childcare challenges
were affecting their workforce, contributing to fatigue,
irritability, and elevated stress levels among their
employees (IFC 2017). Ample research shows that
the lack of childcare alternatives push women to
accept poorly paid, insecure, and precarious types of
work in the informal sector to increase flexibility; and
that bringing children to work is a practice associated
with significantly lower profits (Devercelli and Beaton-Day 2020; Alfers 2016; Hein and Cassirer 2010;
Diaz and Rodriquez-Chamussy 2013; Woetzel et al.
2015; UN Women 2015; World Bank 2018; Revenga
and Dooley 2020).

A 2018 study conducted in Sri Lanka with 2,000

work and personal responsibilities (IFC 2018). These
global findings resonate strongly with the evidence
gathered in this assessment, where employee
respondents in the participating Latin America

Focus group discussions showed that daily

can undermine productivity. Such disruptions are
compounded by lack of trust in available providers

significant share of surveyed employees struggle
with the impact of childcare responsibilities in their

signaling a generational shift toward shared
caregiving. This shift is especially significant in
a male-dominated sector such as water, where
the visibility of men’s caregiving responsibilities

childcare is a workforce issue, not just a women’s

Current situation: Childcare
arrangements and employee
challenges


For employees in water institutions, family members
serve as the main providers of childcare, followed
by private and public day care centers. Survey
data show that many parents depend on relatives
for childcare. Among employees with children age
6 years and under, 17 percent rely on relatives
at home and 22 percent on relatives outside the
home—together representing 39 percent of parents
(figure 5A). Similarly, among parents with children
with disabilities, 16 percent depend on relatives
at home and 18 percent on relatives outside the
home, totaling 34 percent (figure 5B). In focus group
discussions, both mothers and fathers confirmed this
reliance, often citing economic reasons or concerns
about the quality of day care.



However, this arrangement is not always feasible.
Parents who have moved away from extended family,
or those whose children have disabilities and require
specialized care, often cannot depend on relatives
for support. For parents of children with disabilities,
private childcare facilities are the most common
alternative (figure 5B), though this option brings an
additional financial burden.


Day care centers—whether private or public—are the
second most widely used option, with over a third
of respondents reporting usage (37 percent) across
both types (figure 5A). Despite availability of public
options, many parents choose private providers driven by perceptions of higher quality and challenges
related to accessing public services. However, using
private institutions entails considerable expenses
that place a significant burden on household income,
and for some families may be difficult to maintain
over time. A smaller share of employees with children
age 6 years and under rely on a stay-at-home parent
or on hiring a nanny for childcare.

Barriers to childcare provision
in water institutions





An analysis of corporate policies in the participating
water institutions of this study reveals a notable
gap: many organizations lack a formal framework
addressing childcare. Notably, the challenge of
balancing childcare and work responsibilities
remains largely treated as a private matter. Although
there is no explicit opposition, in many institutions
observed, the absence of a formal system for
employee childcare support is primarily rooted in
an organizational culture that has yet to recognize
caregiving as a legitimate aspect of the workplace
and as a shared institutional responsibility. This
institutional logic reflects a cultural perspective that
views caregiving as a strictly private responsibility—
not as a social coresponsibility. This reality is
evident in interviews with managers, where
most interviewees agree that there is a lack of a
comprehensive policy addressing childcare. Current
measures, such as family allowances, lactation
leave, or flexible schedule options, are governed by
general regulations and fail to align with the specific
needs of staff, who often end up relying on informal
arrangements and individual solutions.

Translating commitment into practice requires
adapting childcare solutions to institutional realities.
Water sector managers are not childcare specialists,
and interventions must be feasible within existing
legal and budgetary frameworks. In most cases,
this means starting with incremental steps—such
as partnering with national childcare providers,
reallocating underused spaces for lactation

before investing in larger initiatives such as onsite childcare centers. Sector institutions can also
explore cofinancing or shared service arrangements
with nearby agencies to maximize resources and
coverage. The key is to align ambition with capacity:
identifying quick wins that demonstrate value
while planning for progressive, scalable investment
over time. Because effective solutions depend on
understanding real needs and constraints, each
institution should first conduct a comprehensive
childcare needs assessment to provide an evidence
base for decisions that address employees’ actual
needs, rather than relying on assumptions.



Expanding access to childcare is necessary but not
sufficient; the quality of childcare is equally critical for
ensuring children’s safety and healthy development.
Employer-supported arrangements should
comply with national standards on accreditation,
safety, and caregiver qualifications, and promote
decent working conditions, adequate ratios, and
trained staff. Because these responsibilities carry
oversight, liability, and regulatory requirements,
many institutions may prefer to begin with lower-risk
options or partnerships with accredited providers.
Institutions can adopt phased approaches—
starting with pilots, improving protocols, and scaling
gradually—to strengthen quality assurance, manage
costs, and build internal capacity over time.


The implementation of each intervention entails
different requirements, not only budgetary, but also
with regard to time frames. For example, establishing
and equipping a lactation room, providing access
to parenting workshops, and offering psychological
support (for both children and parents) could be
achieved faster than the development of a fully
operational on-site childcare facility.

While expanding childcare options is critical, their
success depends on employee uptake. Preferences,
cultural norms, and family structures often lead
parents to favor family-based or informal care due
to trust, cost, or tradition—even when formal options
exist. Low fertility among staff, uneven age groups,
and limited awareness of benefits can also limit
participation, affecting the viability of employersupported centers. Recognizing these behavioral
and cultural dynamics is essential to ensure that
childcare solutions are not only available but also
used and valued by employees.


The inclusion of children with disabilities and
their families should be a cross-cutting priority
across all proposed solutions. While some tailored
supports may be necessary, inclusive approaches
are preferable to separate or parallel services. This
means that initiatives such as summer programs,
childcare subsidies, or parenting workshops should
also be designed to accommodate different support
needs and remain accessible to families raising
children with disabilities.



Selecting the right mix of measures depends on
institutional size, workforce composition, and local
service availability. Smaller or decentralized utilities may prioritize partnerships with community
childcare centers or modest subsidies, while large
agencies with concentrated workforces may find
on-site facilities more cost-effective in the long term.
Integrating these efforts with national childcare
programs or social protection systems can also help
ensure sustainability and avoid duplication.


Lastly, certain interventions may necessitate a geographic lens to ensure equitable implementation.
For example, while institutionalizing flexible work arrangements can benefit the entire workforce regardless of location, initiatives that involve partnerships
with local childcare providers may face coverage
constraints due to the dispersed locations of utility
facilities. These geographic considerations may be
especially relevant to explore further when designing
inclusive, scalable strategies.

_Needs assessment._


Begin by carrying out a comprehensive childcare
needs assessment to ensure that childcare solutions
effectively address employees’ real needs and circumstances rather than relying on assumptions. This
process should examine staff preferences, existing
organizational support measures, and applicable
national legal and regulatory frameworks. Findings
from the assessment should inform the prioritization of actions and guide the selection of feasible,
employer-supported interventions. Institutions are
encouraged to leverage the methodology used in
this study as a reference framework for data collection, enabling direct input from employees and
ensuring evidence-based decision-making aligned
with actual circumstances.


Recently, the Dominican Republic’s water utility—
La Vega Water and Sewerage Corporation (Corporación del Acueducto y Alcantarillado de La Vega,
CORAAVEGA)—carried out an internal assessment
to map the childcare needs of its workforce, focusing
on employees with children age 6 years and under
or with disabilities. The study found that 49 employees—10 percent of the workforce—were affected,
with 65 children in need of care, including eight
infants and one child with a disability. A mapping

_Institutional policy framework._


Design a clear institutional policy for work–family balance. Develop a framework document that defines
principles, objectives, and concrete measures aimed
at aligning employees’ work responsibilities with
childcare duties, and where all childcare-related protocols are formalized. Involving employee groups—
such as staff associations or parent committees—in
drafting the framework can help ensure that the measures reflect real needs and build buy-in across the
workforce. Strong institutional frameworks not only
provide clarity for both employers and employees but
also establish accountability for sustained implementation, whether through existing workplace policies or
standard union contracts.


In Argentina, the water utility—Argentine Water
and Sanitation (Agua y Saneamientos Argentinos
S.A., AySA)—developed internal protocols to align
childcare policies. The company expanded parental
leave to support workers’ caregiving responsibilities—
extending paternity and nongestational leave and
adoption leave, and allowing flexible or reduced
hours after birth or adoption. These measures
promote shared responsibility in childcare, ensure
greater equity between men and women, and embed
a gender perspective into the organization’s labor
policies (AySA 2023).


Similarly, the experience of El Salvador illustrates how
national policy can complement workplace efforts.
As of July 2025, the Ley Crecer Juntos (“Growing
Together Law”) facilitated the establishment of 34
early childhood centers (Centros de Atención a la
Primera Infancia, CAPIs) to provide comprehensive
childcare services across three of the four main
regions in the country. At the time of writing this
report, this government-led initiative had only been
in place for a couple of months, thus its impacts are
not yet fully known. Still, early evidence suggests it
may support women’s productive participation in
the economy (Box 1). This example highlights how
institutional commitments—whether developed at
the organizational or national level—can help ensure
that childcare solutions are both demand driven and
sustainable, with benefits extending well beyond
individual workplaces.

Study findings


Using a mixed methods approach, this childcare
needs assessment conducted across seven water
institutions in three Latin America and the Caribbean
countries reveals a significant and underaddressed
gap in institutional support for working parents in the
sector. Childcare is often viewed only as a family or
social policy matter, but it also directly impacts workforce participation (particularly female), operational
performance, and sectorwide talent sustainability.
This study supports what research has long suggested: reliable, affordable, and quality childcare is
fundamental to employee productivity, retention, and
career advancement. While employers in the studied
water institutions recognize the benefits of corporate
childcare investment, interviews reveal that they are
not fully aware of the extent of returns and numerous
ways in which employer-supported childcare would
increase the utility’s productivity and operational
efficiency—or of the costs of inaction.


**Effect on workforce performance.**
In this study, around 60 percent of working parents
surveyed reported that childcare responsibilities
negatively affected their work performance, with
women facing disproportionate burdens and being
more likely to quit their jobs.


**Impacts on both mothers and fathers.**
Childcare burdens affected both mothers and
fathers at similar levels, signaling a generational
shift toward more involvement of fathers in child
raising. Fathers in the study also reported high
levels of stress and anxiety from balancing rigid
work schedules with caregiving responsibilities.
This translates into higher productivity costs for
water institutions, including absenteeism, high
turnover rates, and lost institutional knowledge—
hindering the sector’s ability to recruit, retain, and
grow a diverse, high-performing workforce.



**Reliance on informal arrangements.**
Many parents in the sector reported resorting to
informal and often unreliable arrangements due
to cost, affordability, and quality concerns. Nearly
40 percent relied on relatives for care, a solution
shaped more by necessity than choice—and often
unavailable for employees who lived apart from
extended family or for those that cared for children
with disabilities. While private day care centers were
the second most common alternative, they incurred
drawbacks such as high cost, uneven quality, and
logistical demands in coordinating drop-off and
pick-up times with work schedules.


**Greater impacts for some employees.**
Single parents and those caring for children with
disabilities faced compounded challenges, with
limited access to tailored support. About one in
five parents of children with disabilities reported
considering unpaid leave, and 20 percent of parents
who are single, divorced, separated or widowed said
they had forgone advancement opportunities.


**Lack of institutional childcare modalities.**
Many water institutions lacked a formal institutional
framework for childcare support. Interviews with
managers and human resources representatives
revealed an absence of proper guidelines
and policies to support childcare solutions for
employees, reflecting an organizational culture
that has yet to recognize caregiving as a legitimate
aspect of the workplace and a social responsibility.
That lack of recognition diminished the incentive to
implement organizational interventions.


42


**Desire for actionable solutions.**
Preferred interventions highlighted by employees included on site childcare centers, flexible schedules,
financial subsidies, and after-hours and back-up
childcare services. Critically, the vast majority of employees with young children in the study worked in
headquarters—making centralized childcare options
both feasible and impactful. Moreover, 58 percent
of surveyed employees worked in professional and
administrative roles, many of whom could benefit
from flexible work models.


**Current good practices.**
The report highlights the tangible strides made in the
region, including select water institutions that were
part of this study, as regional good practices. Notable
examples include Mexico’s CONAGUA childcare
center in one facility location as well as their “Fridays
for Little Ones” program for headquarters employees;
in the Dominican Republic, CORAAVEGA’s mapping
exercise of employee homes and proximity to local
day care centers and facilities; and the lessons
learned from the law “Crecer Juntos” in El Salvador,
establishing concrete actionable childcare regulation
involving employers.


Key takeaway messages


The following key takeaway messages outline the
main challenges identified in the participating water
institutions and the opportunities to address them.


**1.** _Childcare is a workforce performance issue,_
_not just a social one._


A significant share of surveyed parents reported
work disruptions from childcare responsibilities,
leading to more frequent absenteeism, reduced
hours, and day-to-day productivity losses. These
pressures also translated into missed promotions,
stalled career growth, and persistent stress—factors
that contribute to lower female representation and
diversity in managerial roles, and ultimately weaken
institutional performance.



Conclusion


**2.** _Childcare is a challenge across the workforce,_
_not just a women’s issue._


The widening challenge of childcare demands
inclusive solutions. Fathers in the study also
reported high levels of stress and anxiety from
balancing rigid work schedules with caregiving
responsibilities. The generational and norm shift
toward shared family roles means that younger
male employees increasingly seek workplaces that
support caregiving, presenting water institutions
with an opportunity to modernize talent strategies.
Recognizing this reality is essential for retaining staff
and building resilient teams.


**3.** _Career costs fall disproportionately on women,_
_fueling attrition risks._


A higher share of women than men reported considering quitting due to childcare demands, potentially affecting retention. Focus group discussions
confirmed that women shoulder heavier caregiving responsibilities, which restricts their ability to
pursue promotion and training and forces career
trade-offs in an already male-dominated sector.
The loss of talent weakens institutional capacity
and slows progress toward gender-diverse, resilient
water institutions.


**4.** _Most water institutions lack childcare policies_
_and underinvest in solutions._


This represents a critical missed opportunity. With
caregiving treated as a private matter, employees
face income loss, job insecurity, and limited support. Employers, in turn, forfeit gains in retention,
equity, and productivity by settling for minimal measures instead of real childcare investment. Failing to
act also threatens service continuity, as staff shortages and turnover reduce water institutions’ ability
to deliver reliable water services.

Conclusions



**5.** _Without adequate support, employees_
_rely on informal childcare._


Informal support may be unsustainable and often
unavailable to employees in vulnerable situations,
such as those with young children below age
6 years, those with children with disabilities,
or those that are single, divorced, separated or
widowed. Arrangements that depend on relatives
can easily collapse, leaving employees scrambling
to balance work and family. While widespread,
these informal arrangements may be unstable,
adding pressure and undermining reliable work
attendance and ultimately disrupting the water
institutions’ operations.


**6.** _On-site childcare and flexible work_
_arrangements are valued solutions._


With 76 percent of parents working in central offices, centralized childcare facilities can reach a large
share of the workforce while addressing priorities
of proximity and trust. At the same time, flexible
options, such as telework and staggered hours,
were consistently ranked among the most valued,
offering institutions a low-cost, high-impact solution
to reduce turnover and stabilize staffing.


**7.** _Childcare support enhances workforce_
_diversity and resilience._


With only 24 percent female representation in the
water sector in Latin America and the Caribbean, childcare support can help recruit, retain, and
advance women, strengthening both institutional
performance and equity. It also signals a modern,
responsive employer, making the sector more
attractive to skilled candidates. For policy makers
and water institutions, this directly contributes to
long-term institutional resilience and reliable service provision.



**8.** _Understanding employees’ childcare needs_
_is the first step toward institutionalizing_
_meaningful change._


Systematic data collection and regular monitoring
allow employers to design solutions that are
evidence-based, well-received, and sustainable.
Expanding the evidence base is essential for the
water sector to move beyond ad hoc approaches
and deliver measurable results for both employees
and institutions. Regular monitoring also ensures
accountability for staff retention and service
continuity, outcomes that matter most to water
institutions and regulators.


Next steps and future actions


Building on the evidence collected and study findings, this report outlines 11 practical and scalable
childcare solutions for water sector employers. With
a wide range of actionable measures—from on-site
childcare and provider partnerships to lactation
rooms and summer camps—employers can tailor
interventions to their budgets and needs. Moving
forward, water institutions should consider treating
childcare support not as a peripheral benefit but as
an operational priority. The relevance extends beyond
water institutions to other sectors across the region—
including science, technology, engineering, and mathematics and infrastructure—where similar caregiving
pressures weigh on productivity, equity, and growth.


While this study provides a first evidence base on
childcare needs and workplace impacts in the water
sector in the Latin America and the Caribbean region,
further research is needed to deepen the analysis.
Future work could include quantifying the productivity and financial costs associated with absenteeism,
turnover, and stress, and comparing them with the
potential returns on childcare investments. Linking
sectoral findings with national data sets on labor and
care (such as household or employment surveys)
would also help benchmark results and inform scaling strategies. Finally, piloting and evaluating flexible
work or childcare partnership models within utilities
or other water institutions could generate practical
evidence to guide implementation. Together, these
next steps would strengthen the economic and policy
case for institutional investment in childcare.

#### _Appendix B_

Profile of participating institutions



The study covered seven publicly owned water
institutions across the Dominican Republic, Peru,
and Mexico. Together, they represent a diverse
mix of organizational models, from large national
authorities to mid-sized and small decentralized
utilities operating at the provincial or municipal
level. The information summarized here is drawn
from human resources surveys administered to
each participating institution. Some data sets date
back as far as 2022 and may therefore not fully
reflect current figures. However, given the stability of
employment patterns and institutional structures in
the water sector, we expect these figures to remain
broadly representative of existing conditions.


Across the seven institutions, workforce size
ranges from fewer than 200 to more than
10,000 employees. Women represent between
approximately 17 and 39 percent of staff, with
lower representation in technical and managerial
roles. Formal childcare support remains limited:
only one institution offers on-site childcare, none
provides childcare subsidies, and only a few
maintain lactation rooms. Parental leave follows
national legislation in all cases, and flexible work
policies vary widely in scope and consistency.



**Dominican Republic**


_Regulatory context_
According to Women, Business and the Law 2024,14

the Dominican Republic regulates maternity leave
(14 weeks) and offers two days of paternity leave,
with protections against dismissal during pregnancy
and after return to work. Childcare centers must be
licensed and meet standards established by the
national early childhood authority—the National
Institute for Comprehensive Early Childhood Care
(Instituto Nacional de Atención Integral a la Primera Infancia, INAIPI)—including staff qualifications,
safety norms, and quality assurance procedures.
There is no employer mandate to provide childcare,
but national law allows employers to offer childcare
services voluntarily.

**Peru**


_Regulatory context_
Peruvian labor law guarantees 14 weeks of maternity
leave and 10 days of paternity leave, along with
protections against dismissal during pregnancy
and breastfeeding. Workplaces with more than
20 women of reproductive age must establish a
lactation room (lactario institucional) that meets
Ministry of Health standards. Early childhood care
providers are licensed and supervised by the
Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Women and
Vulnerable Populations, which set quality, staffing,
and safety requirements. Peru does not mandate
employer-provided childcare, although employers
may voluntarily offer support or partner with
accredited centers.


_EPS San Martín (Tarapoto)_


EPS San Martín is a regional public utility based in
the city of Tarapoto. It employs approximately 180
people, with women representing around 17 percent
of the workforce. The utility is led by a female general manager. Lactation rooms are available at both
headquarters and technical sites. Maternity leave
and paternity leave are provided in line with national
legislation. Paid family leave is not formally reported,
and flexible work options are not offered. There is no
on-site childcare facility and no financial assistance
for external childcare services.


_EPS Aguas de Lima Norte (Lima North)_


EPS Aguas de Lima Norte serves the northern
provinces of the Lima region. It employs approximately 200 people, around one quarter of whom are
women. The utility offers maternity leave and paternity leave, as well as paid medical or family leave
consistent with national law. It maintains an antiharassment policy and provides induction training on
workplace conduct. Flexible work arrangements are
formally offered and include a wide range of options,
such as part-time work, remote work, gradual return
programs, flexible schedules, and compressed work
weeks. However, the utility does not have on-site
childcare facilities, financial assistance for external
care, or lactation rooms.

**Mexico**


_Regulatory context_
Mexico’s Federal Labor Law provides 12 weeks of
maternity leave and five to seven days of paid paternity leave, along with antidiscrimination protections
for pregnant and breastfeeding workers. Employers
with 20 or more women must provide an appropriate space for breastfeeding or expressing milk.
Childcare centers are regulated and supervised by
the Mexican Social Security Institute (IMSS) and Ministry of Education, which enforce licensing, structural
quality standards, safety protocols, and caregiver
qualifications. While Mexico does not require employers to operate childcare centers, employers may
participate in IMSS childcare schemes or voluntarily
provide on-site or partnered childcare services.


_CONAGUA_


The National Water Commission (Comisión Nacional del Agua, CONAGUA) is Mexico’s federal water
authority, operating under the Ministry of Environment and Natural Resources. With headquarters in
Mexico City and regional offices across all states,
it is the largest institution in the sample, employing
10,000 people. Women make up 39 percent of the
workforce. CONAGUA provides maternity leave and
paternity leave, as well as family leave as regulated
under federal law. The institution has an established
Gender Policy, Gender Strategy, and Action Plan,
and maintains a lactation room at its headquarters.
Flexible work arrangements are available to certain
employees and include remote work, staggered
schedules, and special schedules for circumstances
such as illness, study, breastfeeding, nursery use,
or having children age 0–12 years. At headquarters,
the institution offers several child-related programs,
including a holiday program, after-school and homework support for school-aged children called “Kids
on the Attack” (Chilpayates al ataque), and a special
program on school-closure Fridays called “Fridays
for Little Ones” (Viernes de peques). Additionally,
one of CONAGUA’s regional basin organizations
(OCAVM) operates an on-site childcare center
(CENDI) that provides comprehensive early childhood services for eligible employees.
