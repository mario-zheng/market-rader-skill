## **About this Document**

This report was prepared to support the **Building Skills for Human Capital Development Project in South**
**Sudan** . It provides a landscape analysis of digital agriculture in the country, examining the institutional,
technical, and workforce conditions needed to enable effective digital transformation in the agricultural
sector, with a specific focus on skills for digital agriculture. The findings from this desk review informed
the design of a **two-year Diploma Curriculum in Digital Agriculture**, which will be implemented under the
Project.

The report reviews current digital agriculture and skill-building initiatives, extension service systems,
digital infrastructure trends, and emerging innovation ecosystems. Due to the limited availability of
recent, nationally representative data specific to South Sudan, the analysis draws on a combination of
South Sudan–focused program documentation and broader regional and international evidence.

The report identifies key gaps in coordination, human capacity, connectivity, and financing, and proposes
phased, context-appropriate recommendations. It also presents a structured implementation roadmap
and a competency-based curriculum framework designed to strengthen foundational and intermediate
digital agriculture skills while introducing advanced topics for future readiness for South Sudan Youth.

## **Executive Summary**

This desk review provides an overview of the current state of agriculture in South Sudan and assesses
opportunities to integrate digital technologies and digital skills within the sector. Agriculture remains the
backbone of South Sudan’s economy and a critical livelihood source for most of the population. However,
the sector continues to face persistent constraints, including low productivity, inadequate infrastructure,
limited market access, fragmented extension and advisory services, and climate vulnerability as well as
the ongoing effects of conflict and political instability.

Despite South Sudan’s high agricultural potential, including extensive arable land and diverse agroecological systems, only a small portion of cultivated land is currently utilized. This limits production and
undermines food security. Key staple crops include sorghum, maize, millet, and cassava, alongside oilseed
crops such as sesame and groundnuts, which contribute significantly to local diets. Yet agricultural
performance remains highly sensitive to climate variability and operational disruptions, particularly due
to inconsistent rainfall patterns, weak rural connectivity, and limited availability of modern inputs and
services.

Data-driven digital agriculture and innovations offer a practical pathway to address several of these
constraints. In contexts where extension coverage is limited and markets are fragmented, digital tools can
improve access to timely agronomic advice, enhance transparency in input and seed systems, facilitate
farmer registration and targeting, and strengthen early warning systems for climate, pests, and diseases.
Technologies such as mobile based advisory services, digital payment services, geographic information
systems, remote sensing, digital supply chain and traceability, weather advisory and basic farm data tools
can support more efficient decision making and strengthen farmer resilience.

South Sudan has already begun taking steps toward digital transformation in agriculture. A digital
agriculture platform launched by the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security (MAFS), in collaboration
with the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), includes e-extension services, weather advisories, and
seed management functions aimed at improving agricultural efficiency. In parallel, donor-supported
programs, including the International Trade Centre’s Jobs Creation and Trade Development project, are
helping to strengthen training and extension support for farmers and agribusinesses.

However, the effectiveness and sustainability of digital agriculture initiatives will depend on whether
digital solutions are sequenced realistically and supported by the required enabling conditions. Current
implementation bottlenecks include low rural electrification, limited digital infrastructure, constrained
internet access, institutional coordination challenges, and a shortage of personnel equipped to deliver
digital extension and training services. These constraints indicate the need for a phased approach that
prioritizes practical and affordable technologies that can be deployed at scale under current conditions,
while progressively building capacity for more advanced applications over time.

To strengthen South Sudan`s national human capital skills and capacity building, this report proposes the
development of a two-year diploma curriculum in digital agriculture designed to build a workforce
equipped with the practical skills needed to operate and sustain emerging digital agriculture systems. The
curriculum focuses on foundational and intermediate competencies aligned with South Sudan’s current
realities, including digital literacy for agricultural applications, mobile-based data collection, geographic
information systems, climate and weather information tools, basic data analysis, and digital
communication for extension and advisory services. Over time, graduates of this two-year diploma course
are expected to support government programs, extension and advisory systems, development partner

Overall, digital agriculture in South Sudan should be understood not only as a technology modernization

## **Introduction – Setting the stage**

Agriculture remains central to South Sudan’s economy, livelihoods, and national resilience. An estimated
**70–80 percent of the population depends on agriculture and related rural livelihoods** [1], including
farming, fishing, and livestock production, while **approximately 59.25 percent** [2] **of total employment** is
concentrated in the agricultural sector. Together, these figures underscore the sector’s critical role as a
foundation of household food security and income generation. In a context marked by fragility and
recurring shocks, strengthening the agricultural sector is therefore not only an economic priority, but also
a stabilizing force that supports poverty reduction, social cohesion, and long-term recovery.

Despite South Sudan’s significant agricultural potential, sector performance remains constrained by a set
of persistent systemic challenges. Agricultural production is largely dominated by smallholder, subsistence
farming systems, with limited use of modern inputs, mechanization, and irrigation. **Less than** **5 percent of**
**available agricultural land is currently cultivated,** [3] which substantially restricts domestic production and
limits opportunities for commercialization. In 2023, cereals and oilseed crops continued to dominate
production, yet the country still faced a **cereal deficit estimated at 388,250 tons for the January–**
**December 2024 marketing year** [4], reflecting the gap between domestic production and national
consumption needs. At the same time, cereal yields in South Sudan remain among the lowest in East
Africa, averaging close to **1 ton per hectare** **[5]**, significantly below regional leaders such as Ethiopia and
Uganda. These low yields are linked to limited access to improved inputs, weak extension coverage,
climate variability, and constrained market integration. Together, these factors underscore the urgent
need for approaches that can improve farmer access to information, advisory services, and productivityenhancing practices at scale.

A key structural bottleneck is the weakness and fragmentation of extension and advisory services.
Extension provision involves the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security (MAFS) alongside multiple nonstate actors, creating fragmented delivery, uneven coverage, and limited continuity in farmer support. In
practice, this means farmers often lack timely and reliable access to guidance on improved agronomic
practices, pest and disease management, post-harvest handling, and market decision-making. These
constraints are compounded by limited infrastructure and high transaction costs in rural areas, which
reduce value chain integration and limit access to aggregation, storage, processing, and markets.

Climate vulnerability further amplifies these challenges. South Sudan’s agriculture is highly dependent on
rainfall patterns, with a long rainy season that varies across regions and increases exposure to climaterelated shocks including erratic rainfall, flooding, and dry spells. [6] These conditions affect production
stability, disrupt planting and harvesting cycles, and intensify risks related to pests and crop diseases.
Climate-related risks also increase the urgency of strengthening early warning systems and preparedness,
particularly for rural communities with limited capacity for coping. [7]

## **Section I. The Case for Digital Transformation**

### 1.1. Why Digital Transformation Matters for South Sudan’s Agriculture

Digital transformation in agriculture refers to the strategic use of digital technologies and data-driven and
data-enabled services to improve agricultural decision-making, agriculture extension and advisory service
delivery, productivity, and market performance across the value chain. Digital agriculture includes tools
such as mobile advisory platforms, farmer e-registration databases, weather and pest alerts, digital input
and voucher systems, remote sensing, and Geographic Information System (GIS) -supported planning. In
this report, “digital agriculture” is used in line with Food and Agriculture Organization’s (FAO) framing of
digital agriculture as the use of digital technologies to support farm decision-making and improve delivery
of services and inputs. [9]

South Sudan’s agricultural modernization challenge is not only a production issue, but also a service
delivery and coordination challenge. Farmers often operate with limited access to timely information on
agronomic practices, pest and disease risks, weather variability, input availability, and market prices. This
creates persistent **information asymmetry** [10], where farmers have less and later information than traders,
intermediaries, and better-connected actors. Such information gaps raise transaction costs, weaken
bargaining power, and reduce incentives for commercial investment in production and post-harvest
handling. [11] Digital tools are uniquely positioned to reduce these asymmetries by enabling targeted and
timely information delivery and by supporting transparency and traceability within input and output
markets.

A major barrier to agricultural productivity in South Sudan is the limited reach and fragmentation of
extension and advisory services. The extension system includes MAFS and several non-state actors, with
fragmented delivery models that result in uneven coverage and inconsistent support for farmers. An FAO
study [12] in 2020 revealed that despite availability of technical staff, public provision of agricultural
extension services is hardly functional and faces several challenges including lack of motivation and
commitment of staff, absence of agricultural extension kits and limited transport facilities. In this context,
**digitally enabled advisory services** can complement physical extension systems by improving outreach,
consistency, and speed of service delivery. Digital extension approaches enable two-way communication
between farmers and advisory experts, support real-time dissemination of locally relevant guidance, and
build datasets on farmer needs and constraints to improve program design.

Beyond advisory services, digital transformation can address **agricultural market inefficiency and weak**
**value-chain integration**, particularly in settings where infrastructure, aggregation, and market
information systems are limited. Digital platforms can improve coordination across actors by enabling
digital farmer registration, improving input targeting, linking farmers to market, reducing leakages in
public seeds and fertilizer subsidies and crop insurance and other financial support programs, and
supporting timely price and logistics information. These tools are especially relevant where rural
communities face high transaction costs and limited access to reliable market information and financial
services. The value of such tools is strongest when they focus on scalable, mobile-enabled services that
match local connectivity realities.

### 1.2. Enabling Conditions and a Realistic Pathway to Scale


The case for digital transformation must be grounded in South Sudan’s implementation realities,
particularly limited connectivity, infrastructure constraints, and uneven digital inclusion. The report notes
that internet penetration remains low and that a significant share of the population remains offline. These
conditions support a phased transformation approach in which South Sudan prioritizes digital tools that
can deliver near-term value under current constraints while progressively building digital infrastructure,
institutional capacity, and digital skills over time. Digital transformation in South Sudan’s agriculture
sector must be designed around current implementation constraints, particularly limited connectivity,
infrastructure gaps, and uneven digital inclusion. With a large share of the population still offline, digital
agriculture cannot rely on high-bandwidth or device-intensive solutions in the near term. Instead,
progress will depend on a phased approach that prioritizes practical, low-bandwidth tools capable of
delivering immediate service-delivery gains, while gradually strengthening digital infrastructure,
institutional capacity, and workforce skills.

International and regional experience shows that digital agriculture is most effective when embedded
within enabling data and digital ecosystems that combine connectivity, reliable data, governance, and
user-centered design. Digital tools in agriculture have been associated with benefits such as **improved**
**crop yields, reduced waste, greater price transparency, lower costs, and enhanced resilience** when
integrated with extension services and market systems. [13] At the same time, many digital agriculture
initiatives fail to scale when **institutional coordination is weak**, adoption support is under-resourced, or
services are not tailored to users’ literacy and trust contexts. For South Sudan, this underscores the
importance of anchoring digital agriculture within practical delivery structures — especially extension
services and training institutions — that can support sustained adoption and long-term scale. This
transformation requires not only technology investments, but also a workforce equipped with the digital
competencies needed to deliver and sustain these services.

of roles and skills that will be required to support and scale these systems.

### 2.1. Institutional and Program Landscape


South Sudan’s digital agriculture landscape is emerging and is currently driven by a mix of public sector
leadership, donor-supported programs, and early-stage private innovation. MAFS [14] is positioned as a
central actor in agricultural service delivery and policy, while the Ministry of Information, Communication

**e-government** and **national digital transformation initiatives** . The **National e-Government Framework** [16]
oversees the broader government ICT ecosystem, including digital infrastructure, services, and personnel
and is intended to integrate government systems and support the country’s transition toward a digitally
enabled public administration and economy. This evolving governance context is complemented by donor

Within MAFS, the **Directorate of Agricultural Production and Extension Services** **[17]** plays a central role in
shaping and executing policies and strategies to improve agricultural production and productivity. This
directorate is responsible for planning and implementing extension programs, deploying qualified
extension agents across administrative levels (from counties to payams and bomas), facilitating access to
agricultural technologies and best cropping systems, coordinating state ministries, and linking farmers to
credit, market information, and inputs. It also leads training and human resource development efforts,
supports irrigation and cash crop expansion, and acts as a key coordinator with donors, public institutions,
and international partners to align initiatives with government policy. The directorate’s functions are
integral to translating national agriculture policies into operational programs and fostering effective
delivery of services that underpin digital and traditional extension systems alike.

**Digital Agriculture Extension Advisory Services (DAES)** are increasingly positioned as a practical delivery
channel for improving extension reach in South Sudan. DAES use digital platforms, mobile applications,
and online resources to provide farmers with timely information on agronomic practices, crop
management, market trends, and access to financial services. They also support two-way communication
between farmers and extension experts and enable real-time problem-solving and data collection that
can inform policy and program design. In a context where physical extension services face logistical and
staffing constraints, DAES offer a complementary mechanism to improve coverage and continuity.

A key public-sector milestone is the **digital agriculture solutions platform** [18] launched on 22 November
2023 by MAFS in collaboration with FAO. The package includes an **e-registry and voucher management**
**system**, an **e-extension and weather advisory mobile platform**, and a **seed management system**,
designed to create a harmonized farmer database and improve the targeting and delivery of agricultural
interventions and inputs. The platform was developed with support from the Government of South Sudan
and the Africa Development Bank, and it is presented as a national digital ecosystem that can register and
manage beneficiaries, including farmers, livestock keepers, seed producers, and other value-chain
stakeholders.

the foundations for digital agriculture. The **PlantwisePlus** [19] programme, implemented by Centre for
Agriculture and Bioscience International (CABI) and partners, supports the strengthening of plant health
systems through training plant doctors, developing pest management decision guides, and promoting
standardized advisory practices. The initiative also introduced digital plant health tools and surveillance
approaches that can be integrated into broader digital extension and early warning systems.
Complementary efforts have included training-of-trainers workshops for plant doctors in Juba and

### 2.2. Private Sector and Innovation Ecosystem


Private sector innovation in South Sudan remains nascent but is increasingly visible through agritech
initiatives, entrepreneurship programs, and enabling digital service providers. The report highlights an
agritech initiative led by a United Nations Institute for Training and Research (UNITAR) alum [24], with a team
of field agents and service providers supporting farmers in using an application and engaging MAFS and
NGOs for scale. In parallel, innovation and entrepreneurship infrastructure is being strengthened through
hubs such as **Juba iHub** [25], launched as a one-stop shop to support young entrepreneurs with skills,
employment readiness, and innovation support. Development partners have complemented these efforts
through challenge-based innovation programs, including the **WFP–UNDP IGNITE Food Systems**
**Challenge**, which provides funding and incubation to food systems ventures. [26]

Private sector growth in agriculture is also being supported through initiatives that link workforce
development with commercial value chains. The **Accelerating Agriculture and Agribusiness in South**
**Sudan for Enhanced Economic Development (A3-SEED) Project**, [27] funded by the Embassy of the Kingdom
of the Netherlands, has worked with seed companies, agro-dealers, and marketing officers to strengthen
production, processing, and distribution systems. Through co-investment grants, training of agro-dealers
and inspectors, and support for seed marketing networks, the project illustrates how sector-focused skills
development can directly support private agribusiness performance.

### 2.3. Skills and Training Ecosystem


The effectiveness of emerging digital agriculture systems in South Sudan depends heavily on the

## **Section III. Gaps, Bottlenecks, and Capacity**


While digital agriculture presents clear opportunities, the feasibility of implementation in South Sudan
depends on a realistic assessment of binding constraints across infrastructure, institutions, human capital,
and financing. These constraints are not generic. They are shaped by South Sudan’s fragile operating
environment, uneven territorial connectivity, and fragmented service delivery systems. A South Sudanspecific diagnostic is therefore essential to identify where digital agriculture can deliver immediate value,
where it risks becoming pilot-driven without scale, and what institutional strengthening measures should
be prioritized to sustain impact.

### 3.1 Digital Infrastructure Constraints (Connectivity and Power)


A primary bottleneck for digital agriculture in South Sudan is limited connectivity. As noted above, in early
2023 South Sudan had 3.58 million active cellular mobile connections (32.5 percent of population) but
only 773.4 thousand internet users (7.0 percent penetration), with 93 percent of the population remaining
offline. More recent estimates indicate that by early 2024 internet users increased to approximately 1.36
million (about 12 percent penetration), suggesting gradual improvement but continued structural access
gaps. [35] These connectivity constraints imply that digital agriculture interventions must prioritize lowbandwidth solutions, offline functionality, and delivery channels that work on basic feature phones,
including SMS and USSD-based services, rather than relying primarily on smartphone applications. This


35 Kemp, S., 2024

design principle is consistent with global evidence on digital agriculture adoption in low-connectivity
environments. [36,37]

Limited access to electricity significantly constrains digital agriculture delivery and adoption, especially in
rural areas where agricultural production is concentrated. Electricity access affects farmer phone-charging
capacity, device use, connectivity reliability, and the feasibility of running digital service points such as
farmer service centers, digital training hubs, or ICT-enabled extension facilities. World Bank development
indicators estimate that access to electricity in South Sudan remains extremely low, at approximately **5–**
**6 percent** [38] of the population in recent years, underscoring the importance of integrating energy solutions
such as solar charging stations, mini-grids, and community-powered service hubs within digital agriculture
program design. Off-grid solar and community energy models are increasingly used to enable rural digital
service delivery in fragile contexts. [39]

### 3.2 Institutional Capacity and Coordination Bottlenecks


Institutional coordination remains a central bottleneck. Digital agriculture implementation requires
effective collaboration across MAFS, MICT&PS, universities and training institutes, and the private sector.
The report highlights the national digital transformation agenda, including proposed e-government
services and digital economy interventions led by MICT&PS, alongside agriculture-specific platforms and
programs in the MAFS ecosystem and analyzing private digital AgTech ecosystem. However, coordination
between these actors is not institutionalized through a formal governance mechanism, creating risks of
overlapping initiatives, fragmented standards, weak interoperability of data systems, and short-lived
donor-driven pilots. Experiences from other African countries show that the absence of clear digital
agriculture governance structures often leads to duplication of registries, incompatible data standards,
and unsustained platforms. [40,41] This coordination gap also affects curriculum implementation and the
ability to institutionalize digital agriculture skills development within national training systems.

Strengthening collaboration between training institutions and market actors will be essential for aligning
skills development with real service delivery needs. Potential private sector partners that could support
practical training and workforce pathways are outlined in Annex B.

### 3.3 Financing, Sustainability, and Delivery Fragmentation


Financing represents a major bottleneck for sustainability. Many digital agriculture initiatives in South
Sudan are implemented through donor, humanitarian, or development partner programs, including
livelihood recovery and agricultural resilience interventions. While these investments are essential, heavy
reliance on external financing without national ownership introduces risks of discontinuity, platform
abandonment, and fragmented scaling once project cycles end. Sustainability requires that digital
agriculture tools are integrated into national institutional mandates and budgets, supported through
blended financing models that can include donor support, government allocations, and private sector
service provision where viable. Global experience shows that digital agriculture systems embedded in core
public service delivery functions are more likely to be sustained than stand-alone donor platforms. [42,43]

### 3.4. Human Capital and Skills Readiness


The shortage of digitally trained agricultural extension officers and digital trainers limits the ability to
deliver digital agriculture services at scale. The report describes South Sudan’s extension system as
pluralistic and fragmented, with multiple state and non-state actors, and highlights the need for welltrained extension agents and strengthened extension training facilities. In practice, this translates into
capacity gaps in four areas:


i. extension personnel who can integrate data-driven, digital tools into agricultural advisory work,
ii. trainers who can deliver basic ICT and digital extension competencies,
iii. technical staff who can maintain platforms, manage data, and support user adoption, and
iv. private sector entrepreneurs who can scale up technologies and provide digital agriculture
solutions and services to smallholder farmers.

Without these human capabilities, even well-designed platforms risk low uptake and limited continuity
once external project support ends.

Skills readiness for digital agriculture remains uneven. While the report proposes a two-year diploma
program in digital agriculture, including advanced modules such as artificial intelligence, blockchain, and
robotics, these competencies may not match near-term infrastructure realities and implementation
constraints. Under current conditions, the binding constraint is not advanced technology awareness but
the availability of foundational and intermediate skills, including digital literacy, mobile-based data
collection, basic farm registry management, digital advisory service delivery, and practical analytics for
extension and market systems. This skills mismatch risk reinforces the need for a phased skills pathway,
where foundational competencies are scaled first and advanced topics are introduced conceptually for
future readiness rather than immediate deployment.

The analysis above highlights that South Sudan’s digital agriculture ambitions are constrained not only by
infrastructure and institutional coordination gaps, but also by a critical shortage of appropriately trained
human resources. While digital platforms for farmer registration, advisory services, and climate
information are beginning to take shape, the systems required to operate, sustain, and scale these tools
remain underdeveloped. Existing training pathways for extension officers and agricultural professionals
are largely oriented toward traditional service delivery models and do not yet provide structured
preparation for digitally enabled roles. Addressing these capacity gaps requires a deliberate and phased
approach to digital agriculture skills development. The following section therefore outlines a national skills
framework and workforce pathways designed to align training with South Sudan’s current digital
agriculture landscape and future development trajectory.

## **Section IV. Digital Agriculture Skills Framework and Workforce** **Pathways**

### 4.1. Why Digital Agriculture Skills Matter in South Sudan

As South Sudan’s agricultural sector begins to incorporate digital tools such as farmer registries, mobile
advisory platforms, weather information services, and digital input distribution systems, the effectiveness
of these investments increasingly depends on the availability of personnel with appropriate digital
competencies. Digital platforms alone cannot improve service delivery without individuals who are able
to operate, maintain, interpret, and communicate the information these systems generate.

The country’s extension and agricultural support systems are undergoing gradual modernization, yet most
current training pathways remain focused on traditional agronomic knowledge and face-to-face advisory
methods. The growing integration of mobile-based communication, digital data collection, and platformbased service delivery therefore creates an urgent need for a new category of professionals who can
bridge agriculture and digital systems. Strengthening digital agriculture skills is not only essential for
improving advisory reach and data-driven decision-making, but also for enhancing market linkages,
climate resilience, and the efficiency of agricultural programs.

The proposed digital agriculture curriculum responds directly to this emerging skills gap. It is designed to
equip graduates with competencies that support current national systems while preparing them for more
advanced technologies, as infrastructure and institutional capacity evolve.

### 4.2. Core Competency Areas for Digital Agriculture


A context-appropriate digital agriculture curriculum in South Sudan should focus on practical
competencies that enable graduates to support agricultural service delivery in low-connectivity, resourceconstrained environments. Rather than emphasizing highly advanced technologies at the outset, the
curriculum should prioritize skills that align with existing platforms and operational realities.

Key competency areas include:

  - **Digital literacy for agricultural applications**, including the use of smartphones, tablets, and basic
software tools such as, digital soil analysis, access to market information, input and subsidy
services.

  - **Mobile-based data collection and reporting**, supporting farmer registries, extension monitoring,
and program tracking

  - **Digital communication for advisory services**, including SMS, messaging platforms, and
multimedia tools

  - **Geospatial and mapping tools**, such as basic GIS applications for agricultural planning and
targeting

  - **Climate and weather information tools**, enabling the interpretation and communication of
advisories

  - **Basic data management and analysis**, including spreadsheet use and simple data visualization

  - **Digital ethics and data responsibility**, including privacy, data protection, and responsible
information use

These competencies form the foundation for supporting digital advisory systems, agricultural databases,
and information services already being introduced in South Sudan. To ensure relevance and sustainability,


20


digital agriculture skills development should follow a phased progression that reflects current capacity
while building toward future readiness.

**Stage 1 – Foundational Skills (Immediate Application)**
Focuses on essential digital and communication skills required to support existing agricultural service
delivery systems. These include basic digital literacy, mobile-based data collection, messaging platforms
for advisory services, and fundamental computer use. Graduates at this level can directly support
extension programs and field-based digital initiatives.

**Stage 2 – Intermediate Skills (Emerging Systems)**
Builds competencies needed to support expanding digital agriculture platforms. This stage includes
geospatial tools, weather and pest advisory systems, digital market information services, and basic data
entry and analysis. Graduates can work with farmer registries, digital advisory services, and program
monitoring systems.

**Stage 3 – Advanced and Future-Facing Skills (Long-Term Readiness)**
Introduces awareness of more advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence, Internet of Things
(IoT) applications, remote sensing, and blockchain for traceability. While immediate demand for these
skills in South Sudan is limited, exposure prepares graduates for future sector development and regional
employment opportunities.

### 4.4. Employer Expectations in the South Sudan Context


Employers operating in South Sudan’s agricultural and development sectors are likely to seek graduates
who can bridge practical agricultural knowledge with applied digital skills. Beyond technical competence,
graduates must be able to work effectively in low-resource settings and communicate clearly with diverse
stakeholders.

Key expectations include:

  - Ability to use digital tools to support extension and advisory services

  - Competence in collecting, managing, and interpreting agricultural data

  - Capacity to explain technical information to farmers and non-technical audiences

  - Adaptability in environments with limited connectivity and infrastructure

  - Collaboration with agricultural officers, NGOs, private sector actors, and community groups

  - Understanding of local agricultural systems, value chains, and seasonal dynamics

Graduates who combine digital competence with contextual agricultural understanding will be well

## **Section V. Recommendations and Conclusion**


This report concludes that advancing digital agriculture in South Sudan is fundamentally a **human capital**
**and institutional development challenge** as much as a technology agenda. While digital platforms offer
opportunities to expand extension reach, improve targeting inputs, strengthen farmer and seed
registration systems, and enhance market transparency, the sustainability and scalability of these systems


22


depend on a workforce capable of operating and maintaining them. Agriculture continues to underpin
livelihoods for the majority of the population and remains central to economic recovery and resilience.
However, impact will depend on addressing binding constraints including weak coordination across
institutions, limited extension and training capacity, low connectivity and power access, and the
sustainability risks of donor-driven pilots.

Given this context, the **Ministry of Higher Education, Science and Technology (MoHEST)** has a pivotal role
in institutionalizing digital agriculture skills development. The proposed roadmap and phased
implementation timeline (Table 1) outline sequencing, institutional responsibilities, and indicative
timelines over a three-year horizon, with MoHEST serving as the lead entity for curriculum
implementation and training system development.

### 5.1. Policy and Coordination


A national **Digital Agriculture Task Force** should be established to coordinate priorities, standards, and
sequencing of digital agriculture investments and workforce development. The Task Force should be
jointly convened by **MAFS, MICT&PS, and MoHEST**, with representation from universities and training
providers, telecom operators, agritech entrepreneurs, development partners, and farmer-based
organizations.

The mandate should include:


  - Agreeing national priorities and sequencing for digital agriculture services and skills development

  - Ensuring interoperability standards for digital registries and platforms

  - Approving national competency standards for digital agriculture education and training

  - Coordinating investments to avoid duplication and ensure scale

Digital agriculture should be embedded within national economic diversification and human capital
strategies. MoHEST should lead the integration of digital agriculture skills into national higher education
and technical training frameworks, ensuring alignment with agricultural modernization goals and
workforce planning. This includes embedding digital competencies into agricultural education standards
and aligning institutional training plans with national extension digitization priorities.

### 5.2. Capacity and Skills Development


With the digital agriculture skills framework established earlier in the report, the priority now is to
institutionalize its delivery within South Sudan’s higher education and training systems. MoHEST should
lead the formal adoption, accreditation, and rollout of the proposed **Two-Year Diploma in Digital**
**Agriculture** in partnership with selected universities and agricultural training institutions. Anchoring the
program within MoHEST’s mandate ensures that digital agriculture skills development becomes part of
the national education system rather than remaining dependent on short-term, project-based training
initiatives.

MoHEST should work closely with the MAFS and MICT&PS to align training delivery with operational needs
in digital agriculture extension services, digital registries, advisory platforms, and agricultural data
ecosystems. This coordination will help ensure that graduates are prepared for real roles supporting
national digital agriculture initiatives and look to enter in the private sector or can start their own
entrepreneurship journey. To operationalize the digital agriculture skills framework and ensure its
sustainable institutionalization, a phased implementation approach is required. The roadmap below

### 5.3. Infrastructure and Access


Scaling digital agriculture requires targeted investments in connectivity and power in key agricultural
corridors and training hubs. Rather than nationwide upgrades, the Government and partners should
prioritize corridor-based infrastructure planning that links:


  - High-potential agricultural production areas

  - Markets and aggregation zones

  - Training institutions and extension service delivery systems

Investments should bundle last-mile connectivity with reliable power solutions for service points such as
solar-powered digital kiosks, charging hubs, and extension ICT units.

MAFS, MICT&PS, and MoHEST should collaborate with telecom operators to develop **education and**

campaigns should be integrated into both extension outreach and formal training programs to ensure

### 5.4. Private Sector and Innovation


Private sector participation should be strengthened through incentives for local agritech solutions that
align with South Sudan’s capacity and service delivery needs. This can include seed funding windows,
procurement opportunities linked to public extension systems, and incubation support through platforms
such as Juba iHub.

MoHEST can play a catalytic role by linking universities and training institutions with innovation
ecosystems, encouraging student-led agritech projects, internships, and entrepreneurship programs. This
strengthens the pipeline from training to employment and innovation.

Digital finance should be promoted as a key enabler for agricultural markets, including digital voucher
schemes, aggregation payments, and reducing cash handling risks. However, mobile money adoption
remains low. Expansion strategies should focus on rural agent networks, farmer cooperative accounts,
and program-linked transactions where feasible.

### 5.5. Monitoring and Evaluation


A monitoring and evaluation framework should track both **system adoption and workforce development**
**outcomes** . MoHEST, in collaboration with MAFS and MICT&PS, should help integrate education and skills
indicators into national monitoring systems.

Priority indicators should include:

  - Number of students enrolled and graduating from accredited digital agriculture programs

  - Number of extension officers and trainers receiving digital upskilling

  - Employment of graduates in digital agriculture roles

  - Number of farmers registered in digital systems

  - Share of farmers receiving digital advisory services

  - Growth of agritech ventures linked to training institutions

These indicators should be reported annually through a digital agriculture implementation dashboard
managed jointly by MAFS, MICT&PS, and MoHEST.

### 5.6. Conclusion


Digital agriculture in South Sudan is ultimately a **workforce and institutional capacity agenda** . Technology
investment alone will not deliver impact without trained professionals who can operate systems, interpret
data, and translate digital tools into improved services for farmers. By placing MoHEST at the center of
digital agriculture skills development and aligning training with national agricultural priorities, South
Sudan can build a sustainable human capital foundation for digital transformation.

A phased, skills-focused roadmap supported by clear governance arrangements and measurable
indicators offers a practical pathway to strengthening extension delivery, improving market linkages, and
enhancing resilience. With coordinated leadership across ministries and strong institutional anchoring in
the higher education system, digital agriculture skills development can become a cornerstone of inclusive
growth and long-term agricultural modernization.

## **Annex A. Digital Agriculture Diploma Curriculum (Two-Year Program)**

**Curriculum Objective**

This two-year diploma curriculum aims to prepare graduates to support the operationalization of digital
agriculture in South Sudan by strengthening foundational digital skills, building practical competencies for
digital extension and agricultural service delivery, and introducing future-facing concepts for longer-term
modernization. The curriculum is sequenced to reflect South Sudan’s current connectivity, energy,
institutional, and human resource constraints, and prioritizes skills that can be implemented under
existing infrastructure conditions.

**Curriculum Structure (Progressive Pathway)**

The program is organized into three stages:

  - **Stage 1 (Foundational, Year 1):** Immediate deployment skills (low-bandwidth, mobile-first tools
for extension and program support)

  - **Stage 2 (Intermediate, Year 2):** Pilot and scale competencies (GIS, market information systems,
advisory analytics, M&E)

  - **Stage 3 (Advanced/Future-facing):** Conceptual awareness modules (AI, IoT, blockchain)
integrated as seminars/demos (not core deployment competencies)

## **Annex B. Private Sector and Potential Industry Partners**

Private sector engagement is essential to the sustainability and practical relevance of digital agriculture
systems in South Sudan. While public institutions play a central role in policy, training, and extension,
many operational functions, including input supply, aggregation, processing, financial services, logistics,
and technology provision, are delivered through private actors. Understanding which companies are
active, what services they currently provide, and where their business models intersect with digital
agriculture priorities is therefore critical. This annex identifies selected private sector actors operating in
South Sudan that present potential opportunities for collaboration in digital agriculture skills and
workforce development. The objective is not to pre-qualify partners, but to inform a flexible engagement
approach that links skills development and digital system deployment with existing market actors.

**Agricultural Production, Inputs, and Value Chain Companies**

a. **[Nile Agro-Tech and Seed Company Limited (NATSCO).](https://nileagrotech.com/)** NATSCO is a South Sudanese agribusiness

company focused on commercialized agricultural production using modern research and
technologies. The company engages in seed production, value addition, market linkages, and the
introduction of improved agricultural practices. It also provides training and consultancy services
to support small- and medium-scale farmers, including women and youth. NATSCO’s experience
in farmer engagement and technology transfer makes it a relevant partner for piloting digital
advisory, farm registry, and input traceability systems.
b. **[South Farmers Company Limited (SFCL).](https://www.facebook.com/p/South-Farmers-Company-Ltd-100063836088012/)** Established in 2010, SFCL operates in cereal production,

seed multiplication, agrochemical supply, and agribusiness consultancy. The company also
supports dairy and poultry production and supplies agricultural tools. SFCL’s vertically integrated
engagement across production, inputs, and advisory services positions it as a potential partner
for digital farm management tools, e-voucher systems, and digital extension services aimed at
improving productivity and market integration.
c. **[Eastern African Seed and Tools Company](https://www.easeed.com/)** . This local enterprise supplies agricultural inputs and

tools and works with farmer groups such as the Morling Cooperative Society. The company also
collaborates with training institutions, including Magwi College for Agri-Business, to support
farmer development. Its plans to expand mechanization services (e.g., oxen-drawn ploughs)
suggest opportunities to integrate digital inventory management, farmer service booking
systems, and digital extension linked to input use.

**Agroforestry and Plantation Enterprises**

d. **[Equatoria Teak Company (ETC)](https://www.equatoriateak.com/)** . Founded in 2006, ETC is a major agro-forestry enterprise

managing large teak plantations in Western Equatoria State. The company also engages in coffee
production and implements community development programs, with a large share of its
workforce recruited locally. ETC’s structured plantation management systems, workforce
training, and sustainability focus create potential entry points for digital land management,
traceability systems, and climate-smart agriculture data tools.

**Agribusiness Service Providers and Capacity Development Firms**

e. **[Premium Agro Consultants Ltd](https://premiumagroconsult.com/)** . Premium Agro Consultants is a South Sudanese agribusiness

consultancy with strong linkages to higher education institutions such as the University of Juba
and Upper Nile University. The firm provides training and technical support to farmers and has
trained dozens of agriculture graduates in recent years. Its role in bridging academic institutions

f. **[SPARK](https://spark.ngo/programme/food-security-through-agribusiness-in-south-sudan/)** . SPARK is an international development organization active in South Sudan through
